🚚CAN Bus

Every node may speak at any moment, and the wire itself decides who wins — bit by bit, without losing a single one. That one idea gives CAN its multi-master freedom, its provable worst-case latency, and its three most expensive traps: the identifier is the priority, load is not latency, and an error frame belongs to everybody.

The Bagging Line That Gave Away 150 Grams a Bag — at 34 % Bus Load

A FLOUR MILL AT JA-ELA — 22 CANOPEN NODES, 500 kbit/s, AND A SLIDE GATE THAT CLOSED WHENEVER IT FELT LIKE IT

A wheat mill at Ja-Ela runs a 25 kg bagging and palletising line: a gross weigher with a coarse-feed auger and a dribble feed, six servo-driven axes for the bag placer and the palletiser, and sixteen further I/O and sensor nodes — 22 nodes on one CAN segment at 500 kbit/s, CANopen throughout. The specification said the coarse feed must stop within 2 ms of the weigh controller announcing target. Fill accuracy drifted, so the mill did the sensible commercial thing and raised the target weight by 150 g to stay legally safe. Nobody costed that decision for eight months.

The integrator did what any competent engineer does with a fieldbus complaint: he measured the bus. Bus load 34 %. No frames lost. Error counters on the master at zero. Every node in Operational. In network terms the segment was healthy — and it genuinely was. Nothing that was wrong with this machine was visible in a bus-load figure, which is the single most repeated mistake in CAN engineering and the reason this module exists.

Fault 1 — the cut-off command had been given a low identifier, and on CAN the identifier is the priority. The weigh controller was node 10, and its “target reached” flag went out on its third transmit PDO. CANopen's default allocation makes that COB-ID 0x380 + 10 = 0x38A. Every drive PDO in the machine has a numerically lower identifier, so every drive PDO beats it to the wire:

bit time at 500 kbit/s = 2.000 µs worst-case standard frame, n data bytes: C = (55 + 10n) × 2 µs 0 B (SYNC) = 55 bits = 110 µs 2 B (cut-off)= 75 bits = 150 µs 8 B (a PDO) = 135 bits = 270 µs everything the SYNC pulse releases ahead of 0x38A, once per 10 ms cycle: SYNC 0x080 110 µs 6 × TPDO1 0x181 … 0x186 6 × 270 = 1,620 µs 6 × RPDO1 0x201 … 0x206 6 × 270 = 1,620 µs ∑ = 3,350 µs (= 33.5 % bus load) blocking — one lower-priority 8-byte SDO already on the wire B = 270 µs worst-case response of the cut-off message: R = B + ∑interference + C = 270 + 3,350 + 150 = 3,770 µs = 3.77 ms against a 2 ms deadline

Read the load figure and the response time together, because they are the whole of page 7. The bus was 66 % idle and a message still arrived 1.9× later than its deadline. That is not a contradiction and it is not a defect: it is what a priority bus does. Idle time that occurs after your message has already been queued behind twelve higher-priority frames does you no good whatsoever. Utilisation is an average; a deadline is a worst case; on CAN they are almost unrelated.

the delay was not constant — it depended on where the event fell in the 10 ms cycle: event lands on an idle bus → R = 150 µs event lands just after the SYNC → R = 3,770 µs spread → 3.62 ms of pure timing uncertainty coarse feed rate 40 kg/s → 3.62 ms × 40 kg/s = 145 g of fill spread, bag to bag the mill's fix: raise the target by 150 g so no bag is ever short 150 g × 1,200 bags/shift = 180 kg/shift 180 kg × LKR 210/kg = LKR 37,800 per shift, given away × ~150 shifts ≈ LKR 5.7 million

Fault 2 — one node's clock was 0.6 % out, and the bit timing had no room for it. A third-party bag-hook proximity node (node 19) used a ceramic resonator rather than a crystal: ±0.5 % initial tolerance, drifting to about ±0.62 % at the 55 °C the panel reaches in the afternoon. The line's bit timing was the vendor tool's default — and the default sets the synchronisation jump width to one time quantum:

bit timing as configured: 40 MHz clock, BRP 5 → t_q = 125 ns, 16 t_q per bit Sync 1 + Prop 11 + Phase1 2 + Phase2 2 = 16 t_q = 2.000 µs → sample point 87.5 % SJW = 1 ← the default nobody changes permitted clock deviation per node (ISO 11898-1, both conditions must hold): df ≤ SJW / (2 × 10 × N) = 1 / 320 = 0.3125 % df ≤ min(P1,P2) / (2 × (13N − P2)) = 2 / 412 = 0.4854 % → budget = 0.3125 % = 3,125 ppm node 19's actual deviation, hot = ~6,200 ppm → 2.0× over budget

The consequence is not that node 19 stopped working. It is that node 19 sampled other people's bits in the wrong place on long runs of equal bits, saw stuff-rule violations that were not there, and transmitted error frames — and an error frame on CAN destroys the frame everybody was in the middle of. One badly clocked node degrades the whole segment. CAN's fault confinement then did exactly what it is designed to do: node 19's transmit error counter ratcheted up eight at a time, it went error-passive, and roughly twice a shift it went bus-off, recovered silently after 128 × 11 recessive bit times (2.816 ms), and rejoined. The bag-hook signal simply vanished and came back. The maintenance log recorded it as “palletiser sensor intermittent” and the sensor was replaced twice.

Fault 3 — the bus had grown 140 m and nobody re-checked the propagation segment. When the palletiser was added, the CAN trunk was extended from 95 m to 235 m, with a 6 m drop into the new junction box — and that junction box arrived from the supplier with its termination resistor fitted, giving the segment three terminators.

CAN arbitration requires the far end's dominant level to get back to you within the same bit, before your sample point: Prop_Seg ≥ 2 × (t_cable + t_loop) t_cable = 5 ns/m, t_loop ≈ 150 ns as configured, Prop_Seg = 11 t_q = 1,375 ns: L_max = (1,375/2 − 150) / 5 = 537.5 / 5 = 107.5 m actual trunk 235 m needs: Prop_Seg ≥ 2 × (235×5 + 150) = 2 × 1,325 = 2,650 ns > the whole 2,000 ns bit time → impossible at 500 kbit/s at ANY bit-timing configuration (absolute ceiling, 100 % prop segment: (1,000 − 150)/5 = 170 m) and, measured across CAN_H–CAN_L with the panel powered down: 41 Ω — three 120 Ω terminators in parallel is 40 Ω. It should read 60 Ω. longest stub 6.0 m, against a practical limit of about 0.6 m at 500 kbit/s

Why did it work at all? Because an arbitration error needs two nodes that are far apart to start transmitting inside the propagation window — and almost all of this machine's traffic came from the master and the drives, clustered in one MCC. Only the new palletiser node at the far end spoke from the far end, and only on events. So the third fault presented as “the palletiser node occasionally retransmits and its error counter climbs”, load-dependent, worse when the line was busy, and completely invisible from the master's diagnostic page.

Three faults, and three different models of the network. A priority model, in which an eleven-bit number silently decides which message is allowed to be late. A time model, in which every node must agree where the middle of a bit is, using nothing but its own crystal and the edges it can see. And a physical model, in which the length of the cable is not a wiring detail but a term inside the bit timing register. Those three — priority, time and physics — are what this module is about, and the line is repaired with every number recomputed on page 14.

What CAN Actually Is: A Wire That Arbitrates

The whole of CAN in one picture: a single terminated pair, no master, and a wired-AND that resolves simultaneous transmission without destroying anything. Everything else in this module is a consequence of that.

THE DEFINITION, AND WHY EACH WORD IS LOAD-BEARING CORE

CAN is a multi-master, message-oriented serial bus in which the identifier of a message is simultaneously its name and its priority, and in which simultaneous transmission is resolved bit by bit on the wire without loss of data or bandwidth.

Multi-master. There is no controller, no scheduler and no token. Any node may begin transmitting whenever the bus has been idle for three bit times. Nobody grants permission. This is the opposite of EtherCAT and Modbus, where a single master owns the schedule.
Message-oriented. Frames carry no source and no destination address. They carry an identifier that names the content — “engine speed”, “target reached” — and every node decides for itself, in hardware filters, whether it cares. Add a listener and you change nothing on the bus.
The identifier is the priority. Numerically lower wins. This is the most consequential design decision in the protocol and the one most often made by accident, by accepting a default from a configuration tool.
Non-destructive arbitration. When two nodes start together, one of them simply continues. Its frame is not damaged and not retransmitted. Contrast Ethernet's CSMA/CD, where a collision destroys both frames and both back off randomly.

That last property is what makes CAN's latency analysable. Because the highest-priority message always wins and is never damaged, its worst-case delay can be written down exactly — and because arbitration is deterministic, so can everybody else's. A bus with random back-off has no worst case at all. CAN, uniquely among cheap buses, gives you a number you can defend in a design review, and page 7 is about computing it.

WHERE IT CAME FROM, AND WHY IT REFUSED TO DIE STRETCH

Bosch began CAN in 1983 for one problem: a 1980s car was accumulating kilometres of point-to-point wiring, and the loom had become the heaviest and least reliable component in the vehicle. Version 1.0 was published in 1986, silicon arrived from Intel and Philips in 1987, the Mercedes W140 shipped it in 1991, and ISO 11898 standardised it in 1993. The extended 29-bit identifier (CAN 2.0B) followed because 2,048 identifiers were not enough for a truck.

Then something unusual happened: an automotive bus escaped into industry. CANopen (1995, now CiA 301) put an object dictionary and a device-profile system on top; DeviceNet did the same for Allen-Bradley plants; SAE J1939 became the language of every diesel engine, truck, harvester and generator set on earth. Forty years later CAN is still in almost every new vehicle, and it is in your machines because drives, weighers, encoders and inclinometers cost less with a CAN controller than with anything else. A CAN transceiver is a 60-cent part; two wires and a 120 Ω resistor is the entire infrastructure.

SIX THINGS CAN IS NOT — STATED PLAINLY CORE
Not fast. 1 Mbit/s is the classic ceiling, and an 8-byte frame consumes 135 bit times to deliver 64 bits of payload — 47 % efficiency at best. CAN FD improves the payload, not the arbitration. If you need megabytes, you need a different wire.
Not addressed. There is no node address in a classic CAN frame. Higher layers (CANopen node-IDs, J1939 source addresses) manufacture one by convention inside the identifier. The protocol itself does not know who sent anything.
Not fair. A high-priority message can, in principle, starve a low-priority one indefinitely. That is a feature — but it means somebody must own the identifier map, and on most machines nobody does.
Not routable. There is no network layer, no segmentation across buses, no addressing beyond the segment. Crossing segments requires a gateway that makes explicit decisions about what may pass.
Not secure. No authentication, no encryption, no sender identity. Anything on the wire can transmit anything, and can also deliberately drive a chosen node into bus-off (page 13). Security is architectural, not protocol-level.
Not a safety bus. CAN's error detection is superb and it is still not a SIL argument. Safety over CAN means CANopen Safety SRDOs or a hardwired channel — A52. Confusing “reliable” with “safe” is the most dangerous error in this module.

The Physical Layer: A Wired-AND Made of Copper

DOMINANT BEATS RECESSIVE, AND THAT IS THE WHOLE TRICK CORE

CAN's arbitration is not implemented in software or in a controller. It is implemented in the electrical behaviour of the driver stage. A CAN transceiver can actively pull the pair apart (dominant, logic 0) or it can let go (recessive, logic 1). If any node in the segment is pulling while others let go, the bus is pulled. The wire computes a logical AND of every node's output, continuously, at the speed of copper.

recessive (logic 1) CAN_H ≈ CAN_L ≈ 2.5 V V_diff ≈ 0 V (receiver: < 0.5 V) dominant (logic 0) CAN_H ≈ 3.5 V, CAN_L ≈ 1.5 V V_diff ≈ 2.0 V (receiver: > 0.9 V) bus level = AND of all node outputs: any node dominant → bus dominant all nodes recessive → bus recessive so a node transmitting RECESSIVE while reading back DOMINANT knows, within the same bit, that somebody else is also talking — and that somebody has a lower identifier. It stops. Nothing is damaged.

Notice what this buys and what it costs. It buys arbitration for free, with zero protocol overhead. It costs you a hard requirement that every node's dominant level must reach every other node and come back inside one bit time — which is the propagation constraint that will dominate page 5 and which set the 107.5 m ceiling in the cold open. It also costs you the ability for one faulty output stage stuck dominant to silence the entire segment, which no amount of protocol cleverness can fix.

A differential pair with a deliberately asymmetric drive: recessive is not driven, dominant is. The 400 mV band between the two receiver thresholds is the entire noise budget, and it is why ground offsets and long stubs show up as protocol errors rather than as marginal voltages.

TERMINATION, AND THE 60 Ω MEASUREMENT THAT SETTLES ARGUMENTS CORE

The cable is a 120 Ω characteristic-impedance twisted pair, and it is terminated with 120 Ω at each physical end of the trunk and nowhere else. Two terminators, always: the same rule as RS-485 in A01, and for the same reason — an unterminated end reflects the wavefront back into the segment, and a reflection arriving near the sample point is a bit error.

two 120 Ω terminators in parallel, measured across CAN_H–CAN_L, with the panel powered down: R = 120 × 120 / (120 + 120) = 60 Ω ← healthy ~120 Ω one terminator missing (the classic “works on the bench” fault) ~40 Ω THREE terminators — someone's junction box has one fitted ~30 Ω four terminators — every device shipped with one enabled < 5 Ω CAN_H shorted to CAN_L open the trunk is broken; measure from both ends to bracket it 55–65 Ω normal, including a few ohms of copper on a long run

This one measurement, which takes ninety seconds and needs a multimeter, resolves more CAN arguments than any other diagnostic in this module. It found the cold open's third terminator: 41 Ω. It must be done powered down, because a live transceiver's output stage is a low impedance and the reading becomes meaningless.

Split termination STRETCH replaces each 120 Ω resistor with two 60 Ω resistors in series and takes the midpoint to ground through a 4.7 nF capacitor. The differential impedance is unchanged, so the 60 Ω check still reads 60 Ω; what changes is that the common-mode impedance is now defined rather than floating, which measurably reduces radiated emissions and improves common-mode rejection. On any machine that has to pass an EMC test, or that sits next to a VFD, this is worth the two extra components.

STUBS, TOPOLOGY AND THE THINGS THAT QUIETLY RUIN A GOOD BUS CORE

CAN wants a line: one trunk, terminated at both ends, with every node hanging off it on the shortest possible drop. A star is not a topology, it is a collection of unterminated stubs, and it fails in exactly the load-dependent, weather-dependent, replace-the-sensor-twice way the cold open described.

a stub is unterminated, so it reflects. The reflection returns after 2L/v. require it to land well inside the transceiver's edge: 2L × 5 ns/m < t_rise / 5 → L_stub < t_rise[ns] / 50 metres fast transceiver, t_rise ≈ 20 ns (1 Mbit/s) → 0.40 m 500 kbit/s, t_rise ≈ 50 ns → 1.00 m slope-controlled, t_rise ≈ 300 ns (125 kbit/s) → 6.00 m published CiA guidance is roughly HALF of these numbers — that gap is your margin. the cold open's 6.0 m stub at 500 kbit/s was 6× over even the generous rule.

Note what the formula depends on: not the bit rate, but the edge rate. Slope-controlled transceivers deliberately slow their edges at low bit rates, which is why low-speed CAN tolerates sloppy wiring and 1 Mbit/s does not. It is the same physics as the RS-485 stub rule in A01, and it will matter far more again on page 9, because a CAN FD data phase at 2 Mbit/s has edges that make a tolerable 500 kbit/s star into an unusable FD segment.

Ground offsets. ISO 11898-2 classic transceivers survive −2 V to +7 V of common mode. A 60 m run between two panels fed from different distribution boards can exceed that during a motor start. Symptom: errors correlated with load, not with traffic. Cure: galvanically isolated transceivers at panel boundaries, or a common bonding conductor run with the cable.
Shield practice. Bond the screen at one end only if you are fighting a ground loop; bond both ends with a 360° gland if you are fighting radiated noise from a VFD. You cannot have both. Decide deliberately, document it, and see A46 and A56.
Cable choice. 120 Ω twisted pair, 0.34 mm² for trunks over 100 m (resistance, not impedance, is the driver). Never share a multicore with 400 V motor cores. Never run parallel to a VFD output for more than a couple of metres.
The other physical layers. ISO 11898-3 low-speed fault-tolerant CAN (≤ 125 kbit/s) keeps running single-wire if one line shorts or opens — used for car body electronics. SAE J2411 single-wire CAN goes further still. Both trade speed for survivability; both are rare in industrial machines.

The ACK slot deserves a second look, because it is the most misunderstood bit in CAN. It is not an end-to-end acknowledgement. It does not tell the transmitter that the intended recipient got the data, or that anyone acted on it — there is no intended recipient. It tells the transmitter only that at least one node on the segment saw a structurally valid frame. Two nodes on a bench, one of them switched off, produces the single most common first-day CAN symptom: the transmitter retries forever and its error counter climbs.

BIT STUFFING: WHY THE SAME MESSAGE TAKES DIFFERENT AMOUNTS OF TIME CORE

CAN has no separate clock line. Every receiver recovers timing from the edges in the data, so a long run of identical bits would let receivers drift. The protocol therefore inserts a stuff bit of opposite polarity after every five consecutive identical bits, from SOF through the end of the CRC sequence. Receivers remove them. They are invisible to your application and entirely visible in your timing budget.

stuffed region, standard frame with n data bytes: 1 SOF + 11 ID + 1 RTR + 1 IDE + 1 r0 + 4 DLC + 8n data + 15 CRC = 34 + 8n bits maximum stuff bits inserted into k bits: s = ⌊(k − 1) / 4⌋ n = 8 → k = 98 → s = ⌊97/4⌋ = 24 stuff bits total worst case = 47 fixed + 8n data + s n = 8 → 47 + 64 + 24 = 135 bits best case (data patterns that need no stuffing at all): n = 8 → 47 + 64 + 0 = 111 bits the same 8-byte message can occupy 111 or 135 bit times — a 21.6 % spread — depending on nothing but the values of your data.

This is a real and under-appreciated jitter source. Bit Timing: Where Cable Length, Sample Point and Crystal Quality Are One Budget

FOUR SEGMENTS, ONE BIT CORE

There is no clock line. Every node runs its own oscillator, divides it down, and decides for itself where the middle of a bit is. The protocol's job is to make twenty-two independently clocked nodes agree on that instant to within a fraction of a bit, using only the edges they can see in the data. It does this by dividing the nominal bit time into time quanta and grouping them into four segments.

t_q = BRP / f_clk (BRP = baud rate prescaler) Nominal Bit Time NBT = Sync_Seg + Prop_Seg + Phase_Seg1 + Phase_Seg2 = 1 t_q + 1…n + 1…n + 1…n = N t_q Sync_Seg exactly 1 t_q. An edge is EXPECTED here. Prop_Seg covers the physical round trip to the far end of the bus. Phase_Seg1 lengthened by resynchronisation if the edge came late. Phase_Seg2 shortened by resynchronisation if the edge came early. ← THE BIT IS SAMPLED AT THE END OF Phase_Seg1 sample point SP = (Sync + Prop + Phase1) / N = (N − Phase_Seg2) / N

Look hard at that last identity, because almost no reference states it and it collapses the whole problem: the sample point is decided entirely by Phase_Seg2. Prop_Seg and Phase_Seg1 sit on the same side of the sample point, so trading one against the other changes the sample point not at all. That gives you a free variable, and knowing it is free is what turns bit timing from a dark art into a four-step procedure.

This is the cold open's configuration, drawn: 87.5 % sample point, a generous propagation segment — and SJW = 1, meaning one time quantum of correction per edge, which was not enough for a node whose clock was 6,200 ppm out.

SYNCHRONISATION: HARD ONCE, SOFT EVERY EDGE AFTERWARDS CORE
Hard synchronisation happens exactly once per frame, on the falling edge of the SOF bit. The receiver simply restarts its bit time: Sync_Seg is placed on the edge and any accumulated phase error is discarded outright.
Resynchronisation happens on every recessive-to-dominant edge inside the frame. The controller measures the phase error — how far the edge landed from Sync_Seg — and corrects by lengthening Phase_Seg1 or shortening Phase_Seg2, but never by more than SJW time quanta in one go.
phase error e, measured in t_q: e = 0 edge fell inside Sync_Seg → nothing to do e > 0 edge was LATE (after Sync_Seg) → lengthen Phase_Seg1 by min(e, SJW) e < 0 edge was EARLY (before Sync_Seg) → shorten Phase_Seg2 by min(|e|, SJW) SJW ≤ min(4, Phase_Seg1, Phase_Seg2) — a hard limit in the standard and the reason SJW matters more than anything else on this page: SJW is how much drift you may correct at each opportunity. Set SJW = 1 and you may correct one time quantum per edge, no matter how far apart the edges are.

Only recessive-to-dominant edges are used, which halves your correction opportunities immediately. And bit stuffing guarantees an edge only every fifth bit — so in the worst case a node runs for a long time on nothing but its own oscillator. That interval, worked through in the Bosch specification, is what puts the constant 13 into the tolerance condition below.

THE TWO CONSTRAINTS THAT DECIDE EVERYTHING CORE

Constraint 1 — propagation. During arbitration and during the ACK slot, a node transmits a level and must read back what the whole bus is doing within the same bit. The signal must travel to the most distant node and the resulting bus level must travel back, before the sample point:

Prop_Seg ≥ 2 × (t_cable + t_loop) → L_max = (Prop_Seg/2 − t_loop) / 5 ns/m

Constraint 2 — oscillator tolerance. Two nodes' clocks differ. Between resynchronisation opportunities, that difference accumulates as phase error, and the correction machinery must be able to absorb it. ISO 11898-1 gives two conditions, and both must hold, where df is one node's relative clock deviation and everything is counted in time quanta:

(1) df ≤ SJW / (2 × 10 × N) (2) df ≤ min(Phase_Seg1, Phase_Seg2) / (2 × (13 × N − Phase_Seg2)) the factor 2 accounts for BOTH nodes deviating, in opposite directions the 10 and the 13 are the worst-case numbers of bit times the protocol permits between two usable resynchronisation edges permitted deviation = min of the two, and it is a PER-NODE figure: every oscillator on the segment must be inside it.

Now put the two constraints on the same page and something uncomfortable appears. A long bus needs a big Prop_Seg. A big Prop_Seg leaves fewer quanta for the phase segments. Small phase segments force a small SJW. And a small SJW and small phase segments both shrink the permitted clock deviation. Cable length, sample point and crystal quality are not three independent decisions. They are three ways of spending the same N time quanta.

Arbitration: Three Nodes Start Together and Nothing Is Lost

Compare with Ethernet CSMA/CD, where the same event destroys both frames and both stations back off by a random interval. CAN's arbitration costs nothing and produces a deterministic winner — which is the entire reason a worst-case latency can be computed at all.

THE MECHANISM IN FOUR SENTENCES CORE
1. A node may start transmitting when the bus has been idle for three bit times. Several may start on the same bit — that is normal, not a fault.
2. Every transmitting node reads back the bus level during every bit of the arbitration field. Reading back what you sent means you are still in the race.
3. A node that sent recessive and read dominant has lost. It stops immediately, becomes a receiver, and keeps the frame it was about to send in its buffer.
4. Because the identifier is sent MSB first and dominant is zero, the numerically lowest identifier wins. Always. On every bus. Forever.

Two tie-breaks complete the rules. If a data frame and a remote frame share an identifier, the data frame wins — RTR is dominant for data. If a standard and an extended frame share the same leading 11 bits, the standard frame wins, because after the 11 bits the standard frame sends a dominant RTR while the extended frame is obliged to send a recessive SRR. That is what SRR is for, and it is the only reason it exists.

WHY THIS MAKES THE PROPAGATION CONSTRAINT NON-NEGOTIABLE CORE

Arbitration is the reason page 5's propagation requirement is a requirement and not a guideline. Step 2 above says a node must read back the whole bus's level within the same bit it is transmitting. If the far node's dominant level has not arrived by your sample point, you do not know you have lost. You keep transmitting. So does the other node. Two transmitters now believe they own the bus, and the first bit on which they disagree produces a bit error, an error frame, and two retransmissions.

the symptom of a bus that is too long for its Prop_Seg: ✓ works perfectly when only nearby nodes are talking ✓ works perfectly at low load ✗ occasional error frames and retransmissions, ONLY when two nodes at opposite ends of the trunk start on the same bit ✗ frequency rises with traffic, because coincidence rises with traffic which is precisely why the cold open's fault looked like a flaky palletiser node rather than a cable that was 2.2× too long.
THE ONE FAULT CAN CANNOT DETECT: TWO NODES, ONE IDENTIFIER CORE

Arbitration compares bits, not senders. If two nodes transmit the same identifier, they sail through the entire arbitration field in perfect agreement — each reads back exactly what it sent — and both believe they have won. The disagreement surfaces at the first bit of the control or data field where their contents differ:

node A and node B both transmit ID 0x201: arbitration field identical bits → both still transmitting, both content DLC / data A sends recessive B sends dominant → A reads back dominant ≠ what it sent → A raises a bit error → A transmits an ERROR FRAME → the frame is destroyed for EVERYONE → both A and B retransmit... and repeat result: a storm of error frames, both nodes' TEC climbing 8 at a time, both reaching bus-off, recovering, and doing it again. No diagnostic anywhere reports “duplicate identifier”, because the protocol has no concept of who sent what.

The cure is entirely administrative and it is the single most valuable document on a CAN machine: an identifier map. One table, every ID on the segment, its producer, its consumers, its period and its deadline. It costs an afternoon. Nobody has one. Building it is the deliverable of week 5 of the study path on page 19, and it is how you find duplicate identifiers before they find you.

FAIRNESS, STARVATION, AND WHY IT IS NOT A PROBLEM IN PRACTICE STRETCH

A purist objection to CAN is that a stream of high-priority messages can starve a low-priority one forever. This is formally true and practically almost irrelevant, and understanding why is the bridge to the next page. High-priority traffic on a real machine is periodic: it arrives at a bounded rate, so it consumes a bounded fraction of the bus, so a lower-priority message is delayed by a bounded amount. Starvation requires unbounded high-priority load, which means somebody has designed a message set that is infeasible on any bus.

The formal version of that sentence is the response-time analysis of page 7. It is not a rule of thumb, it is not a simulation, and it is not a bus-load percentage. It is an exact worst-case bound — and CAN is one of very few field buses cheap enough to be everywhere and deterministic enough to have one.

THE THREE TERMS CORE

A message m becomes ready to send at some instant. Three things then stand between it and the last bit leaving the wire.

1 · Blocking, Bm
A lower-priority frame may already be halfway out. CAN cannot pre-empt a frame in progress, so you wait for the longest one that could be there:
Bm = max{ Ck : k lower priority }
Usually one 8-byte frame — 270 µs at 500 kbit/s. You pay this once.
2 · Interference
Every higher-priority message that becomes ready during your wait goes first — and while you wait, more of them arrive. This is the term that recurses, and it is why the equation must be solved iteratively rather than evaluated.
3 · Transmission, Cm
Your own frame, at its worst-case stuffed length: (55 + 10n)τ standard, (80 + 10n)τ extended. Once arbitration is won this cannot be interrupted, which is the good news.
The queueing delay is the fixed point of: wm = Bm + ∑j ∈ hp(m) ⌈ (wm + Jj + τbit) / Tj ⌉ × Cj and the response time is: Rm = Jm + wm + Cm hp(m) = all messages with a LOWER identifier Tj = period (or minimum inter-arrival time) of message j Jj = queueing jitter — how late the software can be in handing the frame to the controller. Measure it; do not assume zero. τbit = one bit time. The correction term: a higher-priority message that becomes ready during the bit currently on the wire still gets to contend at the next arbitration. solve by iteration from w(0) = Bm, stopping when w stops changing. if w exceeds the busy period, the message set is unschedulable — not slow, not marginal: there is no bit rate at which that identifier map works.

Two properties make this usable. First, the iteration is monotonically increasing and converges in a handful of steps for any feasible set. Second — and this is what makes CAN unique among cheap buses — the bound is exact, not statistical. There is no confidence interval and no simulation run. If R ≤ D, the message cannot be late; if R > D, there exists a real arrival pattern that makes it late, and on a machine that runs for years, “exists” means “happens”.

THE ERROR TERM, AND THE PAPER THAT REFUTED EVERY TEXTBOOK STRETCH

Two refinements belong in any analysis you would defend in front of a customer.

Errors cost bus time. Every error frame destroys whatever was in progress and forces a retransmission. A conservative and widely used allowance adds, to the queueing window, a term for the errors that can occur within it:

E(w) = ⌈ w / Terror ⌉ × ( 31τbit + maxk ∈ hp(m) ∪ {m} Ck ) 31 τbit bounds the error signalling itself: 6 bits of active error flag, up to 6 more from superposed flags of other nodes, 8 bits of error delimiter, 3 bits of intermission, plus the part of the frame already sent. Terror must come from a MEASUREMENT on your plant, not from a guess. On a healthy industrial segment it is minutes; next to an unfiltered VFD it can be milliseconds — which is the same conversation as A56.

And the analysis you will find in most books is subtly wrong. Tindell's original 1994 formulation assumed each message could be delayed by at most one instance of itself. Davis, Burns, Bril and Lukkien showed in 2007 (Real-Time Systems, “Controller Area Network schedulability analysis: Refuted, revisited and revised”) that this is optimistic whenever a message's deadline can exceed its period: a burst can queue two instances of the same message, and the second waits for the first. The corrected analysis examines every instance released in the priority-level busy period and takes the worst. For the common industrial case D ≤ T the two give the same answer — but if you are quoting numbers for a message whose deadline is longer than its period, use the revised form and say so. Knowing that this correction exists, and why, is a fair marker of the difference between having read about CAN and having studied it.

ASSIGNING IDENTIFIERS: THE ONE DECISION THAT MATTERS CORE

If the response time is decided by who is above you, then the identifier map is the real-time design. There is a theorem for this and it is refreshingly simple: for a message set on CAN, deadline-monotonic priority ordering is optimal — if any priority assignment makes the set schedulable, then assigning the lowest identifiers to the shortest deadlines does too.

The identifier allocation procedure 1. List every message: producer, bytes, period T, deadline D, consequence of lateness. 2. Sort by DEADLINE, shortest first. Not by period. Not by node number. Not by “importance”. Not by the order the configuration tool offers them. 3. Assign identifiers in that order, leaving gaps — use 0x080, 0x100, 0x180, 0x200... so that the message you add next year has somewhere to go. 4. Compute R for every message. Every one, not just the interesting ones. 5. Write the table into the machine documentation. It IS the documentation.

THE ERROR FRAME — AND WHY IT IS EVERYBODY'S PROBLEM CORE

This is the design decision that most surprises engineers coming from Ethernet. When any node detects an error, it does not discard the frame quietly. It immediately transmits an error flag: six consecutive dominant bits. Six dominant bits deliberately violate the stuff rule, so every other node also detects an error and transmits its own flag, and the flags superpose. The frame in progress is destroyed for every node on the segment, and the transmitter retransmits it automatically.

ERROR ACTIVE node: error flag = 6 dominant bits → destroys the current frame globally ERROR PASSIVE node: error flag = 6 recessive bits → disturbs nobody; the node has been demoted to complaining quietly to itself then, in both cases: 8 bits error delimiter + 3 bits intermission cost on the wire per error event: 17 to 23 bit times of signalling, plus the retransmission of the destroyed frame.

Why globalise a local error? Because CAN guarantees data consistency: either every node accepts a frame or no node does. Without global error signalling, a frame corrupted only on one branch of the segment would be accepted by some nodes and rejected by others, and a distributed system built on inconsistent broadcasts is far harder to reason about than one where errors are loud. The price is that a single defective node — the cold open's node 19 — degrades the entire bus. Fault confinement is the mechanism that stops that price from being unbounded.

THE COUNTERS: EIGHT UP, ONE DOWN CORE

Every controller keeps two counters in silicon. They are not configurable, not resettable by software, and identical on every CAN device ever made — which is precisely why they are the most trustworthy diagnostic on the bus.

TEC — transmit error counter. +8 when a transmission fails. −1 when one succeeds.
REC — receive error counter. +1 when a reception fails (+8 in specific cases). −1 when one succeeds.
TEC and REC < 128 → ERROR ACTIVE normal; transmits dominant error flags either ≥ 128 → ERROR PASSIVE recessive error flags; must also wait 8 extra bits (“suspend transmission”) after every frame — a quiet handicap TEC > 255 → BUS OFF the node disconnects itself entirely recovery from bus-off: wait for 128 occurrences of 11 consecutive recessive bits = 1,408 bit times of idle-equivalent at 500 kbit/s = 2.816 ms minimum — longer on a busy bus, because those 11-bit windows have to actually occur.

The 8:1 ratio is not arbitrary. It is the protocol's blame-assignment mechanism. When an error occurs, the transmitter and every receiver all see it — but the transmitter's counter moves eight times faster. Over repeated errors the node that was talking is thrown off long before any node that was merely listening. Since the talker is statistically the most likely cause, CAN removes the probable culprit and leaves the probable victims running. Derivation 4 turns that into two clean thresholds: a transmitter is confined once more than one in nine of its transmissions fails; a receiver needs one in two. That 4.5× difference in sensitivity is the whole design.

Every CAN controller ever made implements exactly this, in silicon, with no configurable parameters. That is why the counters are the most trustworthy number on the bus — and why the 8:1 asymmetry, which decides who gets thrown off, is worth more attention than the thresholds themselves.

WHAT FAULT CONFINEMENT CANNOT DO CORE
A transceiver stuck dominant. The node never transmits a frame, so its TEC never increments, so it is never confined — and the bus is held at dominant forever. Every other node counts up to bus-off around it. The only diagnosis is a voltmeter, and the only cure is unplugging nodes one at a time. Budget for this: it is the classic “the whole line is dead” call.
A node just under the threshold. At a 10 % failure rate a node stays error-active indefinitely, costing the bus an error frame and a retransmission for every tenth of its frames. Everyone else's response times degrade and nothing is ever reported.
Silent auto-recovery. Most controllers re-enter the bus automatically after bus-off. Unless your firmware or PLC explicitly reads the controller's state, a node can leave and rejoin the bus dozens of times a shift with no record anywhere. Always surface bus-off as an alarm.
Automatic retransmission. A node with something urgent and a persistent fault will retry forever, at the highest priority it owns. Some controllers offer one-shot mode (transmit once, no retry) — the right choice for data that is worthless when late, such as a cyclic sensor value that will be superseded in 10 ms.

STRETCH There is a sixth frame type worth knowing: the overload frame, structurally identical to an error frame, sent by a receiver that needs more time before the next frame. On modern controllers it is essentially extinct — if you see one in a trace, you are looking at a very old device or a controller in trouble, and it is a genuinely useful clue.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A bus analyser shows node 7 with TEC = 142, node 3 with REC = 96, and every other node with REC between 4 and 9. Which node is most likely defective?

C, though B is the right first suspicion and is how most people solve it. Read the numbers properly. Node 7's TEC is high because node 7 was transmitting when errors occurred — the 8:1 ratio guarantees the talker's counter leads, whether or not the talker is at fault. Node 3's REC of 96 against everyone else's 4–9 says node 3 is seeing errors the rest of the bus does not, which is an electrical statement about node 3's position, not a statement about node 3's health. Put them together: errors are being made on the stretch between node 7 and node 3, and everyone else is only seeing the resulting error frames. The counters tell you where the error was seen; only the cable route tells you where it was made. Now go and look at what runs alongside that stretch.

CAN FD: Same Arbitration, Faster Middle

THE ONE IDEA CORE

Arbitration is what forces CAN to be slow. A node must read back the whole bus within one bit, so the bit must be long enough for a round trip to the far end. But once arbitration is over, nobody is contending any more — exactly one node is transmitting, and there is no reason for the bits to stay long. CAN FD does precisely and only this: it keeps the arbitration field at the nominal bit rate, switches to a faster bit rate for the data and CRC, and switches back for the acknowledgement. Everything else — the wired-AND, priority by identifier, the error frame, the counters, the response-time analysis — is unchanged.

THE THREE THINGS THAT CATCH PEOPLE CORE
1 · A classic node does not ignore FD frames — it destroys them. A classic controller expects r0 to be dominant. In an FD frame that bit is FDF and it is recessive, so the classic controller raises a form error and transmits an error frame, killing the FD frame for everyone and driving its own counters up. “CAN FD tolerant” classic controllers merely stay silent during FD frames — they still cannot read them. A single non-FD node on the segment makes FD unusable.
2 · The data phase makes topology matter again. A 2 Mbit/s bit is 500 ns; a 5 Mbit/s bit is 200 ns. Stubs and star points that were electrically invisible at 500 kbit/s now ring for a significant fraction of a bit. Symptom: errors only in the data phase, only at the higher rate, only at certain nodes. Fix the topology, or fit CAN SIC (signal-improvement, CiA 601-4) transceivers that actively damp the ringing.
3 · Transmitter delay compensation must be configured. Above roughly 1 Mbit/s the transceiver loop delay is comparable to a data bit, so the transmitter cannot check its own bit at the normal sample point. TDC measures that delay at the FDF/res edge and places a secondary sample point at delay + SSP offset. Get it wrong and you get bit errors that appear only above a certain data rate — a fault that looks electrical and is a register.

STRETCH ISO vs non-ISO CAN FD. Bosch's original 2012 CAN FD had a defect: certain bit errors interacting with stuff bits could survive the CRC. ISO 11898-1:2015 fixed it by adding the stuff-bit-count field and changing the CRC initialisation. The two variants are not interoperable. Most modern controllers can be set to either; the correct answer is ISO, and the only reason to know non-ISO exists is that a decade-old device in your machine might still be it.

CAN XL, BRIEFLY AND HONESTLY RESEARCH

CAN XL (CiA 610-1, folded into ISO 11898-1:2024) is the third generation: payloads up to 2,048 bytes, data-phase rates up to 20 Mbit/s with SIC-XL transceivers, and a redesigned header carrying an 11-bit priority identifier alongside an 8-bit SDU type and a virtual-CAN-network identifier — which lets an XL frame carry a tunnelled Ethernet frame or an FD frame as its payload. The intent is a single physical layer that spans from a 60-cent sensor to a zonal vehicle gateway (A38). Silicon is shipping, industrial adoption is early, and there is no reason to specify it for a Sri Lankan machine today. Know that it exists and that it keeps the arbitration — which tells you everything about what the designers believe is CAN's actual value.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

An engineer upgrades a loaded 500 kbit/s bus to CAN FD (500 k / 2 M). Eleven of the twelve nodes are new FD devices; the twelfth is an existing classic inclinometer that “only listens anyway”. What happens?

C. “Only listens” is exactly the misconception. Every CAN receiver is an active participant in error signalling — that is the whole point of page 8. A classic controller reads FDF recessive where the standard says r0 must be dominant, calls it a form error, and transmits six dominant bits into the middle of somebody else's frame. Not one FD frame will complete. Its REC climbs, it goes error-passive, and from then on it disturbs slightly less while the bus stays broken. D is what people assume “backward compatible” means; CAN FD is backward compatible in the sense that an FD controller can send classic frames, not in the sense that classic nodes tolerate FD ones. The audit before any FD upgrade is a single question asked of every device on the segment: FD-capable, FD-tolerant, or neither?

CANopen: An Object Dictionary Bolted to a Priority Bus

WHAT CANOPEN ADDS, AND WHAT IT COSTS CORE

Raw CAN gives you identifiers and eight bytes. It gives you no way to ask a device what it is, no way to configure it, no device model, no state machine and no naming. CANopen (CiA 301) supplies all five, and it does so by imposing two conventions: every device exposes an object dictionary, and every device takes a node-ID from 1 to 127 which is baked into its default identifiers.

The object dictionary is a 16-bit-indexed, 8-bit-sub-indexed table of everything the device has: its identity, its configuration, its process data, its errors. 0x6041 sub 0 on a drive is the CiA 402 status word. It is the device's entire API, and it is standardised across vendors for the parts that matter.
The node-ID makes the addressing that CAN lacks. It is added to a base value to produce the default COB-IDs for that node's PDOs, SDOs and heartbeat — which is enormously convenient on day one and is, as the cold open showed, a real-time design made by an electrician with a rotary switch.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A commissioning engineer reduces bus load by changing every drive's TPDO transmission type from 1 (every SYNC) to 4 (every fourth SYNC), keeping the 10 ms SYNC. What happens to the worst-case response time of a low-priority message?

B — unless you also stagger the phases, and this is one of the most valuable practical points in CANopen. Reducing every PDO to every fourth SYNC divides the average load by four, and the response-time analysis does not care about averages: it asks what the worst possible instant looks like, and the worst possible instant is the one SYNC in four where every drive's counter rolls over together. If all twelve PDOs share a phase, the burst is identical to before and the interference term barely moves. Give them phases — drives 1–3 on SYNC 0, 4–6 on SYNC 1, and so on — and the burst really does divide, and so does R. A is the answer that gets written in the commissioning report. Reduction ratios without phasing is exactly the same trap PROFINET presents in A02, from a completely different direction.

J1939, OBD and UDS: The Language of Every Diesel Engine on Earth

WHY THIS PAGE IS IN AN INDUSTRIAL MODULE CORE

Every generator set, forklift, harvester, excavator, truck, bus and refrigerated container in Sri Lanka speaks SAE J1939. So does the engine in the standby genset behind your factory. If you can read a J1939 bus you can extract engine hours, fuel rate, coolant temperature, load percentage and fault codes from equipment whose vendor charges a subscription for exactly that data — and you can build the condition-monitoring that A42 and A23 assume you already have.

J1939 uses 29-bit extended identifiers, 250 kbit/s (500 kbit/s and CAN FD in newer J1939-14/-22 networks), and it partitions those 29 bits into a fixed structure. Once you can decode the identifier by hand, most of the protocol follows.

Because the priority bits are the top three, and CAN arbitration compares from the top, J1939's priority field is CAN's priority field. Nothing is bolted on. The higher layer simply chose to spend its three most significant identifier bits on making that explicit — which is the design CANopen did not make.

MORE THAN EIGHT BYTES: THE TRANSPORT PROTOCOL STRETCH

DM1 with several active faults, or the 64-bit NAME, or a software identification string, does not fit in 8 bytes. J1939-21 defines two ways to send up to 1,785 bytes across a sequence of frames.

BAM — Broadcast Announce Message. The sender broadcasts TP.CM_BAM (total size, packet count, target PGN), then a stream of TP.DT packets each carrying a sequence number and 7 data bytes. No flow control and no acknowledgement, with a mandated 50–200 ms gap between packets. Lose one packet and the whole message is lost silently.
RTS/CTS — destination-specific. The sender requests to send, the receiver grants a number of packets, data flows, the receiver acknowledges. Reliable, and only usable point-to-point. Only one RTS/CTS session may be open between a given pair at a time — a limit that surprises people writing diagnostic tools.

Note what BAM does to your response-time analysis: a 1,785-byte BAM is 256 consecutive frames at a bounded but slow rate, from one source address, occupying its priority band for many seconds. If somebody's diagnostic tool starts one on a bus carrying real-time control, the interference term in page 7 changes completely. J1939-22 (CAN FD) replaces this machinery with multi-PG frames that pack several PGNs into 64 bytes, which is a large part of why it exists.

Safety and Security: Two Words That Are Not the Same Word

RELIABLE IS NOT SAFE CORE

Page 8 gave CAN a residual undetected error probability below 4.7 × 10−11 times the frame error rate — roughly one undetected corruption in decades. It is entirely reasonable to be impressed by that number, and entirely wrong to use it as a safety argument, for three separate reasons.

1 · It answers the wrong question. Functional safety asks “what happens when this fails?”, not “how rarely does it fail?”. A message that is never sent passes the CRC test perfectly. There is nothing in classic CAN that notices a message that stopped arriving.
2 · The model excludes the real failure modes. The figure assumes randomly distributed bit errors. It says nothing about a node stuck dominant, a duplicated identifier, a stale value in a buffer, a repeated old frame, or an operator with a laptop.
3 · It is not certifiable on its own. A SIL or PL claim needs a described safe state, a defined fault-detection time, systematic-capability evidence and an assessment. “The CRC is very good” is not any of those. See A52.
CANOPEN SAFETY: HOW IT IS ACTUALLY DONE CORE

EN 50325-5 / CiA 304 defines the SRDO — Safety-Relevant Data Object, and it is a small, clean design worth understanding because the same pattern recurs in PROFIsafe and FSoE (A02, A03).

an SRDO is TWO CAN frames on TWO identifiers: frame 1: the data, normally frame 2: the same data, bit-inverted, on a different COB-ID both must arrive within SRVT (safety-relevant object validation time), and the pair must repeat within SCT (safeguard cycle time). the receiving safety controller declares the safe state if: ✗ the two frames disagree → corruption ✗ only one arrives → loss ✗ they arrive more than SRVT apart → a stale half ✗ SCT expires with no fresh pair → the producer has stopped that last line is the safety function. Everything else CAN already does. what CAN could never do is notice SILENCE, and that is what a timeout is for.

Three consequences follow immediately. The bus below is a black channel — the safety layer makes no assumptions about it at all, so its CRC, its error frames and its counters are irrelevant to the safety argument. The safety function is implemented in certified devices at both ends, not in the wire. And the SCT is a real-time parameter that lands in the response-time analysis of page 7 and then in an ISO 13855 separation distance (A35): safety response time buys guard distance, in millimetres, and the arithmetic is unforgiving.

worked: SCT 20 ms + safety controller reaction 15 ms + output/contactor 30 ms = 65 ms of safety response time ISO 13855 hand approach: S = K×T + C = 1600 mm/s × 0.065 s + 850 mm = 104 + 850 = 954 mm of guard distance halve the SCT to 10 ms → S = 938 mm. 16 mm for a 2× faster bus. the network is almost never the term worth optimising — know that before someone sells you a faster one on safety grounds.
SECURITY: THERE IS NONE, AND THE ATTACKS ARE ELEGANT CORE

A classic CAN frame has no sender identity, no authentication, no integrity protection beyond a CRC that any attacker can compute, no encryption and no access control. Anything electrically on the bus can transmit anything, claiming to be anything. Three attack classes follow directly, and the third is the one worth remembering because it uses the protocol's own safety mechanism as the weapon.

Eavesdrop and replay. Everything is broadcast, so a passive tap sees the whole machine. Record a frame and re-send it later and it is indistinguishable from the original — there is no sequence number, no nonce and no timestamp in the frame.
Inject and out-prioritise. Transmit the same identifier as a legitimate node, more often. Since arbitration is by identifier and there is no source check, your frames are as valid as anybody's, and a lower identifier beats every legitimate message on the bus.
The bus-off attack (Cho & Shin, 2016). Synchronise to a victim's periodic frame. Transmit a dominant bit at a moment the victim is transmitting recessive, after arbitration. The victim's bit-monitoring raises an error, its TEC goes up 8, and it retries. Repeat 32 times and the victim puts itself into bus-off. Keep doing it after each recovery and one specific ECU is silenced permanently — using nothing but the protocol working exactly as designed.
Why it is so hard to defend. Fault confinement cannot distinguish a malicious dominant bit from a noisy one; that is the whole point of it being cheap and deterministic. The defence has to come from a layer that does not exist in classic CAN.

Recap, Failure Modes and Quiz

THE EIGHT IDEAS WORTH KEEPING CORE
1 · The wire arbitrates. Dominant beats recessive, so the lowest identifier wins, bit by bit, with nothing destroyed and no bandwidth lost. Every other property of CAN follows from this.
2 · The identifier is the priority. Permanently, irrevocably, and usually assigned by a configuration tool's default rather than by a deadline. It is the most consequential number on the machine.
3 · Load is not latency. A message can miss its deadline on a 27 % bus and meet it comfortably on a 71 % one. Compute R = B + ∑interference + C; do not quote a percentage.
4 · Frame length is data-dependent. Bit stuffing makes the same 8-byte message take 111 or 135 bit times. Always analyse with 55 + 10n, the only length you can promise.
5 · Length lives in Prop_Seg, the sample point lives in Phase_Seg2, the clock budget lives in SJW. Three faces of one budget of N time quanta — and PS1 is free until it reaches PS2.
6 · An error frame belongs to everybody. One node's local error destroys the frame globally, deliberately, to guarantee consistency. One bad node degrades the whole segment until confinement removes it.
7 · Eight up, one down. A transmitter is confined once more than one in nine of its transmissions fails; a receiver needs one in two. That asymmetry is the protocol assigning blame, and it is why counters localise but do not convict.
8 · Reliable is not safe, and nothing is secure. CAN cannot detect silence and has no sender identity. Safety needs a timeout and a safe state; security needs architecture. Neither is bought with a better CRC.
⚠ SIX FAILURE MODES, AND HOW EACH PRESENTS
⚠ The default identifier

Cause: the higher layer's node-number-based COB-ID map shipped unchanged, so priority encodes the order somebody turned rotary switches. Effect: an urgent message sits behind every drive PDO on the bus. Presents as: a process variable that is right on average and occasionally very wrong, at low bus load, with no errors anywhere. Test: compute R. Fix: one identifier, persisted in the DCF.

⚠ SJW left at 1

Cause: the vendor tool's default. Effect: the oscillator budget is a third of what a sane split gives, so any node with a ceramic resonator or a hot panel drifts outside it. Presents as: error frames from one node, worse in the afternoon, that follow the node when you move it. Test: read the bit timing registers of every node and compute both tolerance conditions. Fix: re-time to PS1 = PS2 = SJW = 3 and accept the small loss of length.

⚠ The bus that grew

Cause: a machine extension added trunk without anyone re-checking Prop_Seg. Effect: two distant nodes starting on the same bit cannot see each other in time, so arbitration fails and both transmit. Presents as: errors that rise with traffic and only involve nodes at opposite ends. Test: measure the trunk, compute L_max from the loaded Prop_Seg. Fix: lower the bit rate, or split the segment with a bridge.

⚠ The third terminator

Cause: a junction box or a device shipped with termination fitted or enabled. Effect: 40 Ω instead of 60 Ω, a smaller dominant differential, and a spent noise margin. Presents as: nothing at all until the electrical environment worsens, then intermittents at the far end. Test: 60 Ω, powered down. Ninety seconds. Fix: remove it and record the reading.

⚠ Silent bus-off recovery

Cause: controllers auto-recover after bus-off and nobody reads the controller state. Effect: a node leaves and rejoins the bus dozens of times a shift, each absence lasting a few milliseconds. Presents as: an “intermittent sensor” that is replaced twice and keeps failing. Test: ip -details -statistics link show can0, or the PLC's node state word. Fix: alarm it — then diagnose the underlying error.

⚠ Two nodes, one identifier

Cause: a spare commissioned with a duplicate node-ID, or two hand-assigned IDs that collided. Effect: arbitration passes because the bits are identical; the disagreement surfaces in the data field as bit errors. Presents as: perfect health for hours, then a storm of error frames the moment the two data sets differ — with no diagnostic naming the cause. Test: capture the bus and count producers per identifier. Fix: the identifier map, which is why you build one.

✍ QUIZ — SIX QUESTIONS, TAP TO REVEAL

1. How long is a standard 8-byte CAN frame at 250 kbit/s in the worst case, and what fraction of the wire is actually payload?

tap to revealC = (55 + 10×8) = 135 bit times. At 250 kbit/s the bit time is 4 µs, so C = 540 µs. The payload is 64 bits in 135 bit times = 47.4 %, giving an effective payload rate of 64 bits / 540 µs = 118.5 kbit/s from a 250 kbit/s wire. Note this is the same 47.4 % at every bit rate — the efficiency is a property of the frame format, not of the speed.

2. A 2-byte message at 500 kbit/s has four higher-priority 8-byte messages, all with a 5 ms period, and one lower-priority 8-byte message. What is its worst-case response time?

tap to revealC(8) = 270 µs, C(2) = 150 µs, B = 270 µs. Iterate: w¹ = 270 + 4×⌈272/5000⌉×270 = 270 + 1,080 = 1,350 µs; w² = 270 + 4×⌈1,352/5000⌉×270 = 1,350 — converged, all ceilings still 1. R = 1,350 + 150 = 1,500 µs = 1.5 ms. Utilisation here is only 4×270/5000 + a little = 22 %, which again tells you nothing useful about the 1.5 ms.

3. A bus runs 500 kbit/s with N = 16 tq, Prop 11, PS1 2, PS2 2, SJW 1. What is the oscillator budget, and what would it cost to raise SJW to 3?

tap to revealAs configured: condition 1 gives 1/(2×10×16) = 0.3125 %, condition 2 gives 2/(2×(208−2)) = 0.4854 %, so the budget is the smaller: 0.3125 %. To use SJW = 3 you need PS1 and PS2 both ≥ 3, so with N = 16 the split becomes Prop 9, PS1 3, PS2 3: condition 1 3/320 = 0.9375 %, condition 2 3/(2×(208−3)) = 0.7317 % → budget 0.73 %, a 2.3× improvement. The cost: Prop drops from 11 to 9 tq (1,125 ns), so L_max = (1,125/2 − 150)/5 falls from 107.5 m to 82.5 m, and the sample point moves from 87.5 % to 81.25 %. That is the trilemma in one question — and note that the better answer is to go to N = 20 with BRP 4, which gives SJW 3, 85 % and 100 m all at once.

4. A node transmits 250 frames per second and 15 % of its transmissions fail. Does it survive, and if not, when does it go bus-off?

tap to reveal15 % is above the one-in-nine (11.1 %) threshold, so no — it is heading for bus-off. Drift per second: 250 × (0.15×8 − 0.85×1) = 250 × 0.35 = +87.5 TEC/s. Error-passive (TEC ≥ 128) after 1.46 s; bus-off (TEC > 255) after 2.93 s. Recovery needs 128 × 11 = 1,408 bit times = 2.816 ms at 500 kbit/s, after which it rejoins and starts again — a roughly 3-second flap cycle that a 100 ms PLC scan will register only as occasional missing data.

5. One classic (non-FD) node remains on a segment that has been upgraded to CAN FD. It is a receive-only device. What happens?

tap to revealEvery FD frame is destroyed. The classic controller expects r0 to be dominant; in an FD frame that bit is FDF and is recessive, so it raises a form error and transmits a six-bit dominant error flag into the middle of the frame — and every receiver on CAN is an active participant in error signalling, whether or not it ever transmits data. Its REC climbs until it goes error-passive, after which it disturbs less but the bus is still unusable for FD. “Receive-only” and “harmless” are not the same thing on CAN. The pre-upgrade audit is one question per device: FD-capable, FD-tolerant, or neither.

6. Decode J1939 identifier 0x18FEEE00 completely.

tap to revealTop byte 0x18 = 0001 1000; the low five bits are the 29-bit field's bits 28–24 = 1 1000, so priority = 110 = 6, EDP = 0, DP = 0. PF = 0xFE = 254, which is ≥ 240, so this is PDU2 — broadcast, and PS is a group extension rather than a destination. PGN = (0≪17)|(0≪16)|(0xFE≪8)|0xEE = 0xFEEE = 65262, which is ET1, Engine Temperature 1 — coolant, fuel and oil temperatures, broadcast once per second. SA = 0x00 = engine #1. Priority 6 rather than 3 because a temperature that is one second old is still useful and a torque figure that is one cycle old is not.

WE4 — FAULT-CONFINEMENT FORENSICS CORE

Problem. A 500 kbit/s bus in a rubber factory. Symptoms: occasional missing data from the mixer station. Measurements: node 7 TEC = 136 (error-passive), node 3 REC = 96, all other nodes REC 4–9, error-frame rate 62/s, bus load 22 %, and the errors cluster when the packer starts. Node 7 transmits about 50 frames/s and roughly 15 % of them fail. Diagnose, and predict what happens next.

Step 1 — read the counters as evidence about POSITION, not guilt. node 7 TEC 136 → node 7 was TRANSMITTING when errors occurred. The 8:1 ratio guarantees the talker's counter leads, whether or not the talker is at fault. node 3 REC 96 → node 3 SEES errors the rest of the bus does not. That is an electrical statement about node 3's position. everyone else 4–9 → they only ever saw the resulting error frames. → errors are being MADE on the stretch between nodes 7 and 3. Everyone else is a witness. The counters tell you where the error was seen; only the cable route tells you where it was made. Step 2 — will node 7 survive? drift = 50 × (0.15×8 − 0.85×1) = 50 × 0.35 = +17.5 TEC/s 15 % > 1/9 = 11.1 %, so no. from TEC 136 to bus-off (256): 120 / 17.5 = 6.9 s recovery: 128 × 11 = 1,408 bit times = 2.816 ms → node 7 will drop off the bus roughly every 7 seconds and rejoin three milliseconds later, indefinitely, and no PLC scan will see it. Step 3 — the trap in the average. 62 errors/s sounds like one every 16 ms — survivable. But interference is BURSTY. A 40 ms burst during the packer's start is ~2,500 bit times of error signalling and retransmission, i.e. the bus is effectively unavailable for that whole window. The average rate tells you the bus is unhealthy; only a timestamped capture tells you whether any single event was long enough to break something.

Step 4 — act. Walk the cable between nodes 7 and 3 and find what the packer's drive shares with it: a common tray, a parallel run alongside a motor cable, a panel gland where the screen was cut back. Confirm with a scope on the common mode during a packer start. The protocol evidence localised the victim in ten minutes with no tools; the plant evidence identifies the aggressor. Neither alone is a diagnosis. Cross-reference A56 and A46.

WE5 — A J1939 CONDITION-MONITORING PROJECT ON A STANDBY GENSET CORE

On a genset bus that is harmless; on a vehicle bus carrying real-time control it changes the interference term in the response-time analysis of page 7, which is exactly the kind of thing a diagnostic tool does at the worst possible moment.

Two costed alternatives: (a) hardwired dual-channel guard switch to a safety relay, ~LKR 45,000, no network involvement, no analysis needed; (b) CANopen Safety SRDOs between certified safety devices, with the safeguard cycle time chosen from the ISO 13855 separation distance — more expensive, more capable, and requiring a safety controller at both ends. 4. Refuse “just raise the bit rate to 1 Mbit/s” before it is proposed: the trunk is 235 m and the 1 Mbit/s propagation limit is about 55 m, so it is not a trade-off, it is an impossibility (→ P4). Marks are for pairing every refusal with a priced alternative, and for noticing that the expensive part of this request is free and the free-sounding part is the one that needs money.

Four Derivations from First Principles

D1 — THE WORST-CASE FRAME LENGTH, AND WHY 55 + 10n IS EXACT CORE

Claim. A standard CAN data frame carrying n data bytes occupies at most 55 + 10n bit times, and an extended frame at most 80 + 10n. These are not approximations — they are exact, for every n.

Step 1 — the stuff-bit bound. A stuff bit of opposite polarity is inserted after every five consecutive identical bits. How many can be inserted into a stuffed field of k bits? The first stuff bit can appear no earlier than after 5 original bits. It is of OPPOSITE polarity, so it breaks the run — but it also counts as the first bit of the next run. So after the first, a further stuff bit can appear after every 4 more ORIGINAL bits. s(k) = 1 + ⌊(k − 5)/4⌋ = ⌊(k − 5 + 4)/4⌋ = ⌊(k − 1)/4⌋ Step 2 — count the fields. standard frame, fixed-form bits: SOF 1 + ID 11 + RTR 1 + IDE 1 + r0 1 + DLC 4 + CRC 15 + CRCdel 1 + ACK 1 + ACKdel 1 + EOF 7 + IFS 3 = 47 bits stuffed region (SOF through the end of the CRC sequence): 1 + 11 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 8n + 15 = 34 + 8n bits Step 3 — assemble. C(n) = 47 + 8n + ⌊(34 + 8n − 1)/4⌋ = 47 + 8n + ⌊(33 + 8n)/4⌋ and since 8n/4 = 2n is an integer, ⌊(33 + 8n)/4⌋ = ⌊33/4⌋ + 2n = 8 + 2n C(n) = 47 + 8n + 8 + 2n = 55 + 10n □ exact for all n Step 4 — extended format, identically. fixed: 1+11+1+1+18+1+2+4+15+1+1+1+7+3 = 67 bits stuffed region: 1+11+1+1+18+1+2+4+8n+15 = 54 + 8n s = ⌊(53 + 8n)/4⌋ = ⌊53/4⌋ + 2n = 13 + 2n C(n) = 67 + 8n + 13 + 2n = 80 + 10n □ exact for all n

Two consequences. First, an extended frame costs a flat 25 extra bit times regardless of payload — 50 µs at 500 kbit/s — so choosing 29-bit identifiers for a machine that needs 200 message types is a real and computable decision, not a preference. Second, the best case is the fixed count with zero stuff bits: 47 + 8n and 67 + 8n. At n = 8 that is 111 against 135 bits, so the same message varies by 21.6 % in duration according to its own contents. Any analysis that uses an average frame length is not a worst-case analysis at all.

D2 — THE PROPAGATION CONSTRAINT AND THE LENGTH×RATE PRODUCT CORE

Where the requirement comes from. During arbitration, a node transmitting recessive must be able to detect that a distant node is transmitting dominant, within the same bit. Nothing else in CAN requires this — and the ACK slot requires it a second time.

Step 1 — the round trip. node A at one end transmits at the start of bit k. → A's transceiver output delay → propagation along L metres of cable → B's transceiver input delay, B's controller reacts and drives dominant → propagation back along L metres → A's transceiver input delay and all of this must complete before A's SAMPLE POINT, which is at the end of Sync + Prop + PS1. Since the phase segments exist to absorb clock error and may be shortened by resynchronisation, the guarantee must be carried by Prop_Seg alone: Prop_Seg ≥ 2 × (t_cable + t_loop) with t_cable = L × 5 ns/m (twisted pair, v ≈ 0.66c → 200 m/µs) and t_loop = the transceiver loop delay, TXD pin to RXD pin. Step 2 — invert it. L_max = (Prop_Seg / 2 − t_loop) / (5 ns/m) Step 3 — bound Prop_Seg by the bit time. let Prop_Seg = f × NBT, and NBT[ns] = 1000 / B for B in Mbit/s: L_max = (500f/B − t_loop) / 5 = 100f/B − t_loop/5 → L × B ≤ 100f − (t_loop / 5) × B [m × Mbit/s] Step 4 — put numbers in. With t_loop = 150 ns and a practical f = 0.65: L × B ≤ 65 − 30B B = 1.000 → L × B ≤ 35 → L ≤ 35 m B = 0.500 → 50 → L ≤ 100 m B = 0.250 → 57.5 → L ≤ 230 m B = 0.125 → 61.25 → L ≤ 490 m

That is the published table, reconstructed from first principles. Every row of the length-versus-bit-rate table that appears in every CAN datasheet and every training course falls out of one inequality and two constants. And the derivation explains the folklore: the widely repeated “bit rate × length ≈ 50 m·Mbit/s” is exactly the value of 65 − 30B at B = 0.5, which is the bit rate most people have in mind when they say it.

Two honest caveats. The −30B term means the product is not constant — the transceiver delay costs proportionally more at high bit rates, which is why 1 Mbit/s is punished harder than the folklore suggests. And below about 50 kbit/s this derivation stops being the binding constraint: DC loop resistance and attenuation over a kilometre of thin pair collapse the dominant differential voltage long before propagation becomes a problem, which is why the published tables are more conservative than this arithmetic at the bottom end.

D3 — THE OSCILLATOR TOLERANCE CONDITIONS, AND THE TRILEMMA FRONTIER CORE

The situation. Two nodes' oscillators differ. Between resynchronisation opportunities, that difference accumulates as phase error. Two things can go wrong, and each gives one condition.

Condition 1 — the correction machinery must keep up. Over an interval of 10 nominal bit times, only one resynchronisation correction (of at most SJW quanta) is guaranteed to be available. Two nodes each deviating by df drift apart at a relative rate of 2·df: 2 × df × 10 × NBT ≤ SJW × t_q divide through by t_q, with NBT = N × t_q: df ≤ SJW / (2 × 10 × N) Condition 2 — the sample point must stay inside the bit. Over the worst-case interval between two usable edges, 13 nominal bit times, the accumulated phase error must be absorbable by the smaller phase segment. Phase_Seg2 of the final bit is already available to be shortened, so it is subtracted from the exposure: 2 × df × (13 × NBT − Phase_Seg2) ≤ min(P1, P2) × t_q df ≤ min(P1, P2) / (2 × (13N − P2)) both must hold; the budget is the smaller. The constants 10 and 13 are the Bosch specification's worst-case edge spacings — they encode the fact that bit stuffing guarantees an edge only every fifth bit, and that only recessive-to-dominant edges are usable.

Now the frontier. Fix N. The segments must satisfy Prop + P1 + P2 = N − 1, and SJW ≤ min(4, P1, P2). Take the symmetric design P1 = P2 = SJW = s (which is optimal: raising P1 above P2 helps neither condition, and raising SJW above P2 is illegal). Then condition 2 always binds, because 2(13N − s) = 26N − 2s > 20N for any sensible N. So:

df_max(s) = s / (2 × (13N − s)) and Prop = N − 1 − 2s L_max(s) = ((N − 1 − 2s) × t_q / 2 − t_loop) / 5 The frontier at 500 kbit/s, N = 20, t_q = 100 ns, t_loop = 150 ns: s = 1 Prop 17 L_max 140 m df_max 0.193 % sample point 95.0 % s = 2 Prop 15 L_max 120 m df_max 0.388 % sample point 90.0 % s = 3 Prop 13 L_max 100 m df_max 0.584 % sample point 85.0 % s = 4 Prop 11 L_max 80 m df_max 0.781 % sample point 80.0 % s = 5 Prop 9 L_max 60 m df_max 0.980 % sample point 75.0 % (SJW capped at 4, so condition 1 binds here: 4/400 = 1.00 %) Every 20 metres of cable costs about 0.20 percentage points of clock budget, and about 5 points of sample point. That exchange rate is the trilemma.

Three design rules fall straight out. The sample point is (N − P2)/N, so it is set by Phase_Seg2 alone and Prop-versus-P1 is a free trade — grow Prop by shrinking P1 first, at zero cost, until P1 reaches P2. Choose s from the worst oscillator you will ever accept on the segment, not from the one you have today. And N is a free parameter: a larger N (smaller prescaler) gives finer control over the sample point and lets you land closer to the frontier, at no cost but a slightly higher controller clock.

D4 — THE FAULT-CONFINEMENT FIXED POINT: ONE IN NINE, ONE IN TWO CORE

The dynamics. The counter rules are trivially simple, and the consequence is not.

Transmitter. Over n transmissions of which a fraction f fail: ΔTEC = n × [ 8f − 1×(1 − f) ] = n (9f − 1) ΔTEC > 0 ⇔ f > 1/9 = 11.1 % Receiver. +1 on a failed reception, −1 on a successful one: ΔREC = n × [ f − (1 − f) ] = n (2f − 1) ΔREC > 0 ⇔ f > 1/2 = 50 % The sensitivity ratio: (1/2) / (1/9) = 4.5× A transmitter is confined at a failure rate 4.5 times lower than a receiver. Since the transmitter is statistically the most likely cause of a failed frame, the protocol removes the probable culprit and leaves the probable witnesses running. That is fault confinement's entire theory of blame, and it is two arithmetic rules deep. Timing. From TEC₀ at transmission rate r (frames/s), for f > 1/9: t_passive = (128 − TEC₀) / [ r (9f − 1) ] t_busoff = (256 − TEC₀) / [ r (9f − 1) ] t_recover = 128 × 11 = 1,408 bit times (2.816 ms at 500 kbit/s) flap period ≈ t_busoff(from 0) + t_recover

Three consequences, and the third is the uncomfortable one.

1 · The threshold is sharp. At f = 12 % a node goes bus-off; at f = 10 % it never does. Two percentage points of failure rate separate “disappears every few seconds” from “runs forever”, and both are the same underlying defect.
2 · Below the threshold the node is invisible and expensive. A node failing 10 % of its transmissions stays error-active indefinitely, and every one of those failures costs the whole bus an error frame and a retransmission — degrading everybody's response time, permanently, with no alarm anywhere. The marginal node is worse than the dead one.
3 · Confinement cannot touch a stuck-dominant node. A transceiver whose output is stuck dominant never completes a frame transmission, so its TEC never increments, so it is never confined — while every other node counts up to bus-off around it. The mechanism's blind spot is precisely the fault that does the most damage.
