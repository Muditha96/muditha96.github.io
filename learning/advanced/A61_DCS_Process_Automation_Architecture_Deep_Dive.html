What a DCS Actually Is

Not “a big PLC”, and not defined by being distributed — everything is distributed now. It is defined by being integrated.

THE DEFINITION THAT SURVIVES SCRUTINY

A Distributed Control System is a control system whose processing is spread across many controllers but which is engineered, operated, secured and maintained as a single system with a single database and a single namespace.

The word that does the work is not distributed — a network of PLCs is distributed too. It is system. In a DCS, a tag defined once is automatically known to the controller, the operator graphics, the alarm system, the historian, the batch engine, the asset-management package and the reporting layer. In a PLC-plus-SCADA architecture, that same tag is defined in the PLC, again in the HMI, again in the historian and again in the report, by three different people, in three different tools, with three opportunities to disagree. Everything else — redundancy, function blocks, faceplates, alarming — can be bought for a PLC system. The single engineering database cannot.

The category was created in 1975, when Honeywell's TDC 2000 and Yokogawa's CENTUM appeared within weeks of each other and replaced the pneumatic and analogue-electronic panel board with microprocessor-based controllers sharing a data highway and a CRT console. Half a century later the physical form has changed beyond recognition and the architectural proposition has not: put the control close to the process, put the information in one place, and let one team engineer the whole thing.

The regions overlap and have been overlapping further every year. The choice is rarely about capability any more — it is about integration, lifecycle and who has to maintain it in fifteen years.

Quick check: A 3 000-I/O plant expansion is quoted two ways: a DCS extension, and a redundant PLC pair with a SCADA package at 60 % of the capital cost. The PLC option meets every functional line in the specification. What questions decide it, and which way would you lean?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The functional specification cannot decide this, and that is the point — if it could, the cheaper option would simply win. What separates them is everything the specification does not say: integration cost, lifecycle cost and organisational cost. Questions that decide it. (1) Is there an existing DCS on the site? If yes, the extension inherits the tag database, the operator's muscle memory, the alarm philosophy, the historian, the spares holding and the maintenance contract. The PLC option creates a second system: a second HMI convention, a second alarm list, a second backup regime, a second set of spares, a second thing to patch, and a boundary across which data must be mapped and kept in step forever. That boundary is where the 40 % saving quietly goes. (2) How many tags cross the boundary, and how often do they change? Every cross-boundary tag is engineered twice and maintained twice. Two hundred exchanged tags is an integration project in its own right; twenty is a non-issue. (3) What is the process? Analogue-rich continuous process with 400 loops, cascades and interlock matrices favours the DCS; a materials-handling or packaging expansion with fast discrete logic genuinely favours the PLC, and forcing it into the DCS is the mirror-image mistake. (4) Who operates it? One control room and one operator crew should see one system. If the operator must switch between two HMIs with different colour conventions and different alarm behaviour during an upset, you have bought a hazard for 40 % off (A53, ISA-101 — Page 7). (5) Who maintains it, and for how long? Compare the twenty-five-year picture: DCS support contract versus PLC/SCADA hardware refresh every 10–15 years plus SCADA licence and OS upgrades, plus the in-house effort to keep two systems patched and backed up. (6) Safety and regulatory: does anything in the expansion interact with a SIF, and can the PLC option's independence and audit trail satisfy the functional safety assessment (Page 11)? Which way I would lean. If there is an existing DCS and the expansion is process control on the same plant with a meaningful tag interface, I would extend the DCS — and I would say so in terms of total cost of ownership and operator safety, not preference, showing the integration hours and the twenty-five-year support comparison as numbers. If the expansion is a physically and functionally separable unit — a new utility area, an effluent plant, a packaging hall — with a small, stable interface and predominantly discrete logic, the PLC option is genuinely the better engineering, and insisting on the DCS is dogma. The general rule: choose the architecture on the boundary, not on the box. A clean, small, stable boundary makes a second system cheap; a large, chatty, changing boundary makes it ruinous.

WHAT ISA-95 ACTUALLY STANDARDISES

ISA-95 (internationally IEC 62264) is not a network standard and not an architecture mandate. It is a set of models and vocabulary for the boundary between business systems and manufacturing systems — so that when the ERP asks the plant for something, both sides mean the same thing by “production order”, “material lot” and “equipment”.

THE TWO WAYS THE MODEL IS MISUSED

Mistake 1: treating it as a network diagram

Purdue levels are functional. They do not require one physical network per level, and a modern plant will have edge devices publishing to Level 3 or the cloud without traversing five hops. The functional separation — who is allowed to command what, and on what timescale — remains valid; the implied five-tier network does not.

Mistake 2: treating it as a security architecture

“We are Purdue-compliant” is not a security statement. IEC 62443's zones and conduits are the security model; the DMZ at “Level 3.5” is a conduit, not an ISA-95 level. Segmentation must follow risk, not the diagram — and flat OT networks behind one firewall are common precisely because people believed the picture was the design (A54).

What is genuinely durable

The timescale separation. Control acts in milliseconds to seconds and must never wait on a system that thinks in shifts. Any architecture — edge, cloud, unified namespace, whatever — that lets a Level 3 or Level 4 outage stop Level 1 has violated the one rule that matters.

The modern shape

Many plants now run a Unified Namespace: an MQTT/OPC UA broker into which every system publishes and from which every system subscribes, replacing point-to-point integrations. It flattens the data topology while keeping the control hierarchy intact — and it is compatible with NOA (Page 14).

The rule that survives every architectural fashion Control flows DOWN and must never depend on anything above it. Data flows UP and may be as clever, as cloudy and as fragile as you like. Test: unplug Level 3. Does the plant keep running safely and controllably for as long as it takes to fix Level 3? If not, you have a control dependency masquerading as an information system, and it will take the plant down one day.
Quick check: A digital-transformation project proposes moving PID tuning parameters and setpoint schedules into a cloud application that writes them to the DCS over OPC UA. Assess the proposal against the hierarchy.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Separate the two halves of the proposal, because one is good and one is dangerous. Setpoint scheduling from a higher level is legitimate and is exactly what Levels 3 and 4 are for — a scheduler or optimiser computing targets and passing them down is the classic supervisory-control pattern (A58's four-layer hierarchy: RTO → MPC → regulatory). But it must obey three rules. (1) The write is a request, not a command. The DCS validates every incoming setpoint against engineering limits, rate-of-change limits and the current operating mode, and rejects anything outside them — the receiving controller owns the decision, not the sender. (2) Loss of the source is a non-event. If the cloud application stops, the plant continues on its last valid targets, with an alarm and a defined fallback after a timeout. Any design in which stale or absent cloud data degrades control has inverted the hierarchy. (3) The operator can take it off, instantly and obviously, with a single visible control and a clear indication of who is currently setting the target. Writing tuning parameters from the cloud is a different matter and I would refuse it as proposed. Tuning constants are not operational data; they are configuration. Changing them changes the closed-loop dynamics and therefore the stability margin (A57) — a bad gain does not produce a bad number, it produces an oscillating plant, and potentially an unstable one. Configuration changes belong under management of change: reviewed, tested, versioned, audited, and applied by someone accountable. There are further problems: the audit trail for a regulated plant must show who changed a tuning constant and why; an adaptive algorithm that retunes automatically on data it does not understand will chase a stiction limit cycle (A59) and make things worse; and the same write path is an attack surface into Level 1 (A54) — a compromised cloud account that can write controller gains can destabilise a plant without ever tripping an alarm. What I would propose instead. Let the cloud application do the analysis and recommend: compute the loop-performance indices, identify the poorly tuned loops, propose gains with the evidence, and present them to the control engineer through the normal MOC workflow. That captures essentially all of the value — the hard part is knowing which loops need attention and what to do, not typing the numbers — while keeping the write barrier intact. If the organisation genuinely wants closed-loop adaptive tuning, it belongs inside the Level 2 platform where the vendor has designed the interlocks for it, not across a Level 4 boundary. The general test again: unplug it. If the plant keeps running safely and controllably, it is an information system. If it does not, it is a control system, and it must be engineered, secured and governed like one.

Controllers & I/O Architecture

Where the control actually executes, and the cabinet architecture that decides how much your project changes cost.

THE CONTROLLER

A DCS controller is a deterministic, redundant computer whose job is to execute a set of control modules (loops, sequences, interlock blocks, device objects) at defined periods, service its I/O, exchange data with peer controllers, and keep doing so for twenty years without being restarted. I/O ARCHITECTURE — THE MARSHALLING REVOLUTION
Electronic marshalling (Emerson CHARMs, Honeywell Universal I/O, ABB Select I/O, Yokogawa N-IO and equivalents) removes the cross-wiring cabinet and decouples the physical design from the I/O assignment.

This looks like a hardware detail and is actually a project-schedule instrument. In a traditional design, the I/O list must be frozen before the marshalling cabinets can be built, which is typically 12–18 months before start-up — and the I/O list is precisely the document that keeps changing as the process design matures. Every late change then costs re-termination, drawing revisions and loop re-testing. With single-channel characterisation, the cabinet is built with generic modules and the signal type becomes a configuration item, so a change costs a module swap and a database edit. On a large project this reliably removes weeks from the critical path and a substantial share of the change orders.

THE REST OF THE I/O DECISIONS

Central vs remote I/O

Remote I/O cuts field cabling dramatically (one fibre pair instead of hundreds of cores) but puts electronics in the field: temperature, vibration, humidity, dust, power quality and physical security all become design problems, and maintenance access is worse. Justify by cable-run length and count, not by fashion.

Intrinsic safety & isolation

Hazardous-area signals need IS barriers or galvanic isolators, which consume panel space, budget and voltage headroom. Decide the protection concept (Ex i, Ex d, Ex e, purge) with the electrical team before the I/O architecture — retrofitting IS into a finished cabinet design is painful.

Signal types & the process image

4–20 mA with HART remains the workhorse; NAMUR NE 43 out-of-range detection (< 3.6 mA / > 21 mA) is what makes a broken wire distinguishable from a real zero (A50). Insist that every AI block implements it — A60's “confirm, never assume” applied to analogue signals.

Spare capacity

Specify spares in three currencies: free channels (typically 20 %), free space in cabinets, and free controller capacity. Projects routinely buy the first and forget the other two, then discover that the spare channels belong to a controller already at 75 % load.

Quick check: A controller is at 68 % CPU load. The project wants to add 60 monitoring calculations, and the obvious place is the existing 100 ms task. Assess, and give three ways to make room.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Refuse the obvious placement. At 0.25 ms per execution, 60 modules at 100 ms add 60 × 0.25/100 = 15 percentage points of load, taking the controller from 68 % to 83 %. That is past every sensible design limit, and the reason is not tidiness: at 83 % steady state there is no margin for an alarm flood, no margin for an online download, and — critically — no margin for a redundancy failover, during which a single CPU must carry the entire load while also re-establishing state. A controller that is comfortable on a quiet Tuesday and overruns during an upset has its capacity exactly backwards, because an upset is when the control must work. Three ways to make room, best first. (1) Put them in the task their physics requires. Monitoring calculations — efficiencies, mass balances, rolling averages, run-hour counters — have no closed-loop deadline whatsoever. At 2 s they cost 60 × 0.25/2000 = 0.75 percentage points, twenty times less, and nobody can tell the difference. This alone solves the problem, and the general lesson is the one from the demo: execution period dominates loading, so the first question about any new module is “how fast does the process need this?”, never “what task is convenient?”. (2) Audit the existing fast task. On a controller that has grown for years, it is normal to find a third of the 100 ms modules have no business being there — loops on slow thermal processes (A57 says T ≤ 0.1τ, so a 20-minute time constant does not need 100 ms), monitoring blocks inherited from a copy-paste typical, and calculations someone accelerated during commissioning to chase a problem that turned out to be elsewhere. Re-basing those to 500 ms or 1 s typically recovers 10–20 points and improves nothing else's behaviour. (3) Move the calculations off the controller entirely. Monitoring maths is Level 2/3 work: put it in the historian's calculation engine, the information server or an edge application that subscribes to the data. This is the architecturally correct answer — the controller's job is control, and every non-control computation on it is capacity you cannot use for control later. It also removes them from the online-change and MOC burden of the control database. If none of that is enough, the honest answer is a second controller and a re-partition of the plant — a capital decision, taken deliberately, with the peer-to-peer traffic across the new boundary designed rather than discovered. What I would not do is accept 83 % on the grounds that the vendor's stated limit is 90 %. The vendor's limit is where the controller stops working; the design limit is where it keeps working through everything the plant will throw at it over twenty years.

The mistake that produces most control-network incidents is applying IT practice to the middle tier. An IT network is designed for average throughput and tolerates a few seconds of reconvergence after a topology change; a control network must deliver a bounded worst-case latency and survive a link failure in milliseconds, because a controller that loses its remote I/O for 30 seconds while spanning tree reconverges has lost the plant. Hence the control tier's characteristic features: parallel media redundancy rather than reconfiguring protocols, fixed topology, no DHCP, no unmanaged switches, no “just plug the laptop in”, and change control on the switch configuration.

Ethernet-APL is the change worth understanding. The reason process instrumentation stayed on 31.25 kbit/s for thirty years was not conservatism — it was physics and law: the field device must be powered over the same two wires, over a kilometre, into a hazardous area, within intrinsic-safety energy limits. APL solves it with 10BASE-T1L single-pair Ethernet plus a defined power scheme and the 2-WISE intrinsic-safety concept, giving 10 Mbit/s, two wires, kilometre distances and Ex ia simultaneously. The consequence is architectural, not just faster: a transmitter can now expose an OPC UA information model directly, so the diagnostic and configuration data that HART has carried unused for decades becomes ordinarily accessible — which is precisely the substrate NAMUR Open Architecture assumes (Page 14).

HART: THE DATA YOU ALREADY OWN

Almost every 4–20 mA transmitter installed in the last twenty-five years speaks HART, and in most plants nothing listens. The digital layer carries the device tag, the primary variable in engineering units, up to three secondary variables, the full diagnostic status, the calibration record and the configuration — all riding on the same two wires as the analogue signal, without disturbing it.

  • 1Why it is unused: the classic AI card reads only the analogue value. Reaching the digital layer historically needed a HART multiplexer wired across the loops, a separate cabinet, a separate software package and a separate maintenance discipline — so it was value-engineered out of the project.
  • 2What changed: modern I/O (particularly electronic marshalling modules) is HART-capable per channel as standard, and HART-IP carries the same data over Ethernet. The barrier is now configuration effort, not capital.
  • 3What it buys: device health before failure, valve travel and stiction diagnostics (A59), verification of calibration without going to the field, correct multivariable data from a single transmitter, and the raw material for the asset-management layer of Page 12.
  • 4The catch: HART is slow — a few updates per second at best — so it is a diagnostic and configuration channel, not a control channel. Control stays on the 4–20 mA analogue value, which is exactly why the arrangement is so robust: the digital layer can fail entirely without affecting the loop.

If you inherit a plant with HART-capable I/O and no asset-management system, this is usually the highest return-on-effort project available to a controls engineer: the instruments are already there, the wiring is already there, the data is already being generated, and nobody is reading it.

Quick check: An engineer proposes putting the control network, the historian traffic and the CCTV system on one physical network to save cost, arguing that modern switches have ample bandwidth. Give the technical response.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Bandwidth is not the argument, and conceding the bandwidth point costs nothing. The engineer is right that a modern switch has ample throughput; the objection is about determinism, failure domains, security and governance — four independent reasons, any one of which is sufficient. (1) Determinism and burst behaviour. Control traffic is small, frequent and latency-critical; CCTV is large, bursty and latency-tolerant. A single camera stream restarting, a recorder pulling an archive, or a multicast storm can fill queues and delay control frames by tens of milliseconds — and the failure is intermittent and load-dependent, which is the hardest kind to diagnose. Quality-of-service tagging helps and is not a substitute: QoS manages congestion, it does not eliminate the shared failure domain, and it depends on every device, every switch and every future addition being configured correctly forever. If genuine convergence is wanted, the engineered answer is TSN (A37) with scheduled traffic classes — not ordinary switching plus optimism. (2) Failure and change domains. On a shared network, a CCTV firmware upgrade, a mis-patched cable, a duplicate IP address, a misconfigured VLAN or a failed camera injecting broadcast traffic is now an event that can affect control. Worse, the maintenance domains merge: the security contractor who reboots a switch at 2 a.m. to fix a camera is now touching the plant's control network. Separation exists so that ordinary maintenance on the unimportant system cannot endanger the important one. (3) Security. CCTV systems are among the most frequently compromised devices on any site — default credentials, unpatched embedded Linux, vendor cloud connections, and often a remote-access arrangement nobody in engineering knows about. Putting them in the same broadcast domain as controllers means an attacker who owns a camera has L2 adjacency to the control system. Under IEC 62443 (A54) these belong in different zones with a controlled conduit between them; a shared flat network is the textbook example of what the standard exists to prevent. (4) Governance and lifecycle. The control network is under change control with a twenty-year life; CCTV is refreshed on a different cycle by a different contractor under a different contract. Merging them means every camera change is a control-system change or, more realistically, that control-system change control quietly stops being enforced. What I would offer instead. Shared physical infrastructure where it genuinely saves money — the same cable trays, the same fibre bundles, the same cabinets — with separate switches for the control network, and the historian traffic crossing to Level 3 through a defined boundary rather than sharing the control tier. If the driver is really cost, quantify it: the saving is a few switches; the exposure is the plant. And if convergence onto one physical network is a strategic goal, do it properly with TSN and a documented zone-and-conduit design, which is a project, not a cost saving.

The uncomfortable truths about redundancy 1. Redundancy adds failure modes. Synchronisation links, arbitration logic and failover software are new things that can fail — and a failover that goes wrong is worse than never having failed over. 2. The commonest real cause of DCS downtime is not hardware at all.
Quick check: A vendor quotes “99.9999 % availability” for their redundant controller pair. What does that claim actually mean, what does it exclude, and what would you ask for instead?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Six nines is 31.5 seconds of downtime per year. Taken literally it is a claim that the controller pair will be unavailable for about half a minute across an entire year, and it is almost certainly a hardware random-failure figure computed for the CPU pair alone, with β = 0, an optimistic MTTR, and none of the things that actually stop plants. What it excludes — and this is the substance of the answer. (1) Common cause. With any realistic β, unavailability is dominated by the βU term, not U². A shared power feed, a shared cabinet, a shared cooling failure, a shared firmware defect present in both CPUs, or a shared lightning surge takes both channels regardless of their individual MTBF. Ask what β was assumed; if the answer is “none”, the number is arithmetic rather than engineering. (2) Everything outside the CPU pair. The loop the operator cares about runs through a field transmitter, wiring, an IS barrier, an I/O module, the controller, the network, the console, and a final element. Series elements multiply: the system availability is the product, and it is dominated by whichever term is worst — usually the field device or the valve (A59), not the controller. Six nines on one link of a chain whose weakest link is three nines gives you three nines. (3) Software, configuration and human error, which on real plants cause far more DCS-attributable downtime than hardware. Both redundant CPUs execute the same erroneous logic identically and reliably. (4) The failover event itself, and whether it is genuinely bumpless for outputs and for sequence state. (5) Systematic failures and maintenance — patching, upgrades, restarts — which are usually excluded from the calculation and are often the largest planned contributor. What I would ask for instead. (a) The assumptions behind the number: which items are in the boundary, what β, what MTTR, what diagnostic coverage, and whether it is availability of the controller or of the control function. (b) A system-level availability model that includes power, network, I/O, field device and final element, so the weakest link is visible — that model is the useful deliverable and the six-nines figure is not. (c) Field evidence: installed-base MTBF data, and the vendor's record of firmware defects that affected both channels of a redundant pair — those exist for every vendor and the honest ones will discuss them. (d) Failover behaviour on test: demonstrated at FAT, with the outputs trended, the sequence state checked and the time measured — and a commitment to repeat it periodically in service, because an untested standby has unknown availability. (e) MTTR commitments — spares holding, response time, and whether a local engineer can restore without waiting for an overseas visit. On a plant in Sri Lanka, an MTTR of 8 hours versus 5 days changes the availability arithmetic by more than any redundancy topology the vendor can sell, and it is the number to negotiate hardest.

The Control Database & Engineering

The single feature that justifies the price of a DCS — and the discipline that decides whether you get the benefit.

CLASS-BASED ENGINEERING

In a class-based system you define a device type once — its logic, its interlocks, its faceplate, its alarms, its historisation, its simulation mode — and then create hundreds of instances that differ only in parameters. Change the class, and every instance changes.

This is the economics of the DCS in one picture. Everything else can be bought elsewhere; this cannot be retrofitted onto a system that was not designed for it.

The corollary is a project discipline, not a software feature: the value only appears if the classes are designed before the instances are built. A project that starts configuring loops on day one and abstracts “later” ends up with three hundred near-identical bespoke modules and none of the benefit — while still having paid for the system that could have delivered it. The first four to six weeks of a DCS project should produce a small, well-argued library, and almost no instances.

THE LIBRARY, IN THREE LAYERS

Vendor library

Tested, supported, documented, and migrated for you at the next release. Never modify it. A forked vendor block becomes yours forever and blocks upgrades.

Corporate / site library

Your standard motor, valve, loop, phase and interlock objects, built on top of vendor blocks. Owned by a named engineer, versioned, with a change process. This is the asset a good organisation accumulates.

Project library

The handful of genuinely project-specific objects. Should be small. If it is large, either the site library is inadequate or someone is reinventing it.

Library rules that survive contact with a project 1. Every object has an OWNER, a VERSION and a change record. 2. A change to a library object is a change to every plant using it: it goes through management of change, not through a quick edit. 3. Version the library WITH the project. "Which version of the valve block is running in Area 3?" must have an answer. 4. Every device class implements a Simulate mode from the start (A60 Page 9) — this is what makes desk testing and operator training possible before there is any plant. 5. Every device class exposes the SAME status interface: healthy, fault, mode, simulated, bypassed. The graphics and the diagnostics then work everywhere, automatically. 6. Resist "just this once" instance-level edits. Each one is a future surprise when the class is next updated and does not propagate.
BULK ENGINEERING & THE INSTRUMENT INDEX

A 20 000-point project cannot be typed in. The professional workflow generates configuration from the engineering data that already exists:

Instrument index / I/O list (the master spreadsheet or database) ↓ Bulk-build tool: for each row, instantiate the right class, set tag, description, range, units, alarm limits, I/O address, area assignment, historisation, security group ↓ Controller configuration + faceplates + alarm database + history + loop drawings + I/O termination schedules, all consistent What this buys: 3000 loops built in hours, not months; zero transcription errors; and — the real prize — the ability to REBUILD when the index changes, instead of patching by hand. What it demands: the index must be the single source of truth and must be kept that way. The failure mode is a project where the index and the DCS diverge after month three, and nobody dares regenerate anything.

Naming deserves its own decision, made once, at the start. Whether you use ISA-5.1 instrument tags, a KKS-style plant breakdown (common in power), or a corporate scheme, the requirements are the same: unique, systematic, sortable, area-decodable, and identical across the P&ID, the instrument index, the DCS, the historian, the maintenance system and the drawings. Every plant where the DCS tag differs from the maintenance-system tag pays for that decision for the rest of its life, in mapping tables and in misdiagnoses.

ONLINE CHANGE, AUDIT & CONFIGURATION MANAGEMENT
  • 1Online change is a feature, not a licence. A DCS is designed to be modified while running because a refinery cannot be stopped to add an alarm. That capability must be governed by management of change, not by whoever has the engineering station open.
  • 2The audit trail is a deliverable. Who changed what, when, from which workstation, with what previous value, and against which change record. In regulated industries this is mandatory; everywhere else it is what lets you answer “what changed before it started misbehaving?” — the single most useful question in troubleshooting.
  • 3Back up the database, and prove the restore. An untested backup is a belief, not a control. Restore to spare hardware annually and time it — that time is your MTTR for the worst case (Page 5).
  • 4Version-control what can be version-controlled. Exported configuration, graphics sources, library objects and documentation belong in a repository with meaningful history, not in a folder called Backup_Final_2.
  • 5Keep the as-built current. The gap between what the drawings say and what the system does opens on the first day of commissioning and never closes by itself. Budget for as-built updating explicitly, or accept that the next project starts with a survey.
Quick check: Six months after start-up, an engineer finds that 40 of 287 motor instances have been individually edited during commissioning — different timeout values, some with extra interlocks, two with the alarm disabled. What has been lost, and how would you recover?
Tap to reveal ↓A: What has been lost is the class itself. A class only means something if every instance genuinely conforms to it; once 14 % of instances differ in undocumented ways, the class is no longer a description of the plant but an aspiration. Concretely, four things are gone. (1) Safe propagation — a future class change can no longer be applied with confidence, because it may overwrite a deliberate local fix or, worse, may not overwrite it and leave the instance silently divergent. Most organisations respond by freezing the class permanently, which forfeits the main benefit of the system. (2) Predictability for operators and maintainers — “motors behave like this” is no longer true, so every diagnosis becomes a per-instance investigation. (3) Testability — testing the class no longer tests the plant. (4) Safety assurance in the two cases with the alarm disabled: an alarm suppressed at instance level, outside any bypass mechanism (Page 8, A53), is an undocumented, unauthorised, permanent bypass — and nobody knows it exists. Recovery, in order. Step 1: measure the divergence before changing anything. Export all 287 instances and diff them against the class, producing a table of every difference. This takes an afternoon with a script and is non-negotiable — you cannot fix what you have not enumerated, and the table is also the evidence for the management conversation that follows. Step 2: triage each difference into one of three buckets. (a) Legitimate and general — a start-fail timeout that was genuinely too short for every large motor: this belongs in the class, perhaps as a parameter rather than a constant, and the class should be updated. (b) Legitimate and specific — an extra interlock that only that machine needs: this belongs in a properly designed extension mechanism (a class parameter, an optional interlock input, or a documented derived class), not in an ad-hoc edit. (c) Not legitimate — the disabled alarms and any commissioning workaround nobody remembers: revert, after confirming with the process owner. Step 3: fix the class first, then re-conform the instances. Add the parameters and optional inputs that the legitimate cases needed, so that after re-conformance every instance is once again a pure instance of the class with its differences expressed as parameter values — which are visible, exportable and reviewable. Step 4: deal with the two disabled alarms as a safety matter, not a housekeeping one: find out why, assess whether the alarm is correctly configured (a nuisance alarm disabled in desperation is an alarm-rationalisation problem, A53), and either re-enable it or take it out through the proper rationalisation process. Step 5: close the hole that allowed it. Instance-level editing should require a privilege ordinary commissioning accounts do not have; the audit trail should be reviewed weekly during commissioning, when this always happens; and the conformance script should run as a scheduled check that reports divergence automatically. The wider lesson: commissioning is exactly when engineering discipline is under the most pressure and when the most damage is done to a system's long-term maintainability. Plan for it — give the commissioning team a fast, legitimate route to make the changes they will need (a parameter, a review, a same-day class update), because if the legitimate route is slow they will use the illegitimate one, and they will be right to.

ISA-101 Consoles & Displays

The part of the DCS that a human actually uses — and the part most often designed by whoever had time.

ISA-101: A LIFECYCLE, NOT A COLOUR CHART

ANSI/ISA-101.01 is a lifecycle standard for the human–machine interface, applicable to continuous, batch and discrete industries. Its central claim is that HMI quality is a process outcome: you get good displays by having a philosophy, a style guide and a toolkit before anyone draws a screen, and by maintaining them afterwards — not by hiring a talented graphic designer for the project.

The ISA-101 lifecycle HMI PHILOSOPHY why displays exist, what the operator's job is, colour and alarm conventions, hierarchy definition, performance targets. A document, approved, ~20 pages. ↓ STYLE GUIDE the concrete rules: exact colours, fonts, sizes, symbol shapes, layout grid, navigation, naming. ↓ TOOLKIT the reusable graphic objects that ENFORCE the style guide. If the toolkit is good, conformance is free. ↓ DESIGN → BUILD → OPERATE → MAINTAIN → (feed back to philosophy) The failure mode this prevents: 400 displays drawn by 6 people over 18 months, each with different colours, different navigation and different ideas about what red means. That is not a cosmetic problem — it is a response-time problem during an upset, when the operator's mental model must survive contact with an unfamiliar screen.
HIGH-PERFORMANCE GRAPHICS
The design principle: the display must be boring when the plant is normal. Colour, motion and contrast are a scarce resource reserved for things that need attention.

Three rules follow directly and are worth stating in your style guide. Never use colour alone to convey information — always pair it with shape, position, text or a border, because roughly 8 % of men have some degree of colour-vision deficiency, and because a monochrome print or a degraded monitor must still be readable. Show deviation, not just value — a number tells you 118 °C; an analogue bar with the setpoint and limits marked tells you instantly that it is high and rising, which is what the operator actually needs. And embed trends: level-2 comprehension and level-3 projection in Endsley's model of situation awareness both require history, and an operator who must open a separate trend display to see whether something is rising has been given the data but not the information.

OPERATOR SPAN OF CONTROL

The hardest question in console design is how much plant can one operator hold, and it has no single number — but it has drivers you can assess. An operator's capacity is consumed by alarm rate (A53: the ISA-18.2 targets of about one alarm per ten minutes in steady operation, and no more than ten per ten minutes in an upset), manual interventions per hour, number of displays needed to cover the area, process dynamics (a fast unit demands continuous attention; a slow one tolerates absence) and abnormal-situation frequency.

The failure mode is silent: an over-loaded console works perfectly during normal operation, which is 99 % of the time, and fails during the upset — when several units go abnormal at once and one person must diagnose, prioritise and act across all of them. Every serious process incident investigation of the last thirty years contains some version of this sentence. If you are consolidating control rooms — a common cost-saving project — the analysis must be done on the upset case, not the average, and it should be documented.

Quick check: A plant is upgrading its DCS graphics. The operators, who have used the colourful mimics for fifteen years, strongly resist grey high-performance displays: “we cannot see anything”. How do you handle this without either overriding them or abandoning the improvement?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Take the objection seriously, because it is partly correct. Experienced operators have built a fifteen-year mental model in which colour encodes identity and location — the blue thing on the left is the heat exchanger — and a grey display genuinely destroys that, replacing it with a scheme where colour encodes state. In the first weeks their performance really is worse, and dismissing that as resistance to change is both wrong and the fastest way to have the new graphics abandoned after go-live. It also matters that some of what they miss is real: badly executed high-performance displays are flat, low-contrast and hard to read, particularly for older eyes and under control-room lighting. What I would do. (1) Involve them in the design, not the acceptance. The ISA-101 lifecycle exists partly for this: the philosophy and style guide should be written with operator representatives in the room, and the toolkit reviewed by the people who will use it. Operators who helped choose the conventions defend them; operators handed a finished product resist them. (2) Test the claim with a measurable comparison, not a debate. Build two versions of one representative display, run realistic scenarios on a simulator or an offline station, and measure detection time and diagnosis accuracy for a set of seeded abnormal conditions. In almost every published and unpublished trial the high-performance version wins substantially on abnormal detection while being slightly slower on familiar navigation — and having the plant's own operators produce that result on their own process settles the argument in a way that no reference to a standard can. (3) Fix the legitimate part of the complaint. Check contrast ratios properly rather than assuming grey is grey; ensure text is large and high-contrast; make equipment identity carry through shape, layout and labelling rather than colour, since consistent spatial layout is what really preserves recognition; and keep the layout topologically similar to the old mimic where possible, so the muscle memory of “the reactor is top-left” survives. Given a keratoconus or ageing-eyes population, the style guide should specify minimum character heights and contrast explicitly — and that is an improvement the operators will notice and value. (4) Phase it. Convert one unit first, keep the old displays available in parallel for a defined period, train on the simulator before go-live, and let the operators who become fluent teach the others — peer advocacy beats management mandate. (5) Keep a small number of deliberate exceptions where a colour convention is genuinely load-bearing and safety-relevant — running/stopped states, for instance — and document them in the style guide rather than letting exceptions accumulate informally. (6) Measure afterwards: alarm response times, time to diagnose seeded faults, and operator survey, at go-live and again at three months. If the numbers do not improve, that is information, not a defeat. The general principle: an HMI change is an organisational change wearing technical clothing. The technical part is a few weeks; the acceptance is the project.

Why time synchronisation is an alarm-system requirement An event's usefulness depends on knowing what happened FIRST. If two devices' clocks differ by more than the interval between the events, the sequence in the alarm list is fiction — and the operator's and the investigator's causal reasoning is built on it. One unsynchronised subsystem makes every cross-system investigation an argument about clocks.

This is the single most common architectural regret after a serious incident. The investigation asks “did the pump trip before or after the level alarm?”, and the answer is “the pump is on the package PLC whose clock was never synchronised, so we do not know”. The fix costs almost nothing at design time and cannot be applied retrospectively to an event that has already happened.

SURVIVING A FLOOD — THE ARCHITECTURAL PROVISIONS
  • 1Event buffering at every hop. During a trip, thousands of events are generated in seconds. Controllers, servers and the historian each need a buffer deep enough to hold the burst and forward it in order without discarding. Ask the vendor for the buffer depth and the behaviour on overflow — “oldest discarded” means you lose the first-out, which is the one event that mattered.
  • 2First-out capture in the controller. The first cause of a trip must be latched in hardware or in the controller's fast task, not inferred later from time stamps. This is the same principle as A60's sequence diagnostics, and it converts a 400-alarm list into a one-line answer.
  • 3Chatter suppression at the block. Deadband and on-delay belong in the alarm block in the controller, configured from the class, so they apply uniformly. A single chattering transmitter can generate thousands of events an hour and consume the buffer that a real flood will need.
  • 4State-based (designed) suppression. Alarms that are meaningless in a given plant state — low-flow alarms on a stopped pump, every alarm on a unit in planned shutdown — should be suppressed by design, driven by the plant state, documented and rationalised. This is the single most effective flood-reduction measure and it is an engineering deliverable, not an operator action.
  • 5Shelving with expiry, audit and visibility. The operator-facing mechanism (A53) must be time-limited, logged, and shown on a standing indicator — the same governance as an interlock bypass (A60 Page 11).
  • 6Consistent state across consoles. An alarm acknowledged on console 1 must be acknowledged on console 3, immediately. Alarm state is system state, not display state — another reason it belongs below the HMI layer.
What to measure (ISA-18.2, and A53 for the full treatment) Average alarm rate per operator target ≈ 1 per 10 min (≈ 150/day) Peak rate ≤ 10 per 10 min Standing / stale alarms < 5 at any time Alarms in flood condition > 10 per 10 min = flood Top 10 "bad actors" typically 60-80 % of all events Priority distribution roughly 80 % low / 15 % med / 5 % high The 80/20 that makes this tractable: in almost every plant, fixing the top ten bad actors removes the majority of the alarm load. Start there, not with a full rationalisation of 4000 alarms.
Quick check: After a unit trip, the alarm list shows 340 alarms in 90 seconds and the operators cannot say what caused it. The DCS vendor proposes a “smart alarm suppression” add-on. What would you do first, and what does the architecture need?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Do not buy anything yet. A 340-alarm flood in 90 seconds is a symptom with several possible causes, and an inference layer bolted on top will hide the symptom while leaving all of them in place. First, get the data right — and check whether you can trust it. Verify time synchronisation across every device that contributed events: controllers, I/O, the package PLCs, the SIS, the drives, the electrical protection. If clocks differ by more than a second, the order in that list is fiction and no analysis of it is valid. Fixing that is the precondition for everything else, and on many plants it is the actual root cause of “we cannot tell what happened first”. Then analyse the flood itself, which takes an afternoon with the event log: how many distinct tags, how many are repeats of the same tag (chatter), how many are consequential (a low-flow alarm on a pump that was correctly stopped by the trip), and how many are genuinely independent causes. In a typical flood of this size the breakdown is roughly: a handful of real causes, a large majority of predictable consequences, and a chunk of chatter from two or three bad actors. That breakdown tells you which of the following four fixes you need, and in what order. (1) First-out capture — if the trip logic does not latch its first cause in the controller, add it. This alone converts “340 alarms and no answer” into “the answer, plus 339 alarms”, and it is cheap, deterministic and needs no new product. (2) State-based suppression — the consequential alarms should be suppressed by design when the unit state makes them meaningless. This is rationalisation work (A53), it is documented and auditable, and it typically removes the large majority of the flood. Crucially it is designed suppression with a defined basis, not an algorithm guessing at run time. (3) Chatter suppression and bad-actor fixes — deadband and on-delay in the alarm block, and repair of the two or three instruments generating repeat events. (4) Rationalise the priorities — if 200 of the 340 are high priority, the priority scheme is not doing its job of telling the operator what to look at. What the architecture needs to support all of this: alarms detected in the controller (not the HMI); event time-stamping at source with adequate accuracy, and SOE-class stamping on the trip-critical inputs; event buffers deep enough that a 340-event burst is queued rather than dropped, with documented overflow behaviour; a plant-state signal available to the alarm blocks so state-based suppression can be driven from it; suppression and shelving that are logged, visible and time-limited; and an alarm historian that can be queried and reported against the ISA-18.2 metrics, because you cannot manage what you do not measure. Where the vendor's product might legitimately fit: after those four are done, a dynamic-suppression or alarm-analysis tool can help with genuinely complex, state-dependent relationships and with ongoing performance reporting. Bought before them, it is an expensive way to avoid rationalisation — and it introduces a new risk, because an algorithm that suppresses an alarm you needed is much harder to defend after an incident than a documented, rationalised design decision.

Historians & the Data Layer

The plant's memory. Everything above Level 2 — every KPI, every investigation, every model — is built on what this layer kept.

WHAT A PROCESS HISTORIAN IS

A time-series database optimised for one shape of data — (tag, timestamp, value, quality) — written continuously by thousands of sources and read by queries that ask for a time range rather than a key.

A relational database can store that shape and will fall over at industrial scale, because the workload is pathological for a general-purpose engine: hundreds of thousands of small writes per second, almost no updates, almost no deletes, and reads that want a whole day of one tag at a time. Process historians win by exploiting the structure — timestamps are monotonic, values change slowly, and consecutive values are highly correlated — achieving compression ratios that a general database cannot approach, and query performance measured in tags-per-second rather than rows.

The four fields, and why "quality" is not optional TAG which measurement e.g. TI-101.PV TIMESTAMP when it was TRUE, not when it was written or received VALUE in engineering units QUALITY good / bad / uncertain / substituted / manual / out-of-service / comms-fail Without quality, a historian cannot distinguish "the temperature was 0 °C" from "the transmitter was dead". Every KPI, model, report and regression built on that history then silently averages in zeros. Every consumer of historical data must filter on quality — and almost none of the home-made ones do.
COLLECTION & COMPRESSION
Swinging-door compression stores a point only when a straight line from the last stored point can no longer represent every intervening sample within the deviation limit. It is lossy but bounded — the error is never larger than the setting.
The two-stage filter, in order 1. Exception / deadband (at the collector, often in the controller) Send a value only if it differs from the last SENT value by more than the exception deviation. Removes noise-level chatter and reduces network traffic. Typically 0.1–0.5 % of span. 2. Compression (swinging door, at the archive) Store a value only if the straight line from the last STORED point would misrepresent some intervening value by more than the compression deviation. Typically 2–3 × the exception deviation. Rules that keep you out of trouble • compression deviation > exception deviation, always • both must be SMALLER than the smallest change you will ever need to see in an investigation — err generous; disk is cheap and a lost transient is gone forever • set them PER TAG from the instrument's accuracy and the process dynamics, never one global default • NEVER compress: SOE, alarms, batch-critical data, custody transfer, regulatory data, or anything feeding a model • a maximum-time rule ("store at least every 10 min regardless") guarantees you can tell "unchanged" from "collector was dead"

THE REST OF THE DATA ARCHITECTURE

Store-and-forward

The collector buffers locally when the historian or network is unavailable and backfills in order when it returns. Without it, every network blip is a permanent hole. Check the buffer depth in hours, not megabytes, and test it by pulling the cable.

Context, not just numbers

A value is far more useful attached to an asset model, a batch ID, a product grade, a campaign and an equipment state. Contextualised history is what makes “compare this batch with the last twenty good ones” a query rather than a project. ISA-95's equipment hierarchy (Page 2) is the natural backbone.

Aggregation & tiering

Keep full resolution online for months, hourly/daily aggregates for years, and archive the rest. Define the tiering deliberately; the default is usually “keep everything until the disk fills, then panic”.

Onward to MES / cloud / UNS

The historian is normally the source for Level 3 and above. Publish through a defined interface — OPC UA, an MQTT unified namespace, or a documented API — and cross the DMZ in one place (A54). Do not let six applications each poll the controllers directly.

Retention & regulation

Pharmaceutical batch records, emissions data and custody transfer have legally mandated retention periods and integrity requirements (ALCOA+ in GxP). Establish these before choosing compression, not after.

The security dimension

The historian usually sits astride the OT/IT boundary and is therefore a favourite pivot point. It should be in the DMZ, patched, with read-only access upward and no path back into control (A54).

Quick check: A data-science team asks for “all the historian data, uncompressed, in the cloud” to build predictive models. What is right about the request, what is wrong, and what would you propose?
Tap to reveal ↓A: What is right: the instinct that compression destroys information models need is correct and under-appreciated. Swinging-door compression is tuned for human trend viewing, where a bounded reconstruction error is invisible; a model learning from high-frequency structure — vibration signatures, valve stiction limit cycles (A59), fast transients, spectral content — is learning from precisely the wiggles that compression discards. If the target application needs sub-deadband detail, the historian's archive is the wrong source and always will be. Their instinct about context is usually right too: models need batch IDs, product grades, equipment states and maintenance events, not bare time series. What is wrong: four things. (1) “Uncompressed” is not retrievable retrospectively. The data was compressed at write time; the discarded samples do not exist anywhere. Asking for uncompressed history is asking for something that was never stored. Going forward you can reduce compression on selected tags — but that is a change, with a cost, taking effect from now. (2) “All the data” is almost never the requirement. Twenty thousand tags at full resolution is a large, expensive, mostly useless dataset; the useful subset is typically a few hundred tags around the specific equipment and question. Requests for everything usually mean the question has not been defined yet, and defining it is the actual first task. (3) Quality codes and context are usually dropped in bulk extracts, so the model trains on transmitter failures as if they were process values. This is the commonest cause of industrial models that perform superbly in development and uselessly in production. (4) Security and governance: a bulk pipeline out of the OT environment must cross the DMZ in a controlled, one-way, audited manner (A54), and the data itself may be commercially sensitive or regulated. What I would propose. Start with the question, not the data: which equipment, which failure or which optimisation, and what would a useful answer look like? Then (a) identify the specific tag set and required resolution with the process engineer; (b) for tags that genuinely need it, reduce or remove compression prospectively and, where sub-second structure matters, collect it at the edge from the source rather than through the historian — a vibration or power-quality analyser writing its own high-rate store, with only features published upward; (c) export with quality codes and context (batch, state, grade, mode) and make filtering on quality a documented requirement of the pipeline; (d) move data through a defined boundary — read-only replica or DMZ historian, one-way, audited, no path back to control; (e) keep the model's outputs on the advisory side of the line until it has earned more, exactly as with the cloud-tuning proposal on Page 2. Finally, use the request as evidence in the compression review: if the plant's own analytics team cannot use its history, the compression settings were probably too aggressive for everyone, and this is the moment to fix that for the next twenty years.

The split for batch deserves emphasis because it is so often got wrong. Phase logic belongs in the controller; procedure execution belongs in the batch server. The phase knows how to charge, heat and hold this vessel, including the HOLDING and ABORTING logic that keeps the product safe — and it must be able to do that when the batch server is being patched, has failed over, or has simply gone. The batch server knows the recipe, the order, the parameters and the record. Put the phase logic in the server and a server outage leaves a reactor half-charged with nothing in control of it; put the recipe in the controller and you have re-created the pre-ISA-88 world where a new product needs a programmer (A60 Page 12).

What must survive a batch-server failover mid-batch • The current procedural state of EVERY active unit procedure, operation and phase — including HELD and RESTARTING states • Every acquired resource and its owner (A60 Page 13) • Accumulated values: quantity charged so far, elapsed soak time, the batch's identity and its report data • The operator's outstanding prompts and their responses The three failure modes to test explicitly, at FAT: 1. Do the phases hold safely, and do they resume without duplicating a charge? 3. Fail BOTH (or restart the server) and observe recovery: does the server re-attach to running phases, or does it start a new batch over the top of a live one? Number 3 is the one that finds real defects, and it is the one nobody schedules. A phase that a restarted server cannot re-attach to is a phase that will be finished by hand at 3 a.m.
ADVANCED CONTROL IN THE ARCHITECTURE

Where MPC runs

Traditionally a Level 2 server writing setpoints to regulatory loops every 30–60 s; increasingly available embedded in the controller for smaller applications. Either way the regulatory layer must be complete, tuned and capable of running the plant alone — A58's “base layer first” is an architectural requirement, not just good practice.

The write interface

MPC writes setpoints, never outputs, into loops that validate limits and rates, with an obvious operator on/off and a bumpless handover in both directions. Service factor — the percentage of time the application is actually on — is the metric that matters, and it is usually far lower than the business case assumed.

Simulation & OTS

An operator training simulator running the real control configuration against a process model is the only way to train for upsets that must not be practised on the plant, and the only realistic way to test emergency procedures. It also becomes the safest test bed for configuration changes (A31 digital twins).

Custom code — the quiet liability

Every plant accumulates scripts, spreadsheets and small applications that became load-bearing. Inventory them, name an owner for each, put them in version control, and record what happens if each one stops. The one nobody owns is the one that breaks after the author leaves.

Quick check: A vendor proposes running phase logic in the batch server rather than in the controllers, arguing that it is easier to engineer and gives better recipe flexibility. Evaluate.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The engineering-convenience claim is true; the architecture is wrong, and the reason is availability, not purity. Phase logic is control: it commands valves, pumps and agitators, it holds interlocks, and it contains the HOLDING and ABORTING logic that puts a vessel into a safe, preservable state when something goes wrong. Running it on a server means a server failure, an operating-system patch, a network outage, an anti-virus scan or a failover leaves a reactor part-charged, possibly exothermic, with nothing executing its safe-hold logic. The severity depends on the process, but the pattern is the same: you have made a batch of product dependent on a general-purpose computer's uptime, and general-purpose computers are the least available component in the architecture by a wide margin. Four specific consequences to put to the vendor. (1) Failover behaviour mid-phase. Ask precisely what happens to a charging phase during a server failover: does the valve stay open? Does the totaliser continue? Can the standby re-attach to a phase already in progress, or does it restart it — charging the vessel twice? These questions usually end the discussion. (2) Latency and determinism. Phase logic interacts with interlocks and device feedback on a sub-second timescale; a server-resident phase communicating over a network adds latency and jitter that are outside your control, and puts a Windows scheduler in the path of a valve command. (3) Independence from the recipe layer. The whole architectural point of ISA-88 is that what to make is separable from how the equipment works. Putting both in the same server re-couples them: a recipe change now touches the same executable environment as the equipment control, and the validation burden in a regulated plant increases accordingly. (4) Security and patching. A server must be patched; a controller is patched rarely and deliberately. Making the phase logic patch-dependent puts routine IT maintenance on the critical path of production. Where the vendor has a real point. Server-side engineering genuinely is faster and more flexible, and the pain of controller-resident phase logic is real: limited memory, clumsier editing, and a longer change cycle. So the answer is not to dismiss the concern but to address it: use the vendor's phase-logic templates and class-based engineering (Page 6) to reduce the controller effort; keep the parameterisation and all recipe flexibility on the server where it belongs; and reserve server-side procedural code for genuinely non-critical, non-actuating steps — operator prompts, reporting, data collection — where a server outage costs a record rather than a batch. The general rule, worth stating in the design basis: anything that can command an actuator, or that must run to keep the process safe, executes in the controller. Everything else may live above it. Convenience is a legitimate consideration for the things above the line; it is not a reason to move something across it.

Safety Systems & Independence

The most consequential architectural decision in the whole system, and the one most often made on the basis of a demonstration.

THE THREE ARCHITECTURES
All three can comply with IEC 61511. What differs is how much independence you must demonstrate by other means as you move to the right.
WHAT INDEPENDENCE ACTUALLY REQUIRES
  • 1Separate logic solver. The SIF must not execute on the same processor as the control that may cause the demand. Integrated platforms satisfy this with physically separate, certified SIS controllers inside a common architecture — check that this is real, not marketing.
  • 2Separate sensors and final elements where the risk assessment requires it. If the BPCS uses a transmitter for control and the SIS uses the same transmitter for its trip, a single transmitter failure both causes the demand and defeats the protection. That is the textbook common-cause failure, and IEC 61511 addresses it directly.
  • 3Separate power and separate wiring routes. Independence at the logic level is worth nothing if both systems share a fuse or a cable duct.
  • 4Write protection. The BPCS may read everything from the SIS. The BPCS writing to the SIS must be strictly limited, justified and access-controlled — and never for anything that could defeat a SIF. A reset command is usually the only defensible write.
  • 5Separate access control and change management. Different credentials, a key switch or equivalent for the SIS program, a separate change process, and an audit trail that an assessor can follow. If one engineer with one login can change both, the human is the common cause.
  • 6Independent proof testing, on a defined interval derived from the SIL calculation, with results recorded — the assumption that makes the PFD calculation true (A52).
The clause that catches people out IEC 61511 limits the risk reduction that may be claimed for the BPCS as a protection layer: a risk reduction factor of no more than 10 (i.e. it may not be credited as better than roughly SIL 1) unless the BPCS is itself designed and managed to the standard. Why: the BPCS is changed frequently, online, by many people, without the discipline applied to a SIS. Its systematic failure rate is dominated by human and configuration error, which no hardware calculation captures. Consequence for the architecture: if a LOPA credits an interlock in the DCS with a risk reduction of 100, that interlock is not a DCS interlock — it is a safety instrumented function, and it belongs in the SIS with everything that implies.
THE BYPASS AND THE OPERATOR VIEW

Two practical items dominate day-to-day safety-system architecture. SIS bypasses must be engineered, authorised, time-limited, alarmed, logged and visible — the same governance as A60's interlock bypasses, but with a stricter approval path and, usually, a physical key or a maintenance-override switch rather than a software click. Forcing an SIS I/O point is not a workaround; it is a reportable event. And the operator view must show SIS status alongside process status on the same console — trip status, pre-trip alarms, bypass status, and the first-out cause — because an operator who must walk to a separate panel to find out why the plant tripped is an operator who will be slower during the worst ten minutes of their career.

The final architectural requirement is shared, accurate time (Page 8). An SIS trip investigation is a sequence-of-events problem: which trip fired first, and did the BPCS action precede or follow it? Without 1 ms SOE stamping on both systems against a common clock, the answer is unavailable — and it is exactly the answer the incident investigation will need.

Quick check: A project proposes a fully integrated safety system from the DCS vendor: one engineering tool, one HMI, one network, certified SIS controllers. The safety engineer objects on principle. Who is right?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Neither, as stated — and “on principle” is the weak part of the objection. IEC 61511 does not prohibit integration; it requires that independence be demonstrated, and integrated systems can demonstrate it if they are designed and governed properly. Equally, the project's “it is certified” is not sufficient either, because certification covers the logic solver's hardware and firmware, not your architecture, your access control or your engineering practice. The correct response is to replace the argument of principle with an argument of evidence, item by item. What genuinely improves with integration: one operator view including trip status, pre-trips, bypasses and first-out; a common time base giving true cross-system SOE (which materially improves incident investigation); consistent alarm handling; a single historian; less engineering effort and fewer interface defects; and no home-made mapping layer between two systems — which is itself a common source of error. These are real safety benefits, not just cost benefits, and a safety engineer who dismisses them is trading a known human-factors improvement for a theoretical independence gain. What must be proven, and is where the objection has force: (1) Physically separate, certified logic solvers and I/O for the SIF — not a software partition on the same processor. (2) Separate power supplies and separate cable routes. (3) Enforced write restriction: demonstrate, on the actual system, that the BPCS cannot write anything to the SIS beyond the justified minimum, and that this is enforced by the platform rather than by convention. (4) Separate credentials and a physical key switch or equivalent for SIS program changes, with a separate change-management process and an audit trail an assessor can follow — because in an integrated system with one tool, the engineer is the common cause, and this is the control that addresses it. (5) Network segregation analysis: what does a broadcast storm, a mis-configured switch or a compromised engineering workstation do to the SIS? Under IEC 62443 (A54) the SIS is its own zone with a tightly controlled conduit; “one network” must not mean one zone. (6) Common-cause assessment in the SIL verification: the β factor must reflect the shared platform, shared firmware lineage, shared tool and shared personnel — and if that pushes a SIF below its target, the architecture must change. (7) Independent proof testing that does not depend on the BPCS being healthy. (8) Field-device independence: separate transmitters and final elements where the risk assessment requires them, regardless of how integrated the logic is — the commonest real common-cause failure is a shared impulse line, not a shared CPU. My conclusion: for most modern process plants, an interfaced or properly integrated architecture is the right answer, and the safety engineer's job is not to veto it but to specify and then verify the eight items above, and to write them into the safety requirements specification so they are auditable in ten years. If any one of them cannot be demonstrated on the actual proposed system — particularly write restriction, credential separation and network zoning — then the objection becomes concrete and decisive, and separation is the answer. The principle worth holding: independence is a property you can evidence, not a property you can assert; and the failure mode of integration is not electronics, it is a single person with a single login and a deadline.

WHAT THE ASSET-MANAGEMENT LAYER ACTUALLY DELIVERS
  • 1Device configuration records. Every transmitter's range, damping, units, tag and calibration held centrally, comparable against the design, and restorable after a device swap — which turns a replacement from a half-day configuration exercise into a download.
  • 2Calibration management (A50). Due dates, results, as-found/as-left, certificates, and traceability — the audit deliverable in regulated plants and the basis of measurement confidence everywhere else.
  • 3Valve diagnostics (A59). Travel deviation, air consumption, friction estimates, stiction signatures and partial stroke testing on the SIS valves — the highest-value diagnostic in most process plants, because the valve is usually the loop's weakest element.
  • 4Loop performance monitoring (A58). Which loops are in manual, which are saturated, which oscillate, which have poor service factor. A plant typically discovers that a third of its loops are in manual and nobody knew.
  • 5Rotating-equipment condition monitoring (A42, A41). Vibration and thermal data joined to the same asset model, so that a pump's process data, its vibration signature and its work-order history are one record.
  • 6The workflow to the CMMS. A diagnostic that does not become a work order changes nothing. The integration that matters is asset-management system → maintenance management system, with the tag as the common key (Page 6 — which is why naming consistency is not pedantry).

The failure mode of this entire layer is worth naming, because it is almost universal: a plant buys the asset-management package with the DCS, it is installed, licensed and never configured, and three years later nobody can say whether it is running. The data is being generated by instruments the plant already owns; the missing ingredient is a named owner and a weekly routine of looking at it. Start with one class of asset — control valves, or the ten most troublesome loops — prove the value in work orders avoided, and expand from there.

Quick check: Maintenance complains that the DCS alarm list is full of instrument diagnostics and operators have started acknowledging everything without reading. Diagnose the architectural error and give the fix.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The architectural error is routing by source instead of by audience. Somebody enabled device diagnostics and connected them to the only notification mechanism that existed — the operator alarm list — so a maintenance-planning signal is being delivered as an operational demand, to a person who can do nothing about it, in the middle of their process picture. The consequence is exactly what has happened: the operator learns that most alarms require no action, and that learning generalises to the alarms that do. This is one of the classic pathways to alarm-system collapse (A53), and it is more dangerous than the nuisance it appears to be, because it degrades response to real alarms across the whole plant. The fix, in four steps. (1) Classify every device diagnostic against NE 107 and route accordingly: Failure goes to the operator (the loop has lost its measurement — that is an operational event) and to maintenance; Function check goes to the operator, usually as a status indication on the faceplate rather than an alarm, so they know the value is not real; Out of specification goes to maintenance, and to the operator only if the loop's control depends on the accuracy; Maintenance required goes to maintenance only, never to the alarm list. That single reclassification typically removes the great majority of the offending events. (2) Give maintenance a destination that is not the alarm list. The asset-management system with a device-status dashboard, a daily or weekly report, and — the step that actually changes outcomes — automatic work-order generation into the CMMS for defined conditions. Without a destination, the diagnostics will drift back into the alarm system, because a signal with nowhere to go always ends up wherever there is a queue. (3) Show device health on the faceplate, not as an alarm. The operator needs to know that TI-101's reading is questionable at the moment they look at TI-101 — a small status icon and a greyed or flagged value does that far better than an alarm did, and it is available exactly when it is relevant. This is the ISA-101 principle from Page 7: colour and attention are scarce, and status is not an event. (4) Rationalise what remains, and measure. Count the alarm rate per operator before and after, identify the bad actors, and report the numbers — because the argument for doing this work is won with the ISA-18.2 metrics, not with an appeal to good practice. Then address the behaviour that the bad architecture created. Acknowledging without reading is a learned response to a system that cried wolf, and it does not disappear the day the alarm list improves. It has to be explicitly retired: tell the operators what changed and why, show them the new numbers, and let them see that an alarm now means something. That conversation is part of the fix, not an optional extra.

WHAT A REAL FAT CONTAINS

A factory acceptance test that consists of walking through graphics and confirming that a valve icon turns green is theatre. A useful FAT is a systematic attempt to break the system in the one place where breaking it is free.

Functional

Every loop, every sequence, every interlock against the cause-and-effect matrix, every phase including HOLD, RESTART and ABORT (A60), every batch recipe, and every graphic against the style guide.

Failure & failover

Pull a CPU. Pull a power supply. Pull a network cable. Pull both network cables. Fail the batch server mid-phase. Restart a server. Each time: what did the outputs do, what did the operator see, what did the log record, and how long did it take?

Alarm & performance

Simulate a plant trip and measure the alarm rate, the buffer behaviour and the first-out capture. Load the system to specified I/O and event rates and measure display call-up time and controller loading (Page 3).

Security & recovery

Account and privilege model, audit trail, removable-media policy, patching approach (A54). Then take a backup and restore it onto spare hardware, timed — that time is your worst-case MTTR (Page 5).

Documentation

As-built configuration, drawings, the library version manifest, and the test records themselves. If it is not written down at FAT it will not exist at site.

People

FAT is the best training opportunity in the project. Bring the operators and the maintenance technicians who will live with it, and let them use it while the vendor is still in the room.

One structural point about loop checking at site: it is tedious, it is expensive, it is repeatedly compressed by schedule pressure, and it is the only activity that proves the field wiring matches the database. Every plant that shortened loop checking discovered the consequences during start-up, at the worst possible moment, with a full crew waiting. Protect it.

Quick check: A 22-year-old DCS is in “limited support”. Management refuses migration on cost grounds. You believe the risk is unacceptable. How do you make the case?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Stop arguing about the control system and start arguing about production risk, in money, with the plant's own data. Management is not being unreasonable: they have been shown a large capital number and no comparable number on the other side. Your job is to supply the other side. (1) Quantify the cost of doing nothing, from records rather than assertion. Pull five years of downtime events attributable to the control system: hours lost, production margin lost, and the trend. Add the support premium being paid for a limited-support contract, the cost of spares now bought from brokers, the labour spent nursing it, and any production constraint being lived with because a change is too risky to make. Plot it. If the trend is rising — and at 22 years it will be — that slope is the argument, and it is far more persuasive than any statement about obsolescence. (2) Quantify the tail risk separately, because it is not in the average. Identify the single points of failure whose failure would be unrecoverable: a controller type with no spares anywhere, a proprietary console, an engineering workstation running an operating system that cannot be reinstalled because the licence server is gone, a configuration backup that has never been restore-tested. For each, state the recovery time honestly — days? weeks? — and multiply by the daily margin. A single credible “we would be down for three weeks and could not rebuild it” item reframes the entire discussion, and it is usually true. Test the backup restore and report the result; if it fails, that is the strongest evidence you will ever have. (3) Present the comparison as a financial model, not a technical case — net present value over ten years, do-nothing versus migrate, with explicit assumptions the finance team can challenge (the demo above is exactly this). Show the breakeven year. Show the sensitivity to the escalation rate, and let management choose the escalation assumption they believe; if the case survives their pessimistic version, it is made. (4) Offer a phased path, not a single number. The refusal is usually to a large lump, not to the idea. Phase it: virtualise the Level 2 layer first to buy several years cheaply; migrate the most obsolete area at the next turnaround; retain field wiring and marshalling to cut cost substantially; spread capital across three budget years. A credible phased plan converts “no” into a scheduling discussion. (5) Include the non-financial exposures that carry weight with a board: cyber security (an unpatchable system with no vendor support is a finding in any assessment, and increasingly a regulatory and insurance issue — A54), functional safety if the system carries protective functions whose modification or proof testing is becoming impractical (A52, Page 11), and the skills risk of a system only two people understand, both close to retirement. (6) Whatever the decision, document it and manage the residual risk. If the answer remains no, put the assessment in the risk register with the numbers, get it formally accepted at the right level, and then do the mitigations that are cheap: buy strategic spares now while they exist, capture and test full backups, document the undocumented, cross-train, and write the recovery plan for each single point of failure. That is not a consolation prize — it is the professional response, and it is also what makes the case unanswerable next year when the trend line has moved again.

MTP, NOA, O-PAS & Virtualisation

Four attempts to break the fifty-year-old bargain in which the DCS's integration is bought with vendor lock-in.

THE PROBLEM ALL FOUR ARE ATTACKING

The DCS's great strength — one integrated system, one database, one vendor — is also its great cost: every extension, every package unit, every new application and every migration is on that vendor's terms, for twenty-five years.

Two specific pains drive the work. First, integration effort for package units: every skid arriving with its own PLC must be manually mapped into the DCS — tags, graphics, alarms, states — typically tens of thousands of engineering hours across a project, repeated identically on the next project. Second, the closed data path: the diagnostic and process data a plant wants for optimisation is inside a system that is deliberately hard to open, so “digital transformation” projects spend most of their budget getting at data the plant already generates.

They are complementary, not competing: MTP addresses modules, NOA addresses data, O-PAS addresses the platform, virtualisation addresses the servers.
MTP — MODULE TYPE PACKAGE

The idea is borrowed from device integration and applied to whole process modules. A skid vendor ships, alongside the equipment, a machine-readable MTP file describing the module's services (“dose”, “heat”, “clean”, each with parameters and an ISA-88-style state machine), its HMI (the graphics, pre-built), its alarms, its historisation and its communication interface (OPC UA). The plant's control system — the Process Orchestration Layer — imports the file and the module appears, fully integrated, in minutes rather than weeks.

What changes, in practical terms Classic: skid arrives → read its documentation → agree a tag list → build the interface → draw graphics → configure alarms → test → repeat for every skid, every project. Typically 100–300 engineering hours per module. MTP: skid arrives with its MTP file → import → orchestrate. The module's own vendor has already done the integration work, once, in a standard form. Where it pays modular plants, pharma and specialty chemicals, containerised units, plants that are reconfigured often, and any project with many package units. Standards route VDI/VDE/NAMUR 2658 (several parts), now moving to an industry specification under NAMUR and PI, with the international route via IEC 63280. The catch it only works if the SKID VENDOR supplies a good MTP file — so it must be in the purchase specification, with acceptance criteria, or you will not get one.
NOA — NAMUR OPEN ARCHITECTURE

NOA (described in NAMUR recommendation NE 175) answers a question every plant faces: how do we get modern monitoring and optimisation without touching a control system that is validated, certified, secure and working? Its answer is to leave the core process control system alone and add a second, deliberately asymmetric path for monitoring and optimisation data.

  • 1The core stays untouched. Control, safety and their lifecycle are unchanged. This is the whole political achievement of NOA — it removes the objection that every digitalisation idea is a change to the control system.
  • 2Data flows outward from the core to a monitoring-and-optimisation domain, through a one-way or strictly controlled gateway — conceptually a diode. Data goes out; commands do not come back in.
  • 3A second channel from the field may bypass the core entirely: NE 107 diagnostics, additional sensors and wireless devices reporting straight to the M+O domain without ever entering the control system.
  • 4Where feedback is wanted, it is a verified, governed request into the core — never an unmediated write. The asymmetry is deliberate and permanent.
  • 5It composes with the rest: Ethernet-APL gives the field bandwidth (Page 4), OPC UA gives the information model, MTP gives the modules, and NOA gives the safe route out.

Notice how exactly this matches the rule from Page 2 — control flows down and must not depend on anything above; data flows up and may be as clever as you like. NOA is that principle turned into a reference architecture, with the security boundary made explicit rather than assumed.

O-PAS AND VIRTUALISATION — THE HONEST APPRAISAL

O-PAS: the ambition

A standards-based, open, secure, interoperable architecture in which control applications are portable across standard hardware — Distributed Control Nodes — from any conforming vendor, connected by an OPC UA-based framework, with standard configuration portability. If it succeeds, the migration cliff of Page 13 disappears: you replace nodes without replacing applications.

O-PAS: where it stands

Version 2.1 is published as a preliminary standard, with V3 work covering the physical node platform, application portability and orchestration. Field trials and prototypes exist; the installed base does not yet. Treat it as a direction to watch and to reference in procurement, not as a system you can buy for a plant next year.

Virtualisation: what it genuinely fixes

Server and workstation obsolescence decoupled from operating-system and hardware refresh; snapshots before every change; a realistic test and training environment for the cost of some RAM; far faster disaster recovery; and consolidation of a rack of ageing PCs into a supported platform. All vendors now support it for Level 2. This is the lowest-risk, highest-return item on this page.

Virtualisation: what it costs

A new dependency — the hypervisor, its storage and its licensing — and a new common cause (Page 5): one storage array failure can take every console and server at once. Design for it: redundant hosts, redundant storage, separated power, and at least one physical console that works when the virtual estate does not.

What to do with all this, as a working engineer NOW Virtualise Level 2 at the next refresh. Insist on MTP files in package-unit purchase specifications. Get NE 107 device diagnostics routed to maintenance (Page 12). Build the data path outward with an explicit boundary — that IS NOA in practice, whether or not you call it that. WATCH O-PAS conformance in vendor roadmaps; reference it in procurement to keep the pressure on, but do not stake a plant on it yet. RESIST Any of the above used as a reason to weaken the rule from Page 2. Openness is not a reason to let Level 3 hold Level 1 hostage, and a container is still a computer.
Quick check: A new plant will have eleven package units from seven vendors. The integration estimate is 2 400 engineering hours. What would you specify, and what is the realistic saving?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Specify MTP in the package-unit purchase documents — and specify it properly, because a vague requirement produces a vague file. Eleven modules at roughly 220 hours each is exactly the profile MTP was created for, and the saving is real but conditional on procurement doing its part before any equipment is ordered. What to put in the specification. (1) An MTP file conforming to VDI/VDE/NAMUR 2658 (naming the parts and revision), delivered with the equipment and included in the acceptance criteria — not “on request”, not “available later”. (2) The services the module must expose, with their parameters and their state model, so that the module can actually be orchestrated rather than merely displayed — if the MTP file exposes only monitoring data, you have bought a graphic, not an integration. (3) HMI, alarms and historisation content in the file, so the operator picture and the alarm list are consistent with the rest of the plant rather than eleven different design philosophies. (4) OPC UA server with the specified security profile, certificate handling and the plant's naming conventions. (5) Acceptance testing of the MTP file itself: import it into the orchestration layer at FAT and demonstrate the module running from it. This clause is what makes the rest enforceable. (6) A fallback: if a vendor genuinely cannot supply MTP, the contract should price the conventional integration and put that cost on the vendor's side of the ledger, so the commercial pressure sits where the decision is made. The realistic saving. Not 100 %. Expect the per-module integration effort to fall substantially — a realistic planning assumption is a 40–70 % reduction on the modules that arrive with good MTP files, so perhaps 1 000–1 600 hours saved if most vendors comply, plus a second, less visible saving on rework, because the vendor's own description of their module is authoritative and does not have to be reverse-engineered from a manual. Against that, budget for: the orchestration layer's capability (check your DCS actually supports MTP import, and to which revision), some effort per module to reconcile naming and alarm priorities with plant standards, and one or two vendors who will supply a poor file that needs correction. The strategic point worth making to the project. The saving on this project is the smaller half of the value. The larger half is that the modules become replaceable and reconfigurable later, that a future project inherits the same interface, and that the plant is not re-integrating a skid every time one is swapped. That argument is what justifies spending the effort on procurement language now — and procurement language is the entire mechanism, because MTP is not something you can add to a skid after it has been built.

RECAP — THE EIGHT IDEAS

1 · Integration, not distribution

A DCS is defined by one engineering database and one namespace. That is what cannot be bought elsewhere and what justifies the price.

2 · Control down, data up

Control must never depend on anything above it. Unplug Level 3: if the plant cannot run safely, you have a control dependency wearing an information-system badge.

3 · Execution period dominates loading

Put every module in the task its process requires, not the fastest available. Design controllers to 60–70 %, because the upset is when you need the headroom.

4 · β and MTTR beat redundancy

Redundancy cannot reduce unavailability below β × U. Ask what takes out both channels, and how fast you can restore.

5 · Design the classes before the instances

The library is the project. Build it in the first six weeks or forfeit the system's main benefit while still paying for it.

6 · Route by audience

Operator alarms answer “what must I do now?”. Everything else — NE 107 diagnostics, maintenance conditions, analytics — goes somewhere else.

7 · Independence is evidenced, not asserted

Integrated safety can comply, but only if separate solvers, separate power, write restriction, separate credentials and network zoning are demonstrated on the actual system.

8 · The system is bought once and lived with for 25 years

Hardware is a fifth of the cost. Engineering effort, migration path, security posture and local support decide what it costs to own.

SIX ARCHITECTURAL FAILURE MODES

The inverted dependency

A Level 3 or cloud system that control has come to depend on. Symptom: an MES outage stops production. Cure: validate every incoming write, define fallback on loss, and test by unplugging it.

Shared common cause

Redundant everything, on one power feed, in one cabinet, through one duct, engineered by one person with one login. Symptom: a “six nines” system down for a day. Cure: hunt β, not MTBF.

The eroded class

Instance-level edits during commissioning destroy the class. Symptom: propagation becomes unsafe, so the library freezes. Cure: parameterise, conform, and give commissioning a fast legitimate route.

Alarms by source

Device diagnostics routed to the operator. Symptom: 4 000 alarms and blind acknowledgement. Cure: NE 107 classification and a maintenance destination.

Unsynchronised clocks

Discovered during an incident investigation, when the sequence of events turns out to be fiction. Cure: PTP/NTP everywhere, stamp at source, SOE on trip-critical inputs — before you need it.

Silent obsolescence

A 20-year-old system nursed along with no plan, no tested backup and no spares. Symptom: a routine failure becomes a three-week outage. Cure: quantify the trend, test the restore, phase the migration.

Final check 1: Give the single test that distinguishes an information system from a control system, and explain why it matters more as architectures open up.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The test: unplug it. If the plant can continue to run safely and controllably for as long as it takes to restore the system, it is an information system. If it cannot, it is a control system — and it must be engineered, secured, governed and maintained as one, regardless of what it is called, who supplied it, or which level of the diagram it was drawn on. The test matters because labels drift and dependencies accumulate quietly. A dashboard becomes the only place a setpoint is calculated; a scheduling system becomes the only source of a recipe parameter; an analytics service starts writing a bias; a “reporting” server ends up holding a lookup table that a controller reads every scan. No single step was unreasonable, and no step was reviewed as a control-system change, because at each step the thing was called an information system. The plant discovers the truth during the first outage of whatever it was. Why it matters more as architectures open up. Every trend in Page 14 — edge computing, unified namespaces, cloud analytics, MTP orchestration, open platforms — makes it easier and cheaper to connect something new to the control system, and easier for that something to acquire a role nobody authorised. The old architecture's rigidity was accidentally protective: it was so hard to get data out that nothing much got in either. Remove that friction without replacing it with governance and the dependency creep accelerates. This is exactly why NOA's asymmetry is its most important feature — not the data path outward, which everyone wants, but the deliberate refusal of an unmediated path back in. What to do with the test in practice. Apply it at design review to every new connection, and write the answer down: what does the plant do when this stops, for a minute, for an hour, for a week? If the answer is “nothing bad”, the system is free to be as clever, as cloudy and as frequently updated as it likes. If the answer is anything else, it inherits the whole burden — availability targets, change control, security zoning, backup and restore, competent support, and a fallback that has been tested rather than assumed. And run the test periodically on things you already have, because the answer changes over time without anybody deciding that it should.
Final check 2: You have one week and no budget on an unfamiliar plant. What five things would you check to assess the health of its control architecture?
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Take a backup and restore it onto spare hardware, timed. Nothing reveals more, faster. It tests whether backups exist, whether they are current, whether anyone knows the procedure, whether the licence and media situation is recoverable, and it produces a hard number — your worst-case MTTR — that feeds every availability argument you will make afterwards. Plants fail this test far more often than anyone expects, and a failure is the single most powerful piece of evidence you can bring to management. (2) Pull the alarm statistics for the last month and compare with the ISA-18.2 targets: average rate per operator, peak rate, standing alarms, top ten bad actors, priority distribution. This measures the health of the operator's working environment, and the bad-actor list is an immediate, cheap improvement plan. If the rate is ten times target, you have found the plant's largest human-factors risk in an hour. (3) Check controller loading and task allocation across every controller, and check clock synchronisation across every device including package units and the SIS. Loading tells you whether the system can absorb an upset or a failover; clock sync tells you whether any future investigation will be possible at all. Both are read-only, take a morning, and are frequently alarming. (4) Ask to see the class library and then diff twenty instances against their classes. This measures engineering discipline — whether the system's main advantage is being realised or has been eroded by instance edits (Page 6) — and it predicts what any future change will cost. While there, look at the audit trail: is it enabled, is anyone reading it, and what has changed in the last month? (5) Walk the control room during a shift and talk to the operators. Ask what they ignore and why; what they have written on sticky notes; which displays they never use; which alarms they acknowledge without reading; what they do when the plant upsets; and what they wish someone would fix. Operators know where the architecture is failing, in detail, and are almost never asked. This also surfaces the bypasses, the workarounds and the informal procedures that no document records. Two more if the week allows: check the obsolescence status of every hardware type against the vendor's lifecycle policy and the spares holding, which sizes the tail risk of Page 13; and look at the OT/IT boundary — how many paths cross it, who has remote access, and when anything was last patched (A54). What I would deliberately not do is start by reading the documentation. The documentation describes the plant that was designed; the five checks above describe the plant that exists, and the gap between them is usually the most interesting finding of the week.
Final check 3: Summarise, in your own words, why the whole control track — theory, advanced control, valves, sequential control and this architecture module — hangs together.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because a control system is a chain, and its performance is set by the weakest link — and the five modules are the five links. A57 (theory) establishes what is achievable at all: poles, dead time, stability margins and the fact that a loop's dynamics are set by the process, not by the controller. It tells you what “good” looks like and why some specifications are impossible. A58 (advanced control) is what you do when a single feedback loop is structurally insufficient — when a disturbance is measurable, when the interaction between loops matters, when a constraint should be handled rather than avoided. Every strategy in it presupposes that the base layer of A57 already works, which is why “base layer first” is the recurring instruction. A59 (final elements) is the physical link that turns a controller output into a change in the plant, and it is where most disappointing loop performance actually originates: a valve with 3 % stiction cannot be tuned into a good loop by any mathematics, and no amount of A57 or A58 will help. A60 (sequential control) covers everything that happens when the plant is not at a steady operating point — start-up, shutdown, batch, interlocks, mode changes — which is where the hazards and most of the engineering hours are, and which regulatory control has nothing to say about. A61 (architecture) is the system all four run on: it decides whether the loops execute deterministically, whether the operator can see what is happening, whether an alarm is trustworthy, whether the sequence's diagnostics survive a server failure, whether the history exists to diagnose any of it afterwards, and whether the whole thing is still supportable in twenty years. Three threads run through all five. First, independence and hierarchy: fast things must not depend on slow things, and protection must not depend on the thing it protects against — the same principle in A57's cascade separation, A58's base layer, A60's control-flows-down rule, A61's Level 3 test and Page 11's SIS independence. Second, evidence over assertion: measure the step response, prove the P-invariant, verify the valve travel, test the failover, compute the availability — in every module the professional move is to replace a belief with a number. Third, design for the abnormal case: tuning for the upset, HOLD and RESTART logic, cavitation at the worst duty, timeouts derived from cold-start data, controller headroom for the flood. The plant is easy to control when everything is normal; every one of these modules earns its keep in the ten minutes when it is not.

WE1 — Controller sizing and partitioning.
A unit needs 60 interlock modules at 100 ms, 220 regulatory loops at 500 ms, 400 monitoring calculations at 2 s, 2 400 I/O points and 800 peer parameters/s. Take 0.25 ms CPU per module execution, 0.01 ms per I/O point per 100 ms I/O scan, 0.02 ms per peer parameter, and 8 % fixed system overhead. (a) Compute the load on one controller. (b) Assess it. (c) What would putting everything in the 100 ms task cost? (d) Partition it.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) The arithmetic, term by term. Each term is n × c / T, expressed as CPU-seconds per second.
Fast: 60 × 0.25 ms / 0.1 s = 150 ms/s = 15.0 %
Normal: 220 × 0.25 / 0.5 = 110 ms/s = 11.0 %
Slow: 400 × 0.25 / 2.0 = 50 ms/s = 5.0 %
I/O: 2 400 × 0.01 / 0.1 = 240 ms/s = 24.0 %
Peer: 800 × 0.02 = 16 ms/s = 1.6 %
Overhead: 8.0 %
Total = 64.6 %. (b) Assessment: reject it, but narrowly. It is inside the vendor's limit and at the very top of the design range of 60–70 %. Two observations decide it. First, the largest single term is I/O scanning at 24 %, which is fixed by the plant and cannot be tuned away — a useful reminder that controller loading is not mostly about control. Second, and decisively, a redundancy failover must be survivable: during and immediately after a failover the surviving CPU carries the whole load plus the re-synchronisation work, and an alarm flood or a plant trip adds event and communication traffic on top. A controller at 64.6 % on a quiet day has perhaps 10 percentage points of genuine headroom, and ten years of small additions will consume it. I would not commission a new design at this figure. (c) The cost of “put it all in the fast task”. Moving the 400 monitoring modules from 2 s to 100 ms costs 400 × 0.25 × (1/0.1 − 1/2.0) = +95 percentage points. Moving the 220 loops from 500 ms to 100 ms costs +44 points. Either change alone destroys the controller. This is the single most important number on the page: execution period, not module count, dominates loading, and the instinct to run everything fast “to be safe” is precisely backwards — it makes the controller less able to do its job when it matters. (d) Partitioning. Split the unit into two controllers along a process boundary, not an alphabetical one, so that tightly coupled loops and their interlocks stay together and the peer traffic across the split is small. Assume roughly half of everything each, and allow peer traffic to rise to 1 400 parameters/s per controller because some interlocks now cross the boundary: each controller carries 30 fast + 110 normal + 200 slow + 1 200 I/O + 1 400 peer + 8 % = 38.3 %. That is a comfortable, expandable design with room for a failover, an upset and a decade of additions. Note that the peer traffic doubled and cost only 2.8 points — peer communication is cheap; latency across the boundary is the real constraint, so any interlock that must act in one scan should not cross it. The design rules this example illustrates: assign every module to the task its process dynamics require (A57: T ≤ 0.1τ); move monitoring maths off the controller entirely where you can (Page 3); partition on process coupling; and size for the failover case, not the steady state.
WE2 — Where the availability actually goes.
A control loop comprises: transmitter (MTBF 40 yr, MTTR 4 h), IS barrier (60 yr, 4 h), I/O module (30 yr, 2 h), redundant controller pair (15 yr per channel, 8 h, β = 5 %), redundant network (25 yr per channel, 4 h, β = 5 %), and control valve with positioner (8 yr, 12 h). (a) Compute the loop's unavailability and annual downtime. (b) Where does it come from? (c) Compare three improvement options.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) Element by element, using U = MTTR/(MTBF + MTTR), and for the redundant pairs Upair = (1−β)U² + βU:
transmitter U = 4/(40×8760) = 1.142×10−5 → 0.10 h/yr
IS barrier U = 7.61×10−6 → 0.07 h/yr
I/O module U = 7.61×10−6 → 0.07 h/yr
controller channel U = 8/(15×8760) = 6.09×10−5; pair = 0.95(6.09×10−5)² + 0.05(6.09×10−5) = 3.05×10−6 → 0.03 h/yr
network pair = 9.14×10−7 → 0.01 h/yr
valve + positioner U = 12/(8×8760) = 1.712×10−4 → 1.50 h/yr
The elements are in series, and for small U the unavailabilities simply add: Utotal = 2.02×10−4, A = 0.99980, downtime = 1.77 h/yr. (b) Where it comes from. The valve alone contributes 84.8 % of the loop's unavailability. The entire electronic chain — transmitter, barrier, I/O, redundant controllers, redundant network — contributes about 0.27 h/yr between them, and the redundant controller pair contributes 0.03 h/yr, less than 2 %. Notice also why the valve dominates: not because its MTBF is catastrophic, but because it has both the shortest MTBF (8 years, being a mechanical device in the process) and the longest MTTR (12 hours, because repairing it usually means isolating and often draining). Both terms work against it. (c) Three improvement options, compared honestly. (i) Make the controller pair perfect — infinite MTBF, zero repair time, free: downtime falls from 1.77 to 1.74 h/yr, a saving of 0.03 h/yr. Any money spent hardening the controller further is wasted, and this is the calculation to produce when someone proposes triple-redundant controllers for an ordinary process loop. (ii) Halve the valve MTTR from 12 h to 6 h — achieved by holding a spare positioner and a spare valve assembly, having a trained technician on site, and providing block-and-bypass so the valve can be changed without a unit shutdown: downtime falls to 1.02 h/yr, a saving of 0.75 h/yr, twenty-five times the benefit of a perfect controller. (iii) Double the valve MTBF from 8 to 16 years — achieved by correct sizing and trim selection to avoid cavitation, better packing, cleaner instrument air, and a condition-based maintenance regime driven by positioner diagnostics (A59, Page 12): downtime also falls to 1.02 h/yr. The generalisable results. Series chains are dominated by their worst element, so improvement effort must be directed by the numbers, not by where the technology is most interesting. In process plants the worst element is almost always the final element, which is exactly A59's argument arriving from a different direction. And MTTR is usually the cheaper lever than MTBF — spares, skills, access and procedures are operational decisions you control, whereas MTBF is largely bought with the equipment. Finally, note the β effect hiding in the controller line: with β = 5 %, the pair's unavailability is 3.05×10−6, of which the common-cause term contributes 3.04×10−6 — over 99 %. The independent-failure term is essentially irrelevant. That is derivation 1 on Page 18.
WE3 — Fieldbus segment design.
A FOUNDATION Fieldbus H1 segment is proposed: 14 devices at 20 mA each, 1.5 km of type-A cable (44 Ω/km loop at 20 °C), power supply 24 V, devices requiring at least 9 V. (a) Will it work? (b) How many devices can the 1.5 km run carry with a proper margin? (c) What does 60 °C ambient do? (d) What are the options?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) No — and not marginally. Total segment current I = 14 × 20 mA = 0.28 A. Loop resistance = 44 Ω/km × 1.5 km = 66 Ω. Voltage drop = 0.28 × 66 = 18.48 V. Voltage at the far device = 24 − 18.48 = 5.52 V, against a requirement of 9 V. The segment fails by 3.48 V — a shortfall of nearly 40 %. The devices at the near end will work; those at the far end will not communicate reliably, and the failure will be intermittent and temperature-dependent, which is the worst possible signature. (b) Device limit at 1.5 km. Requiring a 20 % margin above the 9 V minimum means the far device must see at least 10.8 V, so the permissible drop is 24 − 10.8 = 13.2 V. Then n ≤ 13.2 / (0.020 × 44 × 1.5) = 10 devices exactly. So the segment must be reduced from 14 to 10, or split. (c) Temperature. Copper's resistance rises with temperature at about 0.393 %/°C. From 20 °C to 60 °C the multiplier is 1 + 0.00393×40 = 1.157, so 44 Ω/km becomes 50.9 Ω/km — a 16 % increase that consumes most of a 20 % margin on its own. This is why the calculation must be done at the worst-case installed temperature, not at 20 °C: a segment commissioned in the cool season and failing in April is a classic, and the diagnosis is invariably chased in the wrong place. Checking a better design — 12 devices on 1.0 km — at 60 °C: drop = 0.24 × 50.9 × 1.0 = 12.22 V, far-end voltage 11.78 V, margin 2.78 V (31 % above the 9 V minimum). That is a sound design. (d) The options, and how to choose. (i) Reduce the device count to 10 and start a second segment — the straightforward answer, at the cost of another power conditioner and port. (ii) Shorten the trunk by moving the power conditioner or the junction box closer to the device cluster; drop is proportional to length, so this is often the cheapest fix and it also improves the margin against temperature. (iii) Heavier cable: a lower Ω/km type reduces the drop proportionally, though it costs more and may not be available in the installed route. (iv) Raise the supply voltage within the conditioner's and the IS certification's limits — but note that in a hazardous area the intrinsic-safety entity parameters cap the available voltage and current, and the IS calculation, not the voltage-drop calculation, may be the binding constraint. (v) Reconsider the technology: this is precisely the constraint Ethernet-APL was designed to relieve, offering 10 Mbit/s over 1 000 m trunks with a defined power scheme and 2-WISE intrinsic safety, and for a greenfield design it removes both the bandwidth and much of the segment-planning pain. Two rules to carry away. Design segments at the worst-case temperature, fully populated, with at least 20 % voltage margin — because devices draw more when transmitting, someone will add a device in year three, and the same arithmetic governs FF H1, PROFIBUS PA and APL alike. And remember that the drop is proportional to total segment current, so every added device hurts twice: more current, and usually more length.
WE4 — Historian sizing and the compression decision.
20 000 tags collected at a 1 s base scan, 12 bytes per archived value. (a) What is the raw data rate and annual volume? (b) Size the archive at 2 %, 5 % and 20 % survival after exception and compression. (c) Which would you choose and why? (d) What must never be compressed?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) Raw. 20 000 tags / 1 s = 20 000 values/s. At 12 bytes each that is 240 kB/s, and over a year (31.536 × 106 s) it is 7.57 TB/yr — storable today, but the write rate is the real constraint: 20 000 small writes per second is what a relational database cannot sustain and a time-series engine is built for. (b) After filtering.
2 % survival → 400 values/s → 151 GB/yr → 0.76 TB over 5 years
5 % survival → 1 000 values/s → 378 GB/yr → 1.89 TB over 5 years
20 % survival → 4 000 values/s → 1.51 TB/yr → 7.57 TB over 5 years
(c) Which to choose. The instinct to pick 2 % because it is cheapest is the wrong frame, because the entire difference between the aggressive and generous settings is 7 TB over five years — a trivial amount of storage measured against the value of a single avoided incident investigation or one salvaged batch. I would specify the 20 % figure as the budget and then, far more importantly, set the deviations per tag rather than globally: tight on the tags that matter (control PVs and MVs on critical loops, anything feeding a model, anything used in a batch record), loose on slow, uninformative tags (tank levels that move over hours, ambient temperatures, run-hour counters). A single global default is what produces both problems at once — over-compressing the tags you need and under-compressing the ones you do not. Add a maximum-time rule so every tag is written at least every few minutes regardless, which is what lets you distinguish “unchanged” from “collector was dead”. And size the hardware for the write rate and the query load, not only the disk: 4 000 values/s sustained, with backfill bursts after a network outage, is a specification the storage subsystem must actually meet. (d) Never compress. Sequence-of-events and alarm records (the whole point is the exact order and time — Page 8); batch-critical data and anything in an electronic batch record; custody transfer and fiscal metering; regulatory and emissions data; laboratory results; and any tag feeding a model, a soft sensor or a performance calculation. Also avoid compressing anything you might need for fast transient analysis — trip investigations, surge events, power-quality disturbances (A56) — because compression is tuned for the human eye on a trend and discards exactly the structure those analyses need. The decisive point: compression is a permanent, irreversible decision about what the plant will be able to remember. The discarded samples do not exist anywhere and cannot be recovered later. Disk is cheap; a lost transient is gone forever. Be generous, per tag, and revisit the settings whenever an investigation finds the data was not there.
WE5 — The migration business case.
A 22-year-old DCS. Migration costs LKR 180 million plus 6 days of cutover downtime at LKR 12 million/day of lost margin. Current obsolescence cost is LKR 45 million/year (unplanned downtime, brokered spares, support premium), escalating at 25 %/yr. Discount rate 12 %, horizon 10 years. (a) Build the case. (b) Find the breakeven. (c) What is the weakest assumption and how would you defend it?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) The two sides in the same units. Migrate now: capital 180 + downtime (6 × 12 = 72) = LKR 252 million at t = 0. Do nothing: the annual cost is 45 × 1.25(t−1), discounted at 12 %:
yr 1: 45.0 → 40.2 discounted, cumulative 40.2
yr 2: 56.2 → 44.8, cum 85.0
yr 3: 70.3 → 50.0, cum 135.1
yr 4: 87.9 → 55.9, cum 190.9
yr 5: 109.9 → 62.3, cum 253.3
yr 10: 335.3 → 107.9, cum 691.8
(b) Breakeven in year 5: the discounted cost of doing nothing passes the one-off cost of migrating during the fifth year. Over the ten-year horizon, doing nothing costs LKR 692 million against LKR 252 million — a factor of 2.75. Presented this way, the decision is not “spend 252 million” but “spend 252 million now or 692 million in instalments, and take the risk in the meantime”. Two refinements that strengthen it. The do-nothing case should also carry the tail risk separately — the probability-weighted cost of an unrecoverable failure (no spares, no restore) that puts the plant down for weeks — which does not appear in an average annual figure and is often the largest single number available. And the migrate case should credit any benefits: recovered production from better control, reduced support cost, and the capability improvements (alarm rationalisation, asset management, MTP-ready integration) that the new system enables. Both are legitimate; both should be stated as assumptions and defended separately from the core arithmetic. (c) The weakest assumption is the 25 % escalation, and it deserves to be challenged because it drives the whole answer. Defend it with the plant's own data, not with a rule of thumb: extract five years of control-system-attributable downtime hours and cost, plot them, and fit the trend. If the plant's own history shows 25 % or more, the assumption is evidence. Then present a sensitivity table — breakeven at 10 %, 15 %, 25 % and 40 % escalation — and let management choose the figure they believe. If the case survives their most conservative assumption, it is made; if it only works at 25 %, you have learned something important about its robustness. Also test the discount rate and the downtime-day assumption, since a phased migration can cut cutover downtime substantially and shift capital across budget years, which usually matters more to the decision than the NPV does. The presentational lesson: engineers lose this argument by describing the technology and win it by quantifying the alternative. The cost of doing nothing is real, it is already being paid, and it is invisible until somebody puts it on the same slide as the capital request.

Problem Set

Ten problems of increasing difficulty. P10 is a full architecture synthesis.

P1. A controller is loaded to 55 %. The project adds 800 I/O points to it (I/O cost 0.01 ms per point per 100 ms scan). Compute the new load and state whether the addition is acceptable.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The added I/O load is 800 × 0.01 ms / 0.1 s = 80 ms/s = 8 percentage points, taking the controller from 55 % to 63 %. That is inside the 60–70 % design band but at its upper half, and the decision should not be made on the number alone. Three checks decide it. (1) Is there also a module load coming with those points? 800 I/O points implies perhaps 300–500 new control modules; at 0.25 ms and 500 ms that is another 15–25 points, which would take the controller to 78–88 % and make the addition clearly unacceptable. The I/O term is rarely the whole story, and quoting it alone is a classic way to slide a controller past its limit. (2) What is the failover and upset behaviour at 63 %? Check the vendor's guidance on load during failover and re-synchronisation, and confirm the event-handling headroom for an alarm flood. (3) What is the ten-year growth? If this plant has historically added 3–5 % of load per year, 63 % today is 80 % in five years, and the capital decision to add a controller will then be made under pressure rather than deliberately. My answer: acceptable only if the 800 points bring little module load and there is a documented plan for the next controller. Otherwise, add the controller now and partition on a process boundary while there is time to do it properly.
P2. Two identical channels each have unavailability U = 1×10−4. Compute the pair's unavailability for β = 0, 1 %, 5 % and 10 %. At what value of U does the common-cause term equal the independent term for β = 5 %?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Using Upair = (1−β)U² + βU with U = 10−4:
β = 0: Upair = 1.00×10−8 — the ideal, a ten-thousand-fold improvement
β = 1 %: 0.99×10−8 + 1.0×10−6 = 1.01×10−6
β = 5 %: 0.95×10−8 + 5.0×10−6 = 5.01×10−6
β = 10 %: 0.90×10−8 + 1.0×10−5 = 1.00×10−5
Even at β = 1 %, the common-cause term is 100 times the independent term, and at 5 % it is over 500 times. The U² term — the entire mathematical justification for redundancy — has become numerically irrelevant. The crossover: setting (1−β)U² = βU gives U = β/(1−β) = 0.05/0.95 = 0.0526, i.e. the terms are equal only when each channel is unavailable more than 5 % of the time — roughly 460 hours a year. For any component worth putting in a control system, U is orders of magnitude smaller, so common cause always dominates. The engineering conclusion: the redundancy calculation you should actually do is not the U² one, it is the β one — and β is reduced by physical separation (separate power feeds, separate cabinets, separate cable routes, separate cooling), by diversity (different firmware versions, different sensing principles), and by governance (separate credentials, staged rollouts, two people rather than one). Those are design and organisational choices, not purchasing choices.
P3. An MPC application writes setpoints to twelve loops every 60 s from a Level 2 server. List everything that must be specified about its failure behaviour, and say why each matters.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) What each loop does when the writes stop. The default must be: hold the last valid setpoint and continue on regulatory control, indefinitely and safely. This is the “unplug it” test — if the plant cannot run on the base layer, the MPC has become a control dependency and the base layer is inadequate (A58's “base layer first”). (2) The watchdog and its timeout. Each loop needs a heartbeat from the application with a defined staleness limit — typically a small multiple of the write period, say 3–5 minutes — after which the loop declares the source dead, alarms, and reverts to a defined state. Without staleness detection, a frozen application writing the same value forever is indistinguishable from a working one. (3) Limit and rate validation at the receiving loop. Every incoming setpoint is clamped to engineering limits and to a maximum rate of change, in the controller, regardless of what the application sends. The receiver owns the decision. (4) Bumpless transfer in both directions, on drop-out and on re-engagement — and on re-engagement the application must initialise from the current plant state rather than resuming from its own stale model. (5) Operator visibility and a one-click off. The operator must see, per loop, whether the setpoint is coming from the application or from them, and be able to take it off instantly and obviously. (6) Mode interaction: what happens if an operator puts one of the twelve loops in manual, or if a loop saturates? The application must detect the loss of a handle and degrade gracefully rather than fighting it or continuing to assume control it no longer has. (7) Partial failure — can it write to some loops and not others, and is a partially-applied solution safe? For a multivariable controller the answer is often no, in which case the design must be all-or-nothing. (8) Service factor measurement, because the metric that determines whether the business case was ever realised is the percentage of time the application is actually on, and it is usually far lower than assumed. (9) Restart behaviour after a server reboot or failover: does it re-engage automatically, or require an operator action? For a supervisory application, requiring a deliberate re-engagement is usually correct. (10) Security and audit of the write path (Page 2). The unifying principle: an advanced application is an optional layer whose loss must be a non-event. Design the failure behaviour first, and the rest follows.
P4. A PROFIBUS PA segment has 9 devices at 15 mA, 900 m of cable at 44 Ω/km, supply 24 V, minimum device voltage 9 V. Compute the far-end voltage at 20 °C and at 55 °C, and state how many devices could be added.
Tap to reveal ↓A: At 20 °C: I = 9 × 15 mA = 0.135 A; R = 44 × 0.9 = 39.6 Ω; drop = 0.135 × 39.6 = 5.35 V; far-end voltage = 24 − 5.35 = 18.65 V, a margin of 9.65 V over the 9 V minimum — very comfortable. At 55 °C: the copper multiplier is 1 + 0.00393×35 = 1.1376, so R becomes 44 × 1.1376 = 50.1 Ω/km, giving 45.1 Ω over 900 m; drop = 0.135 × 45.1 = 6.09 V; far-end voltage = 17.91 V. Still ample. How many more devices? Working at the worst case (55 °C) and requiring a 20 % margin, the far device must see at least 10.8 V, so the permissible drop is 13.2 V and the permissible current is 13.2 / 45.1 = 0.293 A, i.e. 0.293/0.015 = 19 devices — so 10 more could be added on voltage grounds alone. But voltage is only one of four constraints, and on this segment it is not the binding one. Check also: (i) the power conditioner's current rating, commonly 350–500 mA, which 19 devices at 15 mA (285 mA) approaches; (ii) in a hazardous area, the intrinsic-safety entity parameters, which typically cap the segment at a far lower current than the voltage budget allows and are usually the real limit; (iii) the maximum device count for the protocol and the host, and the macrocycle time — more devices means a longer scan, and on PA a heavily loaded segment can slow the update rate below what the loops need; and (iv) spur lengths and the total cable length including spurs, which have their own limits. The engineering habit worth forming: compute all four constraints and quote the smallest. A segment designed only on voltage drop will be re-engineered on site when the IS calculation or the macrocycle is checked.
P5. An incident investigation cannot determine whether a compressor trip preceded or followed a downstream valve closure. The compressor is on a package PLC, the valve on the DCS. What went wrong architecturally, and what would you install?
Tap to reveal ↓A: What went wrong: no common, accurate time base, and no sequence-of-events capability on the events that mattered. The package PLC's clock was never synchronised to the plant's, so its event times are drifting by an unknown amount — typically seconds to minutes over months — and the two systems' logs cannot be merged into a valid sequence. Compounding it, both systems probably time-stamped at the point of reception by a server rather than at the source, adding queueing and polling delays of hundreds of milliseconds to events that may be separated by tens of milliseconds. The result is that the single most important question in the investigation — which was cause and which was consequence — is unanswerable, and no amount of later analysis can recover it, because the information was never captured. What to install. (1) Plant-wide time synchronisation, from a single authoritative source (a GPS-disciplined master clock), distributed by NTP to everything and by PTP (IEEE 1588) where sub-millisecond accuracy is needed. Everything means everything: controllers, I/O, consoles, historian, the SIS, package PLCs, drives, analysers, electrical protection relays and the CCTV recorder. One unsynchronised subsystem re-creates the problem. (2) SOE-capable inputs with 1 ms hardware time-stamping at the module for the trip-critical signals — compressor trip contacts, valve limit switches, protective relay outputs — so that events are stamped when they physically occur, not when a server noticed. (3) First-out capture in the controller for each trip group (Page 8), so that the causal answer is latched at the moment it happens rather than reconstructed afterwards. (4) A contractual and specification change: every package unit must expose its events with synchronised timestamps and support the plant's time protocol. This is a purchasing clause, and it is the only way to fix it, because it cannot be retrofitted to a skid that has already been built. (5) A single, merged event view in the historian so that an investigator sees one ordered list across all systems. The wider point: time synchronisation is cheap, boring, and invisible until an incident, at which moment it becomes the difference between an investigation that finds a cause and one that produces a plausible story. It belongs in the design basis, and the way to justify it is precisely this incident — the cost of the investigation that could not conclude.
P6. Compare the engineering effort for a change of instrument range across 40 transmitters in (a) a class-based DCS with a maintained library, and (b) a PLC + SCADA system with tags defined in three tools. Estimate the ratio and identify where the error risk sits.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) Class-based DCS. The range is a parameter of the analogue-input instance, and it appears once. The work is: raise the change record; edit 40 parameter values (or, better, re-import the corrected rows from the instrument index, which is a bulk operation); the faceplate scales, the trend scales, the alarm limits (if defined as percentages of span) and the historian's compression deviation all follow automatically because they are derived from the same parameter; test a sample; update documentation, which is largely generated. Realistically 4–8 hours including the paperwork, and the risk is concentrated in one place that is easy to review. (b) PLC + SCADA with three tool-chains. The range appears in the PLC's scaling block, again in the SCADA tag definition, again in the faceplate or display object, again in the alarm configuration, again in the historian's tag setup, and possibly again in a report template and an OPC alias. Each of the 40 instruments must be edited in each place, by different people using different tools, with no mechanism to check consistency. Realistically 30–60 hours, plus testing, plus the near-certainty of finding a missed instance months later. The ratio is roughly 5–10× for this size of change, and it grows with the number of instruments and with the number of tools involved. Where the error risk sits — and this matters more than the hours. In (a) the risk is a single mistyped parameter, which is visible, testable and reviewable in one list. In (b) the risk is partial application: the PLC scaled correctly and the SCADA not, so the operator sees a wrong number while the control is right, or the alarm limits still refer to the old span so a high alarm now fires at the wrong process value. This class of defect is silent — nothing fails, everything runs, and the plant is quietly operating on inconsistent information until an incident or an audit exposes it. It is also very hard to find by testing, because you must test every consumer of every tag. The generalisable lesson: the cost of a duplicated definition is not paid at build time, when it is one extra entry; it is paid on every change for twenty-five years, and it is paid in silent inconsistency rather than in visible failure. That is the argument for the single database, and it is best made with exactly this kind of concrete, boring example rather than with architecture diagrams.
P7. A plant proposes to virtualise all sixteen operator stations and six servers onto two hypervisor hosts with shared storage. Identify the new common-cause exposures and specify the mitigations.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Virtualisation is the right move — and it converts twenty-two independent failures into a handful of shared ones, which must be engineered deliberately. New common-cause exposures. (1) Shared storage — the single largest one. A storage array, controller or SAN-fabric failure takes every console and every server simultaneously; the plant loses its entire operator interface at once, which no arrangement of sixteen physical PCs could ever have done. (2) Hypervisor host failure or resource exhaustion — with two hosts, one failure means all workloads must run on the survivor, so each host must be sized to carry everything, not half. (3) Shared network for storage and management traffic. (4) Shared power and cooling if both hosts sit in one rack or on one feed. (5) Common software — a hypervisor patch, a driver, a licence-server expiry or a snapshot-consolidation error applied to both hosts at once. (6) Shared credentials and one administrator: the human common cause again. (7) Time — virtual machine clocks drift differently from physical ones and must be properly disciplined (Page 8). Mitigations to specify. (a) Redundant storage with no single controller or path, or better, hyper-converged/replicated storage so that a whole storage node can be lost; and physically separated storage networking. (b) At least three hosts, or two hosts each sized for 100 % of the load, with automatic restart of workloads on the survivor and tested failover. (c) Separate power feeds, separate racks, and ideally separate rooms for the two hosts — this is the cheapest β reduction available and it is routinely skipped. (d) At least one or two physical, non-virtualised operator stations capable of running the plant, on independent power and storage. This is the critical mitigation and it is the one most often argued away on cost: the whole point is that the plant must remain operable when the virtual estate is down. (e) Staged patching — never both hosts at once, with a proven rollback and a snapshot before every change. (f) Separate administrator credentials from the DCS engineering credentials, with change control on the virtual infrastructure equal to that on the control system. (g) Tested restore of the whole environment, timed, annually (Page 5) — the great advantage of virtualisation is that this becomes practical, so actually do it. (h) Vendor support: confirm the DCS vendor supports the exact hypervisor version for the exact product version, in writing, or you will discover the exception during an incident. The framing to use with management: virtualisation genuinely improves availability against the failure mode that dominates today — ageing individual PCs — while introducing a new, smaller set of failures that are correlated and therefore severe. The design task is to make those few failures very unlikely and always survivable, and the physical console is the last line of that argument.
P8. A LOPA assigns a risk reduction factor of 100 to a “high level closes the feed valve” interlock currently implemented in the DCS. What does IEC 61511 require, and what changes?
Tap to reveal ↓A: A risk reduction factor of 100 corresponds to SIL 2, and IEC 61511 does not permit that to be claimed for a BPCS function. The standard limits the risk reduction credited to the basic process control system as a protection layer to no more than about 10 — not because BPCS hardware is unreliable, but because the BPCS is changed frequently, online, by many people, under commercial pressure, without the systematic rigour applied to a safety system. Its dominant failure mode is systematic — configuration and human error — which no hardware calculation captures and which the plant has no way to bound. So the function as described is not a DCS interlock at all: it is a safety instrumented function, and it must be implemented as one. What changes, concretely. (1) It moves to the SIS — a certified logic solver, separate from the BPCS controller that operates the feed valve in normal service (Page 11). (2) Dedicated sensor and final element, or a documented justification for sharing: if the same level transmitter is used for control and for the trip, a single transmitter failure both creates the demand and defeats the protection, which is the textbook common-cause failure the standard exists to prevent. Usually this means a separate level switch or transmitter, and often a separate shut-off valve rather than the control valve. (3) A safety requirements specification defining the SIF, its SIL target, its safe state, its process safety time, its trip point and its reset philosophy. (4) SIL verification — PFDavg calculation for the whole loop (sensor + logic solver + final element), including β for common cause and the diagnostic coverage, demonstrating that the architecture meets SIL 2, plus the architectural constraints on hardware fault tolerance. (5) A proof-test interval and procedure, derived from that calculation, with records — because the calculated PFD is only true if the proof testing actually happens at the assumed interval. (6) Separate access control and change management, with an audit trail. (7) Bypass management under a stricter regime than a BPCS interlock. (8) Lifecycle documentation through to decommissioning, and a functional safety assessment. What to do about the existing DCS interlock: keep it. It remains a useful control-layer protection and will prevent the great majority of demands from ever reaching the SIF — but it must be credited at no more than RRF 10 in the LOPA, and the SIF must be independent of it. The wider lesson for the architect: the LOPA is where the architecture is really decided. Any protection layer credited with more than a factor of ten has just been specified out of the DCS, and if that is discovered late — after the I/O is bought and the cabinets are built — it is expensive. Get the LOPA and the safety requirements specification done in FEED, before the architecture is frozen.
P9. A historian is configured with a single global compression deviation of 1 % of span. Give three specific ways this causes harm, and specify a better policy.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Harm 1 — it destroys the data on precisely the tags that matter most. On a well-controlled loop the process variable may only move a fraction of a percent of span in normal operation, so a 1 % deadband stores almost nothing: the trend becomes a flat line and the loop appears perfectly controlled. Every subsequent use — tuning assessment, loop performance monitoring, variability reduction (A58's benefits calculation depends on measuring σ), model identification — is impossible, and the plant concludes its control is excellent because it deleted the evidence. On a 0–300 °C range, 1 % is 3 °C, which is larger than the entire control band of most temperature loops. Harm 2 — it hides short excursions. A 20-second spike that trips a downstream unit or spoils a batch can fall entirely inside the compression corridor and never be written. The investigation then finds nothing in the history, concludes the process was normal, and looks in the wrong place. This is the failure that is discovered too late and cannot be undone. Harm 3 — it is simultaneously too tight for the slow tags. A tank level that moves over hours, an ambient temperature, a run-hour counter: on these, 1 % still writes far more than necessary, wasting the storage that was supposedly being saved. A single global setting manages to be too coarse and too fine at once, which is the signature of a setting chosen for convenience rather than engineering. A better policy. (1) Set the exception and compression deviations per tag, derived from two things: the instrument's accuracy and the smallest change that would ever be operationally significant. A good rule is exception deviation ≈ the instrument's repeatability (often 0.05–0.1 % of span), compression deviation ≈ 2–3× that — never the other way round. (2) Classify tags into a few tiers so this is administrable at 20 000 tags: critical (control PVs and MVs, model inputs, batch-relevant, quality) — minimal or no compression; standard — modest; slow/monitoring — generous. Apply the tier from the device class (Page 6) so new instruments inherit the right setting automatically. (3) No compression at all on SOE, alarms, batch records, custody transfer, regulatory data and anything feeding a model (WE4). (4) A maximum-time rule — write at least every few minutes regardless — so “unchanged” is distinguishable from “dead collector”. (5) Size the storage for the result and accept it: the difference between an aggressive and a generous policy on a 20 000-tag plant is a handful of terabytes over five years, which is trivial against the value of one successful investigation. (6) Review it whenever an investigation finds the data was not there — that event is the feedback loop, and most plants never close it. The principle: compression settings are an irreversible decision about the plant's memory. Make them deliberately, per tag, and err generous.
P10 — Synthesis. Design the control architecture for a new 4 500-I/O specialty chemicals plant: two reaction trains with full ISA-88 batch, a continuous solvent-recovery section, six package units from four vendors, a SIS with eleven SIFs (highest SIL 2), a 25-year design life, one control room, and a site in Sri Lanka with limited local vendor presence. State your architecture and justify every major decision.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
1. Platform: a DCS, and the reasoning is not size but batch and lifecycle. 4 500 I/O is within PLC+SCADA range, so the decision rests elsewhere. Full ISA-88 batch across two trains with recipe management, electronic batch records and phase arbitration is exactly what a DCS batch engine does natively and what a SCADA package does badly; the continuous solvent-recovery section wants proper regulatory control with cascades and possibly APC; and a 25-year life with one control room and one operator crew argues strongly for one integrated database, one alarm system and one operator picture. A PLC+SCADA solution would be cheaper in capital and would cost far more in engineering, integration and twenty-five years of dual maintenance (WE-style reasoning from Page 1). 2. Vendor selection is dominated by local support. With limited in-country presence, MTTR is the availability lever that matters (Page 5, WE2): a vendor whose engineer arrives in 4 hours beats one whose engineer arrives in 4 days by more than any redundancy topology can compensate for. I would weight local presence, spares holding in-country, and trained local engineers very heavily in the evaluation, ahead of several functional line items, and I would negotiate a contractual response time and an on-site spares kit as part of the purchase. Where local support is thin, invest in in-house capability: two engineers trained to configure and restore the system, a full offline engineering environment, and a tested restore procedure. 3. Controllers and partitioning. Partition on process boundaries: one redundant controller pair per reaction train (each carrying its own phase logic and interlocks so a train can run independently), one pair for solvent recovery, one pair for utilities and off-sites. Size each to ≤ 50 % at handover to leave room for failover, upsets and twenty-five years of additions (Page 3, WE1). Keep cross-controller interlocks to a minimum and never let a fast interlock cross a controller boundary. 4. I/O: electronic marshalling. With six package units and a specialty-chemicals process whose I/O list will still be changing late in the project, single-channel characterisation removes the schedule dependency on freezing the I/O list and turns late changes into module swaps rather than re-terminations (Page 3). Specify 20 % spare channels, 20 % spare cabinet space and 20 % spare controller capacity — all three. 5. Field level. 4–20 mA with HART as the baseline, with HART-capable I/O on every analogue channel and the asset-management package actually configured and owned from day one (Page 12) — on a plant with limited local support, device diagnostics that let you plan a failure rather than react to it are worth disproportionately more. Consider Ethernet-APL for any new-build segments if the vendor's offering is mature at the time of order. NAMUR NE 43 out-of-range detection on every AI, and NE 107 diagnostics routed to maintenance, never to the operator. 6. Networks. Redundant control network with two physically separate paths on separate routes; no shared network with CCTV, business systems or anything else (Page 4); managed switches under configuration control; and a documented IEC 62443 zone-and-conduit design with a DMZ at the Level 3 boundary (A54). 7. Safety: interfaced or integrated-but-separate, with independence evidenced. Eleven SIFs to SIL 2 justify a certified SIS on the same platform family for one operator picture and a common time base, provided separate logic solvers, separate I/O, separate power, enforced write restriction, separate credentials with a key switch, and network zoning are all demonstrated on the actual proposed system (Page 11). Separate sensors and final elements where the SIL verification requires them. Get the LOPA and the safety requirements specification done in FEED, before the architecture is frozen, because any protection layer credited above RRF 10 lands in the SIS and changes the I/O count and the cabinets. 8. Package units: specify MTP. Six units from four vendors is the exact case for MTP (Page 14). Put a conforming MTP file, with defined services, HMI, alarms and an OPC UA server, into every package purchase specification, with import-and-demonstrate as an acceptance criterion. Where a vendor cannot comply, price the conventional integration on their side. Also require synchronised time and event stamping from every package unit — a purchasing clause, because it cannot be retrofitted (P5). 9. Batch architecture. Phase logic in the controllers, recipe and procedure execution on a redundant batch server, electronic batch records to the historian with store-and-forward (Page 10). Test the three failover cases at FAT, including restarting the batch server mid-phase. Design the resource arbitration for shared services — solvent transfer, CIP, vacuum — with atomic allocation and a fixed global acquisition order (A60 Page 13). 10. Operator interface. ISA-101 lifecycle: philosophy, style guide and toolkit before any graphic is drawn; high-performance graphics with a four-level hierarchy; console design to ISO 11064; alarm philosophy and rationalisation to ISA-18.2 during the project, not after (A53). One control room, with the console loading assessed on the upset case — two batch trains plus a continuous section plus six package units is a lot for one operator when several go abnormal together, and that analysis should be documented and may well justify two consoles. 11. Data layer. Historian with per-tag compression tiers set from the device class, no compression on batch-critical, SOE and quality data, store-and-forward at every collector, contextualised to the ISA-95 equipment hierarchy so batch comparison is a query (Page 9). Plant-wide time synchronisation from a GPS master, PTP where SOE needs it. 12. Lifecycle. Virtualise the Level 2 layer from day one with three hosts, replicated storage, separated power and two physical consoles that work when the virtual estate does not (P7). Budget the FAT properly — failure and failover testing, alarm-flood testing, a timed backup-restore onto spare hardware, and the operators present. Protect loop checking in the schedule. Establish the class library in the first six weeks, with a named owner and version control, and enforce it through commissioning with a conformance check (Page 6). 13. The two decisions I would fight hardest for: the local-support weighting in vendor selection, and the class library being built before the instances. Both are cheap, both are invisible in the capital cost, and both determine what this plant costs to own for the next twenty-five years.

Derivations & Traps

Four results to reproduce from memory, and the seven mistakes that recur on every project.

DERIVATION 1 · WHY COMMON CAUSE DEFEATS REDUNDANCY
Setup. Two identical channels, each with unavailability U. A fraction β of each channel's failures are common-cause — they take both channels at once. Split the failure rate accordingly: independent part of each channel: (1 − β)U common-cause part (shared): βU System unavailability. The pair is down if BOTH independent parts have failed, OR if a common-cause failure has occurred: Uₛₖₛ = [(1 − β)U]² + βU (usual first-order form: Uₛₖₛ ≈ (1 − β)U² + βU) Which term dominates? Take their ratio: βU / [(1 − β)U²] = β / [(1 − β)U] Numbers. With β = 0.05 and U = 10⁻⁴: ratio = 0.05 / (0.95 × 10⁻⁴) = 526 The common-cause term is 526 times the independent term. The U² term — the entire mathematical case for redundancy — is numerically irrelevant. Crossover. The terms are equal when (1−β)U² = βU, i.e. U = β / (1 − β) β = 5 % ⇒ U = 0.0526 i.e. only when each channel is unavailable more than 5 % of the time. No component in a control system is anywhere near that. The floor. As U → 0, Uₛₖₛ → βU, so redundancy cannot reduce unavailability below β × U, no matter how many channels you add. Consequences for design — all four are non-electronic: • separate power feeds, cabinets, cable routes, cooling (physical β) • diverse firmware versions and sensing principles (systematic β) • separate credentials, staged changes, two-person rule (human β) • diagnostics, so a failed standby announces itself (hidden β)
DERIVATION 2 · THE SERIES CHAIN AND THE WEAKEST LINK
Setup. A control loop is a chain of n elements, all of which must work: transmitter, barrier, I/O, controller, network, valve. If failures are independent, the availabilities multiply: Aₜₒₜ = ∏ Aᵢ = ∏ (1 − Uᵢ) Expand for small U. ∏(1 − Uᵢ) = 1 − ΣUᵢ + ΣUᵢU₣ − ... With every Uᵢ of order 10⁻⁴ or smaller, the cross terms are of order 10⁻⁸ and negligible, so Uₜₒₜ ≈ Σ Uᵢ unavailabilities simply ADD The sensitivity result. ∂Uₜₒₜ / ∂Uᵢ = 1 for every i. A one-hour-per-year improvement is worth exactly the same wherever in the chain you make it — so effort should go to the element with the LARGEST Uᵢ, because that is where a given percentage improvement buys the most absolute hours. Improving a term that is 2 % of the total can never deliver more than 2 %. Applying it to WE2 valve 1.712 × 10⁻⁴ 84.8 % of the total transmitter 1.142 × 10⁻⁵ barrier, I/O 7.61 × 10⁻⁶ each controller pair 3.05 × 10⁻⁶ 1.5 % network pair 9.14 × 10⁻⁷ Perfect controller → saves 0.03 h/yr Valve MTTR 12 → 6 h → saves 0.75 h/yr (25× more) And since U = MTTR/(MTBF + MTTR) ≈ MTTR/MTBF, the two levers are symmetric: halving MTTR and doubling MTBF give identical results. MTTR is usually the cheaper one — spares, skills, access and procedures are operational choices; MTBF is bought with the equipment.
DERIVATION 3 · THE TWO-WIRE SEGMENT LIMIT
Setup. n devices each drawing Iᴴ sit on a trunk of length L with a loop resistance R per unit length. Supply voltage Vₛ; the furthest device needs at least Vᵐᵢₙ to operate, plus a design margin m. Ohm's law along the trunk. Worst case, treat all devices as if at the far end (conservative, and correct for the last device): V_drop = Iₜₒₜ · R · L = n · Iᴴ · R · L V_end = Vₛ − n · Iᴴ · R · L Requirement V_end ≥ Vᵐᵢₙ (1 + m) Solve for the device limit nᵐₐₓ = [ Vₛ − Vᵐᵢₙ(1 + m) ] / ( Iᴴ · R · L ) Temperature. Copper's resistivity rises at α ≈ 0.00393 /°C: R(T) = R₂₀ [ 1 + α(T − 20) ] at 60 °C: R = 44 [1 + 0.00393 × 40] = 50.9 Ω/km (+15.7 %) A 16 % rise in R consumes most of a 20 % margin on its own — which is why the calculation MUST be done at the worst-case installed temperature. Segments commissioned in the cool season and failing months later are a standard field failure with a standard cause. Worked check (WE3): Vₛ = 24, Vᵐᵢₙ = 9, m = 0.20, Iᴴ = 20 mA, R = 44 Ω/km, L = 1.5 km: nᵐₐₓ = (24 − 10.8) / (0.020 × 44 × 1.5) = 13.2 / 1.32 = 10 Remember the other three constraints — power conditioner rating, intrinsic-safety entity parameters, and protocol/macrocycle limits. Quote the SMALLEST of the four, not the voltage one.
DERIVATION 4 · CONTROLLER LOADING AND THE HEADROOM RULE
Setup. Modules of type k: nₖ instances, execution period Tₖ, CPU cost cₖ per execution. Over one second each instance executes 1/Tₖ times. L = Σₖ nₖ cₖ / Tₖ + Lᵢᴼ + Lₓₒₑₑₛ + Lₒₖₑₖₜₑₔₑₜₒ Sensitivity to the period — the key result. ∂Lₖ / ∂Tₖ = − nₖ cₖ / Tₖ² The load varies as 1/T, so HALVING a task period DOUBLES its load contribution, and moving a group from 2 s to 100 ms multiplies it by 20. Compare with adding modules, where load is merely linear in n. Period dominates count. 400 monitoring modules at 2 s = 5.0 % of CPU the same 400 at 100 ms = 100 % of CPU the difference = 95 percentage points The headroom rule. Let L be the steady-state load. During a redundancy failover a single CPU carries the full load plus re-synchronisation work, and a plant upset adds event and communication traffic. Writing those as multipliers: Lᶠᶜᶟᶜᶜ ≈ L (1 + fᶜᴴᵢₗₒᵛᵉᵣ + fₖₛᴸᴸᵗ) + Lᶜᵛᴴₙᵌᵉ With plausible values (f ≈ 0.15 each and a few points for online change), a steady-state L of 0.65 reaches ≈ 0.95 in the worst minute of the plant's year. ⇒ design to L ≤ 0.60–0.70, and to 0.50 at handover on a plant expected to grow. The vendor's limit is where the controller STOPS; the design limit is where it keeps working through everything the plant will do over 25 years.
SEVEN TRAPS
  • 1“Distributed” is not the defining feature. A network of PLCs is distributed. What defines a DCS is one engineering database and one namespace — the thing that cannot be retrofitted and the only thing that justifies the price. Evaluate on engineering hours and change propagation, not on controller specifications.
  • 2An availability figure with no boundary is meaningless. “Six nines” usually means the CPU pair, with β = 0, excluding field devices, software, configuration error and planned maintenance. Always ask what is inside the boundary, what β was assumed, and what the MTTR was — then model the whole loop, where the valve will dominate.
  • 3Redundancy quoted without β. Two channels sharing a power feed, a cabinet, a cable duct, a firmware version or an engineer are not two channels. Redundancy cannot get below β × U, and β is reduced by separation and governance, not by buying more hardware.
  • 4Purdue treated as a network diagram or a security architecture. It is a functional model. Segmentation follows IEC 62443 zones and conduits and must follow risk; “Level 3.5” is a conduit, not a level. The durable content of the model is the timescale separation: control must never wait on a system that thinks in shifts.
  • 5Compression settings are irreversible. The discarded samples do not exist anywhere. A single global deviation is simultaneously too coarse for control tags and too fine for slow ones. Set them per tag, from the class, and err generous — disk is cheap, a lost transient is gone.
  • 6Alarms routed by source instead of audience. Device diagnostics in the operator's alarm list teach the operator that alarms need no action — and that lesson generalises to the alarms that do. Classify to NE 107, give maintenance a real destination, and show device health on the faceplate rather than as an event.
  • 7Time synchronisation left until last. It is cheap, boring, invisible — and the day you need it, it is too late, because the events have already happened. Synchronise everything including package units and the SIS, stamp at source, and put SOE-class stamping on trip-critical inputs before the first trip, not after.

Two honourable mentions. Selecting on hardware price optimises perhaps a fifth of the capital and a much smaller fraction of the twenty-five-year cost, while ignoring engineering effort, migration path, security posture and local support — the four things that actually decide what the system costs to own. And letting the class erode during commissioning: instance-level edits made under schedule pressure destroy the propagation guarantee, after which the library freezes and the system's main benefit is forfeited while still being paid for.
