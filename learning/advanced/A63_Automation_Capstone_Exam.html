How This Capstone Works

Every other module in this track taught one subject. This one asks whether you can hold eleven of them in your head at the same time, on one plant, under time pressure — which is the only condition under which engineering is ever actually done.

WHY A CAPSTONE AT ALL

Subject knowledge is stored by subject. Engineering problems arrive by plant.

You can score full marks on a PID tuning exercise, full marks on a valve sizing exercise, and still fail to notice that the valve you sized is the reason the loop cannot be tuned. That failure is not a knowledge gap — it is an integration gap, and only an integrated assessment exposes it. This paper is built so that the answer to one section changes the correct answer to another: the dead time you identify in Section B is caused by the valve you assess in Section D; the interlock you specify in Section E is the layer you are forbidden to double-count in Section F; the network you draw in Section H determines the scan period that limits Section E.

Treat it as a real paper. Sit it once cold, mark yourself honestly against the scheme, then use the ranked weakness list the marking demo produces to decide what to re-read. Then sit the second paper on page 17 two weeks later.

Tap to reveal the examiner's reasoning ↓ Because automation competence is not compensatory in practice. The Plant — Scenario Brief

THE BRIEF

Ceylon Spice & Foods (Pvt) Ltd, Line 3 — a dehydrated vegetable and spice powder line near Kurunegala, Sri Lanka, producing 3 200 t/yr of finished powder on two shifts, 320 days a year.

Lines 1 and 2 were rebuilt four years ago onto a modern PLC with a small SCADA. Line 3 was not. It still runs on a 22-year-old panel: standalone single-loop temperature controllers, relay interlocks, a strip-chart recorder that nobody replaces the paper in, and a hardwired lamp box. The board has approved a retrofit and you are the lead automation engineer. The plant manager wants three things, in this order: consistent product moisture, no repeat of the two over-temperature near-misses, and a lower electricity bill.

You have been given the process description, an extract of the instrument index, a step test performed by the maintenance team last month, one month of alarm data from the Line 1–2 SCADA (Line 3 has no alarm history because it has no alarm system), and the site electrical single-line summary. Nothing else. Where data is missing you must state the assumption you make — marks are available for stating assumptions and lost for inventing data silently.

Q2 · Before answering a single technical question: what are the three most consequential facts on this page, and why?
Tap to reveal ↓ (i) The controller output is a square wave while the PV is triangular (symptom 1). That is the classic signature of valve stiction, not of bad tuning. It means the correct first action is mechanical, and it makes every "retune the loop" answer in this paper worth fewer marks than the candidate expects.

(ii) TSH-309 is wired normally open and its proof test history is unknown. A protection contact that closes on demand is energise-to-trip: a broken wire, a loose terminal or a failed switch is silently unrevealed. Combined with an unknown test interval, no risk reduction can honestly be claimed for it in Section F.

(iii) The blancher and the dryer share one steam header, and the header sags 1.8 bar (symptom 3). That converts a "dryer control problem" into a plant-wide utility interaction — a measurable disturbance with a known origin, which is the textbook precondition for feedforward, and something no amount of feedback tuning can fully remove.

Each of these is a structural fact. Structural facts change which technique is correct; parameter facts only change the numbers you put into it.

Section A · Measurement & Field Devices [15 marks]

Eighteen minutes. Nothing downstream of a measurement can be better than the measurement, and three of the four field devices on this line are quietly lying to you.

THE LOOP UNDER EXAMINATION

One temperature loop, one shared header, one protection switch. Every question in Section A is about something on this drawing not being what it appears to be.

QUESTION A1  [5 marks]

TT-301 is a Pt100 in a thermowell whose outer surface is heavily coated with product fines. (a) Explain the effect of the coating on the dynamic behaviour of the measurement, quantifying it as far as the data allows. (b) State how you would confirm your explanation without shutting the plant down. (c) State the effect on the tuning you will later calculate.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ (a) [2] The coating adds a thermal resistance in series with the wall and increases the thermal mass the process must heat. The sensor therefore behaves as an additional first-order lag on the measurement, with time constant τs ≈ (m·cp)/(hA); a fouled well commonly gives 30–90 s where a clean one gives 5–15 s. Crucially, this lag is inside the control loop — the controller cannot distinguish sensor lag from process lag, so the "process" identified from the step test already contains it. Part of the τ = 180 s and possibly some of the θ = 40 s belongs to the sensor, not to the dryer. [1 for the lag mechanism, 1 for it being indistinguishable and inside the loop]

(b) [2] Two on-line tests: (i) compare against a second, independent measurement — a portable thermocouple or an infrared reading of the discharge stream during a known transient; a fouled sensor will lag it visibly. (ii) Step test the sensor alone is not possible on-line, so instead look at the shape of the recorded PV during a fast disturbance (a feed interruption): a heavily lagged sensor rounds off a step that the process actually made sharply. A third acceptable answer: pull and inspect the well at the next planned stop, and record the as-found coating thickness. [1 per valid method, max 2]

(c) [1] Because the sensor lag is included in the identified model, the tuning derived from that model is consistent and the loop will behave as predicted — but the achievable performance is worse than the dryer deserves. If the well is later cleaned, τ and θ both fall and the controller becomes over-tuned, so retune after any sensor maintenance. That statement alone earns the mark.

Examiner's note: the most common wrong answer is "it causes an offset". A coating does not shift a steady-state temperature reading appreciably once thermal equilibrium is reached — it destroys the dynamics, and dynamics are what control lives on.
QUESTION A2  [5 marks]

FT-304 is an orifice plate with a DP transmitter, ranged 0–2 500 kg/h, with the square root extracted in the transmitter. Operations report it is useless below about 25% of span. (a) Show quantitatively why. (b) State the practical turndown of the installation. (c) Recommend a fix, with one alternative.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ (a) [3] For an orifice, Δp ∝ Q², so Q = k√Δp. Differentiating: dQ/Q = ½·d(Δp)/Δp. The transmitter's uncertainty is a fixed fraction of its span, not of the reading — take ±0.5% of span. At 25% of full flow the DP is (0.25)² = 6.25% of full DP, so the relative DP error is 0.005/0.0625 = 8%, and the flow error is half of that: ±4% of reading. At 10% flow, DP is 1% of span, DP error is 50%, flow error ±25%. The square-root law compresses the whole low end of the range into the bottom few percent of the DP cell. [1 for the square law, 1 for the halving relation, 1 for a correct numerical example]

(b) [1] With ±2% of reading as the usable limit, the flow must stay above Δp = (0.005/0.04) = 12.5% of DP span, i.e. Q ≥ √0.125 = 35% of range → a practical turndown of about 3:1. Classic orifice turndown; nothing is wrong with the instrument.

(c) [1] Best fix: re-range the DP cell to the actual operating flow (a 0–1 200 kg/h range instantly restores resolution), or fit a modern multivariable DP transmitter with a wider rangeable cell. Alternatives, each acceptable: a vortex meter (10:1 turndown, no impulse lines), a stacked-DP arrangement (two cells, low and high), or a Coriolis meter if the budget exists. Deduct for "replace the orifice with a bigger one" — that makes it worse.

Examiner's note: the elegant part of this answer is dQ/Q = ½ dΔp/Δp. It also explains why square-root extraction should never be done twice (in transmitter and in the DCS block) — a mistake that produces a beautifully plausible flow reading that is wrong everywhere except at 100%.
QUESTION A3  [5 marks]

The plant manager asks you to "put moisture on automatic control using MT-305". (a) Explain, with reference to the data given, why closing a feedback loop directly on MT-305 will not work. (b) Propose a workable architecture. (c) State the one calibration action you would take before anything else, and why.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ (a) [2] MT-305 is a laboratory oven method: one sample per hour with a two-hour result delay. As a feedback measurement it presents θ ≈ 7 200 s of dead time with a 3 600 s sample interval, against a process τ of 180 s. The controllability ratio θ/τ is of the order of 40 — a regime in which feedback can achieve almost nothing: any controller fast enough to matter would be acting on information about a product that was made two hours ago and has already been packed. Further, sampling theory forbids it: a disturbance with a period shorter than 2 h cannot even be observed, let alone corrected. [1 for quantifying the delay against τ, 1 for the sampling/observability point]

(b) [2] Use moisture as a slow outer trim, not a controller: an inferential (soft-sensor) estimate of moisture from the variables that are measured fast — dryer outlet temperature, feed rate (WT-306), and ambient/inlet humidity — controls the fast loop, while each lab result is used to bias the inference. In control terms: TIC-301 runs as it always did; a supervisory block adjusts its setpoint once per lab result, with a heavily damped or dead-band-limited update (e.g. move no more than 0.5 °C per result). This is the standard cascade of a fast inferential inner loop under a slow analytical outer correction. [1 for the inferential estimate from fast variables, 1 for the lab result correcting the estimate rather than driving the valve]

(c) [1] Calibrate WT-306, the feed belt weigher, first. Feed rate is the dominant input to any moisture inference and to any energy balance; an uncalibrated weigher makes every downstream model wrong in a way that looks like process variation. It is also the cheapest action on the list. (Accept: verify TT-301 against a reference first — but the weigher is the better answer because it has never been calibrated.)

Examiner's note: full marks require noticing that the useful answer is architectural, not algorithmic. Candidates who propose "a PID with a long integral time on MT-305" have not internalised what dead time does.

Section B · Loop Dynamics, Identification & Tuning [20 marks]

Twenty-four minutes. The step test on page 2 is all you are given, and it is enough — provided you also know what it is telling you about the valve.

THE SUPPLIED STEP RESPONSE

Two points on one curve give the whole model. The 28.3% point exists because 1−e−1/3 = 0.283 — it is the one-third-time-constant point, chosen so that the two equations solve cleanly.

QUESTION B1  [6 marks]

From the step test data, identify a first-order-plus-dead-time model of the dryer temperature loop. State the process gain with units, the time constant and the dead time, show the method, and classify the controllability of the process.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ Gain [2]  K = ΔPV/ΔMV = (124.5 − 118.0)/(50 − 40) = 6.5/10 = 0.65 °C per % valve. Units are compulsory — a bare "0.65" scores 1.

Time constant and dead time [3] Two-point (Smith) identification:
τ = 1.5(t₆₃ − t₂₈) = 1.5(220 − 100) = 180 s
θ = t₆₃ − τ = 220 − 180 = 40 s
Cross-check: the first visible movement was reported at t ≈ 40 s, which agrees with θ = 40 s. That agreement is worth stating — identification without a cross-check is a guess with decimals. [2 for the two values, 1 for the cross-check or for correctly quoting the method]

Controllability [1] θ/τ = 40/180 = 0.22 — a lag-dominant, easily controllable process (θ/τ < 0.5). Feedback will work well here; the problems on this loop are therefore not caused by difficult dynamics.

Examiner's note: the last sentence is the one that matters. Having established that the process is easy, the candidate should already suspect that the reported oscillation has a mechanical cause. Section B is quietly asking you to rule the process out.
QUESTION B2  [6 marks]

Using SIMC (Skogestad) rules, calculate PI settings for λ = θ and for λ = 3θ. Express the gain both as %/°C and as a proportional band, given that TT-301 is ranged 0–200 °C. Recommend which you would commission on this plant today, and say why.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ SIMC PI [2]  Kc = τ / [K(λ + θ)]   Ti = min(τ, 4(λ+θ))

λ = θ = 40 s (tight) [1]
Kc = 180 / (0.65 × 80) = 3.46 %/°C   Ti = min(180, 320) = 180 s
λ = 3θ = 120 s (robust) [1]
Kc = 180 / (0.65 × 160) = 1.73 %/°C   Ti = min(180, 640) = 180 s
Note Ti = τ in both cases: the min() is not active, so only the gain changes with λ. Saying that earns credit.

Proportional band [1] Convert to a dimensionless gain first — the controller sees % of span, not °C:
Kc(dimensionless) = 3.46 %/°C × 200 °C/100% = 6.92 %/% → PB = 100/6.92 = 14.4%
For λ = 3θ: 3.46 %/% → PB = 28.9%
Getting this wrong by a factor of 2 (forgetting the 200 °C span) is the single most common numerical error in the whole paper.

Recommendation [2] Commission the λ = 3θ (robust) settings today, and only move towards λ = θ after the valve has been repaired. Reasons: (i) the valve is sticking, and a higher controller gain against a sticking valve produces a larger and faster limit cycle, not a better response; (ii) the shared steam header means the true process gain varies with header pressure, so the model is uncertain and a robust setting protects against that; (iii) the plant's stated objective is consistency, not speed. [1 for the choice, 1 for a valid reason tied to this plant]

Examiner's note: for reference, Ziegler–Nichols on this same model gives Kc ≈ 5.3 %/°C and Ti ≈ 123 s — roughly 3× the robust SIMC gain with a shorter reset. On a plant with a sticking valve and a variable steam supply, that is an invitation to oscillate. Quoting ZN as a comparison earns credit; proposing it as the answer does not.
QUESTION B3  [4 marks]

The loop currently cycles with a period of 9 minutes; the PV is close to triangular and the controller output is close to square. Using your identified model, prove that this is not a tuning problem, and name the true cause and the test that confirms it.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ The proof [2] A tuning-induced (linear) oscillation occurs at or near the loop's ultimate frequency. For FOPDT, ωu solves ωθ + arctan(ωτ) = π. With θ = 40 s and τ = 180 s this gives ωu = 0.0425 rad/s, so the ultimate period is
Pu = 2π/ωu = 148 s ≈ 2.5 minutes (and Ku ≈ 11.9 %/°C).
An integral-dominant detuned loop cycles a little slower than Pu, typically up to ~1.5×. The observed period is 540 s = 3.65 × Pu — far outside anything the linear loop can produce at any PI setting. Therefore the oscillation is not linear instability. [1 for computing Pu, 1 for the comparison and conclusion]

The cause [1] Valve stiction on TV-301. The waveform confirms it: a stick–slip valve makes the controller output ramp (integral action winding up against a stuck stem) then jump — a square/rectangular MV — while the process integrates each jump into a triangular PV. Independent corroboration in the brief: the valve has no positioner, and its packing has been replaced twice this year (over-tightened packing is a leading cause of stiction).

The test [1] A valve travel (step) test in manual: with the controller in manual, make successively smaller output steps (5%, 2%, 1%, 0.5%) and record actual stem position from the positioner feedback or a dial indicator. The smallest step that produces movement is the stickband. Equivalently, plot MV against PV (or against stem position) over one cycle — stiction draws a parallelogram, tuning draws an ellipse.

Examiner's note: a candidate who writes only "it's stiction because the output is square" earns 2 of 4. The mark scheme rewards quantitative exclusion of the alternative — that is the difference between an opinion and a diagnosis.
QUESTION B4  [4 marks]

A previous engineer added a 30-second first-order filter to the TT-301 signal to "stop the noise". Quantify what that filter costs the loop at the closed-loop bandwidth implied by your λ = θ design, and state what you would do instead.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ The cost [3] A SIMC design with λ = θ targets a closed-loop response time of λ, so the gain-crossover frequency is approximately
ωgc ≈ 1/(λ + θ) = 1/80 = 0.0125 rad/s
A first-order filter contributes phase lag −arctan(ωτf):
−arctan(0.0125 × 30) = −arctan(0.375) = −20.6°
Against a typical phase-margin target of 60°, the filter alone consumes about one third of the entire stability budget — for comparison, the dead time itself costs ωθ = 0.0125 × 40 rad = 28.6°. Put another way, at low frequencies a filter behaves like added dead time of roughly τf, so 30 s of filter has increased the effective dead time from 40 s to about 70 s: a 75% increase, which by the SIMC formula reduces the achievable gain by nearly half. [1 for ωgc, 1 for the phase calculation, 1 for interpreting it against a margin or as added dead time]

What to do instead [1] In order: (i) find the noise source — on a Pt100 it is usually earthing, a shared cable route with the VFD, or a loose terminal, and none of those are cured by filtering; (ii) if filtering is still needed, size it as τf ≤ θ/10 → about 4–5 s here, which costs only 3.6°; (iii) put derivative action, if any, on the measurement with its own filter rather than filtering the whole PV.

Examiner's note: this question is included because the filter is almost always invisible during a tuning exercise — it lives in the transmitter or the AI block, not in the controller faceplate, and it silently converts a good design into a mediocre one.

Section C · Advanced Control Strategy [20 marks]

Twenty-four minutes. Single-loop PID has five structural limits. This plant is sitting on three of them at once, and the marks are for recognising which limit — because the limit chooses the strategy.

THE DECISION THE PAPER IS TESTING

Advanced control is not a ladder you climb; it is a menu you select from once you have named the limit. Selecting before naming is the most expensive habit in the discipline.

QUESTION C1  [7 marks]

The steam header sags from 8.0 to 6.2 barg whenever the blancher demands steam, and the dryer outlet falls 5–7 °C before TIC-301 recovers it. (a) Name the two strategies that address this, (b) recommend one, (c) identify the obstacle to your recommendation that is already documented in the instrument index, and (d) state the numerical check that must pass for your recommendation to be valid.

Tap for the model answer and mark scheme ↓ (a) The two candidates [2]
Cascade: TIC-301 no longer positions the valve; it sets the setpoint of a fast steam flow controller FIC-304, which positions the valve. A header pressure change alters the flow through a fixed valve opening; the inner flow loop sees that immediately and corrects it, so the disturbance is largely eliminated before the temperature ever moves. This is the textbook remedy because the disturbance enters through the manipulated path.
Feedforward from PT-303: measure the header pressure and push a correction straight to the valve.

(b) Recommendation [2] Cascade, because the disturbance is inside the manipulated variable's own path — that is precisely the case cascade was invented for, and cascade also corrects supply changes the feedforward model never anticipated (e.g. a partially closed isolation valve, condensate flooding, a fouled orifice). Feedforward is the fallback if the flow measurement cannot be made trustworthy, and the two are not mutually exclusive: cascade first, feedforward on top only if a measurable residue remains. [1 for the choice, 1 for the manipulated-path justification]

(c) The obstacle [2] FT-304 is unusable below about 25–35% of span (Section A2). A cascade inner loop whose measurement dies at low fire will fail exactly during start-up, low-rate operation and night shift — the periods when the header is least loaded and most stable, so the failure is not immediately obvious, and the loop will be left in a bad mode. The obstacle must therefore be removed first: re-range the DP transmitter to the real operating band (e.g. 0–1 200 kg/h) or replace it with a vortex/multivariable meter. Answering C1 without mentioning FT-304 caps the question at 5 marks — this is the integration test.

(d) The numerical check [1] Speed separation. The inner loop must be at least 3–10× faster than the outer. A steam flow loop has a closed-loop response time of a few seconds; taking τinner ≈ 5 s against an outer response time of order λ+θ = 80–160 s gives a separation of 16–32× — comfortably satisfied. State the ratio, not just the rule.

Examiner's note: the professional habit being examined is refusing to design on top of a broken measurement. Cascade multiplies the value of a good inner measurement and multiplies the damage of a bad one.
QUESTION C2  [5 marks]

THE PRESSURE PROFILE THAT DECIDES EVERYTHING

QUESTION I3  [4 marks]

It is invisible on a datasheet and unmistakable under a microscope.

WE1 · IDENTIFICATION, TUNING AND HOW WRONG YOU CAN AFFORD TO BE

Given: the step test of page 2. Find: the model, the SIMC PI settings, and the sensitivity of those settings to a misread of t63 by ±20 s.

Step 1 — gain K = ΔPV/ΔMV = (124.5 − 118.0)/10 = 0.65 °C/% Step 2 — two-point identification τ = 1.5(t₆₃ − t₂₈) = 1.5(220 − 100) = 180 s θ = t₆₃ − τ = 220 − 180 = 40 s cross-check: first movement reported ≈ 40 s ✓ Step 3 — controllability θ/τ = 40/180 = 0.22 → lag dominant, easy Step 4 — SIMC PI, λ = θ Kₜ = τ/[K(λ+θ)] = 180/(0.65 × 80) = 3.46 %/°C Tᵢ = min(τ, 4(λ+θ)) = min(180, 320) = 180 s Step 5 — proportional band on a 0–200 °C span Kₜ(dimensionless) = 3.46 × 200/100 = 6.92 %/% PB = 100/6.92 = 14.4 %

The sensitivity that matters. Suppose t63 was actually 240 s, not 220 s — a 9% misread, entirely plausible on a noisy trend:

ReadingτθKc at λ=θChange
t63 = 200 s150 s50 s2.31 %/°C−33%
t63 = 220 s180 s40 s3.46 %/°C—
t63 = 240 s210 s30 s5.38 %/°C+56%

A 9% error in one timestamp moves the recommended gain by more than half. The reason is structural: τ and θ move in opposite directions when t63 shifts, and Kc is proportional to τ and inversely proportional to θ, so the two errors compound rather than cancel. Two consequences follow. First, always do more than one step test, in both directions, and average the models. Second, this is why λ = 3θ is the safe commissioning choice — not because the process is difficult, but because the identification is uncertain, and a robust setting tolerates a model that is 50% wrong.

PROBLEM 7  [8 marks] · THE SEQUENCE THAT SKIPS A STEP

A ladder-implemented sequence occasionally executes three steps in a single scan, skipping the dwell in each. (a) Name the defect and its mechanism. (b) Give two fixes. (c) State the general rule.

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) The run-through (or "fall-through") bug. In ladder, rungs execute top to bottom within one scan. If the step-advance rungs are written in ascending order, then when step 1's transition becomes true, the rung sets step 2 — and the step-2 rung, which has not yet executed this scan, immediately sees itself active, evaluates its transition and sets step 3, and so on. The whole chain can collapse in a single scan. Written in descending order (highest step first), each rung has already executed before its step is set, so exactly one transition occurs per scan. The identical logic therefore behaves completely differently depending on rung order — which is why the bug is intermittent, appears only when transitions happen to be simultaneously true, and vanishes when you add a trace.
(b) Fixes: (i) write the step-advance rungs in descending order; (ii) use a one-scan latch or an edge/flag so that a step which has just been entered cannot advance in the same scan; (iii) better still, implement the sequence as an explicit CASE state machine in Structured Text, where only one branch executes per scan by construction; (iv) use SFC, whose execution model already enforces one transition per step per cycle.
(c) The general rule: in a scanned controller, execution order is part of the logic. Two functionally identical programs with different rung order are not the same program. This is also why "it worked in simulation" is not evidence — the simulator may resolve the order differently from the target.
PROBLEM 8  [8 marks] · ERROR PROPAGATING INTO AN INFERENCE

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) A fixed 6% high feed reading makes the inference believe more wet material is entering than actually is, so it over-estimates the required drying and biases its moisture estimate in one direction at every operating point. Worse, the error is proportional, not constant: it grows with rate, so the inference is wrong by a different amount at every throughput — which appears in the trend as a "rate-dependent process change" and invites someone to add gain scheduling to compensate for an instrument fault.
(b) The lab bias corrects the inference at the operating point where the sample was taken. Because the weigher error is proportional, the bias fitted at 3 t/h is wrong at 2 t/h and at 4 t/h. The correction therefore chases the rate: every rate change produces a fresh offset, the bias update never settles, and operators conclude that "the model does not work". A bias term can absorb an offset; it cannot absorb a gain error — that is the general principle worth stating.
(c) Correct order: calibrate the instruments → verify with an independent mass balance over a shift → collect data across the full operating range → fit the inference → validate on data it has never seen → only then commission the bias update, with limits and a validity check. Fitting a model on uncalibrated data bakes the instrument error permanently into the coefficients, and the model then appears to work — right up until the weigher is finally calibrated, at which point the model breaks and the calibration gets blamed.
PROBLEM 9  [8 marks] · THE SECOND BEARING FAILURE

Mechanism. The inverter's three output voltages do not sum to zero at every instant, producing a common-mode voltage that couples capacitively across the air gap to the rotor. When the resulting shaft voltage exceeds the dielectric strength of the lubricant film (a few volts), it breaks down and a current discharges through the rolling element contact, melting microscopic craters. Repeated at kilohertz rates, the craters coalesce into fluting.
Prescription, in order: (1) fit a shaft grounding ring to give the current a low-impedance path that bypasses the bearing; (2) insulate the non-drive-end bearing or use hybrid ceramic rolling elements to break the circulating-current path — note that insulating one bearing without a grounding path can simply redirect the current through the driven equipment's bearings, so do both together; (3) install symmetrical shielded motor cable with 360° screen terminations at both ends and a proper high-frequency bonding path; (4) consider an output dV/dt or sine filter, which also protects the winding insulation on long cable runs; (5) verify by measuring shaft voltage with a shaft-riding probe before and after.

The wider lesson: an energy-saving retrofit introduced a new failure mode that no one costed. Every retrofit should be asked the question "what does this break that used to work?"

Derivations & Traps

Four results that appear again and again across this paper, derived rather than quoted — because a formula you can rebuild is a formula you can trust when the numbers look wrong.

DERIVATION 1 · WHY τ = 1.5(t₆₃ − t₂₈)

The two-point method looks like a recipe. It is not; it falls out of the FOPDT step response in three lines, and knowing that tells you exactly when it breaks.

Normalised FOPDT step response, for t ≥ θ: y(t) = 1 − exp[−(t − θ)/τ] Point A — the 63.2% point 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.6321 requires (t₆₃ − θ)/τ = 1 ⇒ t₆₃ = θ + τ …(1) Point B — the 28.3% point choose the time at which (t − θ)/τ = 1/3: y = 1 − e⁻¹⃗³ = 1 − 0.7165 = 0.2835 — i.e. 28.3% ⇒ t₂₈ = θ + τ/3 …(2) Subtract (2) from (1): t₆₃ − t₂₈ = τ − τ/3 = (2/3)τ ⇒ τ = 1.5 (t₆₃ − t₂₈) Substitute back into (1): ⇒ θ = t₆₃ − τ

What the derivation tells you that the recipe does not. First, the 28.3% point is not a curiosity — it is chosen as the one-third-time-constant point precisely so the two equations subtract cleanly. Second, both equations assume a pure first-order response with a pure delay: if the real process is second order or higher, the early part of the curve is S-shaped rather than exponential, the 28.3% point arrives late, and the method reports a longer θ and shorter τ than the truth — conservative, which is why it remains the standard field method. Third, and most practically, θ is obtained by subtraction of two large numbers, so it inherits the errors of both: this is exactly why a 9% error in t63 moved the recommended gain by 56% in WE1. Always cross-check θ against the observed time of first movement.

DERIVATION 2 · WHY PFDs MULTIPLY — AND WHEN THEY STOP

The LOPA multiplication rule is a statement about conditional probability, and its entire validity rests on one word.

A hazardous event requires the initiating event AND the failure of every layer: fₘₓₜ = fₓ × P(L₁ fails | IE) × P(L₂ fails | IE and L₁ failed) × … IF each layer's failure is INDEPENDENT of the initiating event and of the other layers, the conditionals collapse: P(L₂ fails | IE and L₁ failed) = P(L₂ fails) = PFD₂ ⇒ fₘₓₜ = fₓ × Π PFDₓ WHEN INDEPENDENCE FAILS — the beta-factor model: PFD₋ₘ₃ₓₖ₃₄ = (1−β)²·PFD₁·PFD₂ + β·PFD ⌜———————⌞ ⌜———⌞ vanishes fast DOES NOT As PFD → 0 the product term → 0 quadratically, but the common-cause term only falls linearly. ⇒ the achievable risk reduction is FLOORED at roughly 1/(β·PFD).

The two consequences you must be able to state. (i) In Section F, striking out the BPCS layer was not pedantry — if the initiating event is a BPCS failure, then P(BPCS layer fails | IE) is close to 1, not 0.1, and multiplying by 0.1 overstates the protection by an order of magnitude. LOPA's arithmetic is trivial; its discipline is the independence audit. (ii) In Problem 4, common cause consumed 81% of the redundant valve pair's unavailability. Duplicating hardware attacks only the first term; once β·PFD dominates, further redundancy is money spent on the term you are not reducing. The lever that remains is diversity — different devices, media, routes, power supplies, crews and test schedules — because every one of those reduces β.

The most-used equation in functional safety is a time average, and seeing it as one explains everything that follows from it.

A dangerous undetected fault, once it occurs, stays hidden until the next proof test. Starting from "as good as new" at t = 0: PFD(t) = 1 − e⁻ᵥᵗ ≈ λt for λt ≪ 1 Average unavailability over one proof-test interval: 1 ⌠TI 1 ⌠TI 1 ⋅ λTI² λ·TI PFDₐ₣ₔ = —— ⌡ PFD(t) dt = —— ⌡ λt dt = —— ⋅ ——— = ——— TI ⌟0 TI ⌟0 TI 2 2 THE 1/2 IS THE WHOLE POINT: on average, a hidden fault has been present for half the test interval when the demand arrives. PARTIAL STROKE — split the failure population by test coverage C: a fraction C is revealed by the short-interval test TIₖₛₜ a fraction (1−C) still waits for the full test TI PFDₐ₣ₔ = C·λ·TIₖₛₜ/2 + (1−C)·λ·TI/2

Three things this derivation hands you free. (i) PFD is linear in TI: halving the test interval halves the PFD exactly, which is why proof-test frequency is the cheapest single lever on a marginal SIF — and why an interval assumed in a calculation is a binding requirement, not a suggestion. (ii) Coverage is capped by physics: partial stroke exercises the actuator and stem but cannot prove tight shut-off, so C is typically 60–80%, and the residual (1−C) term sets a floor no amount of partial stroking can pass — in WE2, even C = 100% would leave the sensor and logic at 4.6×10−3. (iii) The approximation λt ≪ 1 quietly requires λ·TI to stay small; for a poor device with a long interval, use the exponential form rather than the linear one, or you will flatter the design.

DERIVATION 4 · FROM STANDARD DEVIATION TO MONEY

The benefits case in Section K rests on two conversions. Both are short, and both are places where candidates lose money in the wrong direction.

STEP 1 — variability to target To keep the fraction of product outside the limit acceptably small, the operating target must be held k standard deviations inside it: target = limit − kσ Differentiate with respect to σ (k and the limit are fixed): d(target)/dσ = −k ⇒ Δtarget = k · Δσ The benefit depends on the CHANGE in σ, not on its level: the first third of a variability reduction is worth exactly as much as the last third. STEP 2 — moisture target to saleable mass Dry solids are conserved. If m is the moisture fraction, mass = DS/(1 − m) mass₂/mass₁ = (1 − m₁)/(1 − m₂) For m₁ = 0.070, m₂ = 0.0835: ratio = 0.9300/0.9165 = 1.01473 → +1.47% STEP 3 — the sensitivity to k ∂(value)/∂k = Δσ × (d value/d target) ⇒ the benefit is LINEAR in k. Moving from k=3 to k=4 multiplies the claimed margin by 4/3 — or, as in WE4, pushes the target past the limit and deletes the benefit entirely.

The trap that this derivation exposes. It is tempting to compute the extra mass as simply "1.35% more moisture, therefore 1.35% more product". That is wrong, and it is wrong in the optimistic direction only by a little (1.47% versus 1.35%) — which is precisely why it survives review. The correct route is through the conserved quantity. In every yield calculation, find what is conserved (dry solids, moles, energy, dry gas) and work the ratio on that basis; percentages of a total that is itself changing will always mislead you. The same discipline applies to combustion excess-air calculations, evaporator mass balances and blending recipes.

Permissive / interlock / tripMust be true to start / prevents or forces at any time / takes the plant to a defined safe state, latched.
De-energise to tripHealthy state holds the circuit energised, so any break gives the safe state. The opposite arrangement makes every wiring fault an unrevealed failure.
ISA-88: Unit / phase / recipeA Unit is where a batch has an identity. Phase logic lives in the controller; the recipe supplies parameters. HOLD is recoverable; ABORT is not.
IPLIndependent protection layer. "Independent" means independent of the initiating cause and of the other layers — the word doing all the work in LOPA.
PFDavg, RRFAverage probability of failure on demand for a low-demand function; RRF = 1/PFDavg. SIL bands: 1 → RRF 10–100, 2 → 100–1 000, 3 → 1 000–10 000.
β (common cause)The fraction of failures that defeat all redundant channels at once. Falls only linearly with redundancy and therefore sets the floor.
Alarm floodMore than 10 alarms in any 10-minute interval, per operating position. Target: under 1% of intervals.
ISA-95 levels0 field, 1 control, 2 supervisory, 3 operations, 4 business, with a DMZ between 3 and 4 (or 2 and 3). Control flows down and never depends upward.
Controller loadingΣ(execution time / task period). Keep below 60–70%; loading rises as the inverse of the period.
OEEAvailability × performance × quality. Blind to giveaway: product that is within specification but better than it needs to be counts as perfect quality.

EIGHT-WEEK STUDY PATH
  • 1Week 1 — Dynamics. Re-read A57. Then identify three real step tests from your own plant (or from recorded trends) by the two-point method and compute SIMC settings for each. Derivation 1 must be reproducible from memory by the end of the week.
  • 2Week 2 — Diagnosis. Compute Pu for each model. Find one oscillating loop anywhere you have access to, classify it as tuning, stiction or external, and prove your classification with numbers. Sit Section B under timed conditions.
  • 3Week 3 — Strategy. Re-read A58. For five loops you know, name the structural limit each one is hitting and the strategy it implies. Design one feedforward completely, including the sign check and commissioning rules. Sit Section C.
  • 4Week 4 — Final elements. Re-read A59. Size three valves from real duty sheets, running all four tests in order every time. Do WE3 again with different numbers until the system-ceiling calculation is instinctive. Sit Section D.
  • 5Week 5 — Safety. Re-read A52 and Derivations 2 and 3. Perform one full LOPA on a real hazard, including the independence audit, then verify a SIF architecture against it. Sit Section F. Do not skip this week — it is the section with the compensation floor.
  • 6Week 6 — Sequence, alarms and architecture. Re-read A60, A53 and A61. Write one seven-state machine in Structured Text and test every abort path. Pull one month of real alarm data and compute the G1 metrics. Sit Sections E, G and H.
  • 7Week 7 — Power and reliability. Re-read A55, A08, A19 and A42. Do the power-factor and affinity-law calculations for a real motor list. Sit Sections I, J and K.
  • 8Week 8 — Synthesis. Sit the whole paper cold, in four hours, closed book. Mark it with the demo on page 15. Then write the 30-mark design question again from scratch on your own plant — that final exercise is worth more than everything before it, because it is the only one you cannot revise for.
