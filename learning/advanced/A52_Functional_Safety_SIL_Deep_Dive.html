Functional Safety & the Onion of Protection

Safety achieved by a function that actively does something — measured, engineered, and never assumed.

Definition

Functional safety is the part of overall safety that depends on a system actively performing a function in response to a dangerous condition — a high-pressure trip closing a valve, a gas detector starting shutdown. Its unit of delivery is the Safety Instrumented Function (SIF); the system that hosts SIFs is the Safety Instrumented System (SIS). Its core discipline: quantified risk reduction — every claim has a number, and every number has evidence.

Two standards rule. IEC 61508 is the parent — how to build safety-related equipment (used by device manufacturers). IEC 61511 is the process-sector child — how to apply that equipment in refineries, chemical plants, power stations and factories (used by you). Machinery has its own dialect (ISO 13849 / IEC 62061 — the A35 world); Page 11 builds the bridge.

The onion — independent layers

Defence in depth: each layer is independent — a failure that defeats one must not defeat another. The SIS is one ring, not the whole onion.

BPCS vs SIS — the great separation

BPCS (control system)

Keeps the process at setpoint — PID loops (A10), operators, SCADA (A14). Active all day, fails often, tuned for production. May claim at most modest risk-reduction credit.

SIS (safety system)

Does nothing for months, then must work on demand. Separate sensors, separate logic solver, separate final elements. Its whole design centres on one number: the probability it fails when called.

Separation is doctrine (IEC 61511-1): a common transmitter or a shared valve means a single failure removes two layers at once. The plant that "saves money" by tripping from the control transmitter has one layer wearing two hats — and the onion has a hole.

🧠 Quick check

A plant proposes using the BPCS level transmitter both for control and for the high-level trip "since it's already there". Give the two distinct arguments against, in 61511 language.

Tap to reveal ↓ (1) Independence: the most likely initiating event for high level is failure of that very transmitter (control loop drives the valve wrong). The "protection" then shares the initiating cause — its risk-reduction credit is zero exactly when needed. (2) Separation/claim limits: 61511 caps risk reduction claimed for BPCS functions (max RRF 10 per function, and the BPCS is not built, proof-tested or access-controlled to SIS rigor). Fit a dedicated trip transmitter; diversity (different technology) buys extra common-cause immunity.

The Safety Lifecycle — Cradle to Decommissioning

61511's deepest idea: SIL is not a purchase, it's a lifecycle property that decays without maintenance.

The wheel

Three phases, sixteen clauses, one rule: every phase produces evidence the next phase consumes. Skip a phase and the SIL claim is folklore.

Where SIFs come from — HAZOP feeds LOPA

A HAZOP walks the P&ID (A50's language) node by node with guidewords — no flow, more pressure, reverse, other than — and surfaces scenarios: initiating cause → consequence. Most are handled by design or the BPCS. The dangerous residue — "reactor overpressure → vessel rupture" — needs quantified protection: those scenarios flow into LOPA (Page 5), which decides how much risk reduction is still missing after every non-SIS layer has been credited. That missing factor becomes a SIL target for a new SIF.

Chain of custody: HAZOP scenario → LOPA gap → SIL target → SRS entry → designed SIF → verified PFDavg → validated installation → proof-tested for life Each arrow is a document. The SRS is the contract: what the SIF must do, how fast, what the safe state is, its SIL, its test interval — before anyone picks hardware.
Roles you'll play

Analysis engineer

HAZOP scribe/leader, LOPA facilitator — decides what protection is needed.

SIS designer

Turns SRS lines into architectures and PFD budgets — this module's core math.

Maintenance owner

Runs proof tests, guards bypasses, logs demands — where SIL is kept or quietly lost.

🧠 Quick check

Why does 61511 insist the SRS be written before hardware selection, and name three things a good SRS states per SIF.

Tap to reveal ↓ Because hardware chosen first becomes the requirement ("we have 2 transmitters, so 1oo2 it is") — design justifying itself, the classic systematic failure. The SRS fixes the target independently: per SIF it states (any three of) the process safe state, the trip setpoint and logic, the required SIL / RRF, the response time (process safety time!), the demand mode, proof-test interval assumptions, bypass and reset philosophy, and action on fault detection. Every PFD calc on Pages 6–9 silently assumes an SRS line exists.

SIL, PFDavg & Risk-Reduction Factor

Four bands of trustworthiness, each an order of magnitude apart. SIL is a property of the whole loop — never of a device.

The bands

A Safety Integrity Level quantifies how reliably a SIF performs on demand. For low-demand functions (called < once/year — most process trips) the measure is PFDavg, the average probability the function is dead when the demand arrives. Its inverse is the risk-reduction factor RRF.

Demand modes matter: a function called less than once a year lives in the PFDavg column; called more often (or acting continuously, like a burner-management flame monitor or a machine's safety function — A35's PL world) it lives in the PFH (dangerous failures per hour) column. Using the wrong column is a classic audit finding: a valve tripped weekly by a batch sequence is not low demand — each start is a demand, and the proof-test-interval math of Page 6 no longer applies as written.

Required RRF = unmitigated ÷ tolerable. The SIL band is read from the table — and if it says SIL 3+, first ask whether other layers (or a better process) can shrink the gap.

Three truths about SIL
  • 1SIL belongs to the SIF, not the device. A "SIL 3 transmitter" is marketing shorthand for "suitable for use in a SIL 3 loop" — the loop's PFD is the sum of sensor + logic + final element, and the valve usually dominates.
  • 2Each SIL step is 10× harder. Not 10% more paperwork — an order of magnitude less permitted failure, pushing you from single devices (SIL 1) to redundancy and rigorous testing (SIL 3).
  • 3SIL has two dimensions: the random-failure math (PFD, this module's arithmetic) and systematic capability (design process quality — Page 8). A perfect PFD calc on a badly specified function still fails: you'll compute precisely how rarely it does the wrong thing reliably.
🧠 Quick check

A SIF achieves PFDavg = 4×10⁻³. State its SIL band, its RRF, and what it means physically if the hazardous demand arrives once every 10 years.

Tap to reveal ↓ 4×10⁻³ sits in 10⁻³–10⁻² → SIL 2 band. RRF = 1/0.004 = 250. Physically: demands at 0.1/yr × 0.004 dead-on-arrival probability → the protected event leaks through at ~4×10⁻⁴/yr — once per 2 500 years on average. Whether that's tolerable is the LOPA target's decision (Page 5), not the SIF's — the SIF only delivers the 250× discount.
Page 4 / 16 · The Unit

Anatomy of a SIF

Sensor, logic solver, final element — three subsystems, one PFD budget, and a safe state that must be reachable by losing power.

The chain

A SIF is only as good as its weakest subsystem — and the weakest is usually the one with moving parts in dirty process fluid.

De-energise to trip — the polarity of safety

The SIS convention: the healthy state holds energy — solenoid energised, valve held open against a spring, contactor closed (A51). A trip, a cut cable, a dead PSU, a fire in the marshalling cabinet — all remove energy → spring drives the process to the safe state. Failures reveal themselves as trips, not as hidden dead protection.

De-energise-to-trip: broken wire = spurious trip = annoying but safe Energise-to-trip: broken wire = silent loss of protection = dangerous undetected Energise-to-trip is allowed (61511 permits it with line monitoring + fault alarms), but it must be justified — e.g. tripping would itself create a hazard (fire pump!).

The same polarity logic drives the wiring craft of A46 (NC stop contacts, monitored lines) and A35 (positively-opened contacts). Safety engineering keeps rediscovering one idea: arrange the physics so entropy votes for the safe state.

🧠 Quick check

An ESD valve's solenoid loses its 24 V supply during maintenance and the plant trips, costing four hours of production. The maintenance manager wants the trip circuit made energise-to-act "so a blown fuse can't shut us down again". Frame the correct reply.

Tap to reveal ↓ That spurious trip was the system proving its dangerous-failure coverage: any failure that removes energy is converted into a visible, safe event. Flipping to energise-to-trip converts the same fuse failure into silent loss of protection — the plant would run unprotected until the next proof test (months), which is precisely the PFD the SIL calculation forbids. The legitimate fixes: better fuse coordination and PSU redundancy (attack spurious-trip causes), or a 2oo3 sensor architecture (Page 7) that tolerates single faults without tripping — buying availability without selling safety.

LOPA — Layer of Protection Analysis

Order-of-magnitude bookkeeping: multiply the initiating frequency by each independent layer's failure probability, and see what's still missing.

The method

For each HAZOP scenario, LOPA computes: mitigated frequency = finitiating × Π PFDIPL. If the result still exceeds the tolerable target, the gap must be closed — usually by a SIF whose required PFD is the gap. An IPL (Independent Protection Layer) must be: independent of the initiating cause and of other IPLs, specific to the consequence, dependable (auditable PFD), and auditable in service.

Generic numbers (CCPS tables) — plants tune them with their own data. The discipline is the independence test: a level alarm on the same transmitter as the initiating control loop earns zero, however loudly it annunciates.

🧠 Quick check

In the tank scenario above, the "alarm + operator" credit assumed 20 minutes between alarm and overflow. A debottlenecking project doubles the feed pump. What must LOPA do, and what commonly goes wrong instead?

Tap to reveal ↓ Doubling feed halves the response window to ~10 min. If that's below the demonstrated operator-response time (detect, diagnose, walk, act — typically 10–20+ min), the alarm IPL's ×0.1 credit is void → mitigated frequency rises 10× → the SIF target tightens by one SIL band. This is Management of Change: process changes silently invalidate LOPA credits. What goes wrong: the pump is swapped as a "mechanical like-for-like" job, no MOC review touches the LOPA sheet, and the plant runs for years with a SIL 1 SIF doing a SIL 2 job.

Failure Rates, λDU & the PFD Integral

Four ways to fail, one that matters most: dangerous and undetected — the failure that waits for the demand.

The four-way split

Diagnostics convert dangerous-undetected into dangerous-detected — a detected fault alarms and can be repaired in hours instead of hiding for months. That conversion is why smart transmitters carry safety pedigrees.

The central formula
A dangerous-undetected fault can occur any time in the proof-test interval TI and sits hidden until the NEXT test. Average downtime = TI/2. PFDavg (1oo1) ≈ λDU · TI / 2 (+ λDD·MTTR — usually negligible) Example: λDU = 5×10⁻⁷/h (500 FIT), TI = 1 year = 8760 h PFDavg = 5×10⁻⁷ × 8760/2 = 2.2×10⁻³ → SIL 2 band, RRF ≈ 455 Halve the test interval → halve the PFD. Proof testing is a design variable.

Sources of λ: certified device reports (FMEDA), industry databases (OREDA, exida), or the site's own history ("prior use"). All assume the useful-life plateau of the bathtub curve — a valve at year 25 is off the plateau, and no formula rescues an ancient actuator. Also note the mission-time fine print: certificates quote a useful life (often 10–15 years); beyond it, replace or re-justify.

Spurious trips — the other ledger

λS buys you nuisance shutdowns: MTTFspurious ≈ 1/λS for 1oo1. Every spurious trip costs production, flares product, and — the subtle danger — erodes belief: a plant that sees weekly false trips starts bypassing. Safety and availability trade against each other through architecture (Page 7): 1oo2 halves PFD but doubles spurious-trip rate; 2oo2 does the reverse; 2oo3 buys both at the price of three devices. The best SIS engineers argue about spurious rates as fiercely as PFD.

🧠 Quick check

A transmitter datasheet claims λD = 800 FIT with DC = 90%. With a 2-year proof test, what PFD does it contribute in 1oo1 — and what does the 90% actually buy?

Tap to reveal ↓ λDU = (1−0.9) × 800 = 80 FIT = 8×10⁻⁸/h. TI = 17 520 h → PFD ≈ 8×10⁻⁸ × 17520/2 = 7×10⁻⁴ — SIL 3 territory for this subsystem. The 90% DC moved 720 FIT into the detected column: those faults annunciate within seconds and are repaired in hours (their PFD contribution λDD×MTTR ≈ 720×10⁻⁹×24 ≈ 1.7×10⁻⁵, negligible). Without diagnostics the same device would give 7×10⁻³ — ten times worse. Diagnostics are a 10× PFD lever that costs firmware, not iron.

2oo3, drawn

Three measurements, majority rule: single failures in either direction are survived. The price: three taps, three devices, and honest comparison diagnostics between them.

Common cause — the β factor
Redundancy assumes independence. Reality: shared tap lines freeze together, one contractor miscalibrates all three, one firmware bug lives in every unit. Model: a fraction β of dangerous failures hit ALL channels at once (typical 2–10%). PFD1oo2 ≈ (λDUTI)²/3 + β·λDU·TI/2 With λDUTI = 4×10⁻³ and β = 5%: independent term 5.3×10⁻⁶, common-cause term 1×10⁻⁴ → the β term is ~20× larger. Beyond 1oo2, β — not more hardware — sets your PFD.

Buying a third transmitter improves the (λTI)² term that no longer matters. Reducing β does: diversity (different measurement principles — echoes A50's radar+fork rule), separate taps and impulse lines, different calibration crews, staggered proof tests. The 61508 β-factor checklist scores exactly these. This is also A07/A35's lesson wearing process clothes: the common-cause ceiling is organisational as much as technical.

🧠 Quick check

A design review proposes upgrading a trip from 1oo2 to 1oo3 transmitters (same tap, same model, same calibrators) to reach SIL 3. Why will the calculated improvement be largely fictional?

Tap to reveal ↓ With shared tap, model and calibration, β is at the high end (~10%). The 1oo2 PFD is already dominated by β·λDUTI/2 — a term the third transmitter does not touch (a plugged common tap blinds all three identically). The honest paths to SIL 3: reduce β (separate taps, diverse technology — e.g. one DP + one radar, A50), shorten TI, raise DC, or attack the final element, which probably dominates the loop PFD anyway (Page 4's 50%). Redundancy multiplies independence; it cannot create it.

Hardware Constraints & Systematic Capability

PFD arithmetic alone can't authorise a SIL claim — the standard adds structural minimums and a design-quality dimension the math can't see.

Why constraints exist

Failure-rate data is uncertain — optimistic FMEDA numbers could "prove" SIL 3 with a single cheap device. So the standards cap what architecture may claim regardless of arithmetic: hardware fault tolerance (HFT) — how many dangerous faults the subsystem survives — sets a ceiling on SIL. HFT 0 = single device; HFT 1 = survives one fault (1oo2, 2oo3); HFT 2 = survives two (1oo3, 2oo4).

Claim ceiling (61511 route)HFT 0HFT 1HFT 2
Typical field device, good prior useSIL 1SIL 2SIL 3
Certified device w/ high SFF / diagnosticsSIL 2SIL 3SIL 4

The 61508 original ties the ceiling to SFF (Safe Failure Fraction) — the share of all failures that are either safe or detected — via Route 1H tables; Route 2H (and 61511's simplified table) leans on field-proven reliability data instead. The engineering intuition survives every table revision: a single unmonitored device may not carry a high SIL alone, however good its datasheet claims.

Systematic capability — the second dimension
Random hardware failures → PFD arithmetic (Pages 6–7) — quantifiable Systematic failures → design/spec/software errors — NOT random, NOT in the math wrong setpoint in the SRS · firmware bug · wrong material for the fluid · calibration procedure that zeros the wrong range (A50) · copy-paste logic error Defence: SC ratings (SC 1–4) earned by development-process rigor (61508 audits), plus lifecycle discipline — reviews, validation against the SRS, MOC.

A device certificate therefore has two halves: the FMEDA numbers (random) and the systematic capability (SC N — fit for use in SIL N loops). Both must meet the target. And no certificate covers your application errors — which is why validation (does the installed function actually close the right valve in the required time, from the real sensor?) is a lifecycle stage, not a paperwork checkbox.

Reading a safety certificate without being fooled
  • 1Check the boundary: "SIL 2 capable" for the transmitter electronics may exclude the impulse line, manifold and process seal — the parts that actually plug (A50's slopes and manifolds).
  • 2Check λDU at your conditions: FMEDA assumes a mission profile; a corrosive, vibrating, 60 °C installation is not the lab.
  • 3Check useful life: certificates commonly assume 10–15 years and a functioning proof-test regime at the stated coverage — miss either and the numbers are void.
  • 4Check the fine print on diagnostics: claimed DC often requires the diagnostic outputs to be actually wired and acted on (fault relay → maintenance alarm). An unwired fault contact contributes zero DC.
🧠 Quick check

A vendor offers a "SIL 3 certified" solenoid valve and suggests a single one (1oo1) will make your SIL 3 trip. Two reasons the offer fails as stated?

Tap to reveal ↓ (1) Architectural constraint: SIL 3 with HFT 0 requires the top row of exceptions (very high SFF certified route) — for final elements with mechanical dominant failures this is rarely granted; expect HFT 1 (e.g. dual solenoids / 1oo2 valves) for SIL 3. (2) SIL is the loop's: even if the solenoid alone could claim it, the SIF's PFD budget includes sensor + logic + the process valve and actuator the solenoid pilots — the certificate covers one link. "SIL 3 certified" means "eligible as a component", not "instant SIL 3".

Proof Testing — Where SIL Lives or Dies

The PFD formula contains TI because someone promised to test. Every skipped or shallow test quietly rewrites the SIL claim.

The deal you signed

PFDavg = λDUTI/2 assumes that at every interval TI, a proof test finds and repairs all dangerous-undetected faults — resetting the clock. The test is therefore part of the SIF: interval, procedure, and coverage were fixed in the SRS. Change any of them and you have changed the SIL — usually without noticing.

Imperfect testing: real tests catch only a fraction PTC (proof-test coverage). PFDavg ≈ PTC·λDU·TI/2 + (1−PTC)·λDU·MT/2 (MT = mission time to a REAL full test — overhaul or replacement, maybe 10+ yr) PTC 100%, TI 1 yr: PFD = 2.2×10⁻³ (500 FIT example) PTC 70%, TI 1 yr, MT 10 yr: PFD = 1.5×10⁻³ + 6.6×10⁻³ = 8.1×10⁻³ — nearly 4× worse, and the uncovered 30% climbs relentlessly for a decade. Coverage beats frequency.

What limits coverage? The classic: exercising the trip relay but not the valve; stroking the valve unloaded when the dangerous failure is "won't move against process pressure"; simulating the signal downstream of the very impulse line that plugs (A50 again). A proof test that doesn't challenge the dominant λDU mechanisms is theatre.

Partial stroke testing — buying PFD without a shutdown

Big ESD valves can't be fully closed while the plant runs — so PST moves the valve 10–20% and confirms it breaks away and responds. That catches stuck stems and dead actuators (a large share of λDU) between full tests, effectively acting as a diagnostic with its own coverage (~50–70% of valve faults). The full-closure test still happens at turnarounds — PST stretches the interval it can safely wait. Modern positioners automate PST and log signatures; a drifting breakaway torque trend is condition monitoring for your last line of defence (kin to A42's philosophy).

Operate-phase discipline

Bypass control

Every bypass logged, time-limited, risk-assessed, alarmed, and physically minimised. A SIF in bypass is a LOPA layer that doesn't exist — the plant is running on fewer rings of the onion.

Demand recording

Every real trip is a free proof test AND a datum: demands more frequent than the LOPA assumed (once/10 yr?) mean the analysis — and possibly the demand mode — is wrong. Investigate the initiator, don't celebrate the save.

Failure feedback

Found-failed proof tests update the site's λDU evidence. Three found-dead solenoids in five years is data screaming louder than any FMEDA.

MOC everywhere

New impeller, new fluid, new setpoint, new firmware — each re-opens the LOPA/SRS question (Page 5's pump story). The lifecycle wheel must actually turn.

🧠 Quick check

A site "improves" proof testing by doubling frequency (6-month instead of annual) but keeps a procedure that only injects a signal at the logic solver input. Why is the SIL claim now further from reality, not closer?

Tap to reveal ↓ The injected-signal test covers only the logic solver and output wiring — a small slice of λDU (Page 4: the valve owns ~50%, the sensor ~35%). Testing that slice twice as often polishes the already-shiny part while the sensor impulse line and the valve stem — the dominant hidden failures — go unchallenged for the whole mission time. The paperwork now shows comforting "tests passed" at double rate, deepening false confidence. Fix: redesign the procedure for end-to-end coverage (real process stimulus or manifold-level injection + actual valve stroke), then choose the interval the PFD budget needs.

The Logic Solver — Safety PLCs & Implementation Craft

Why a safety PLC is not a careful ordinary PLC, and how the application program becomes the most dangerous component.

The restrictions are the feature: 61511 pushes application logic toward a Limited Variability Language — cause-and-effect matrices and certified blocks — because systematic software error is the logic solver's real threat, and expressiveness is where systematic errors breed. A cause-and-effect matrix that operations can read is a safety mechanism.

Implementation craft — where 61511 meets the panel
  • 1Physical separation from the BPCS: separate cabinets or clearly segregated sections (A46 discipline), separate power feeds, no shared barriers or marshalling that lets one fault cross layers.
  • 2Fail-safe I/O wiring: NC/de-energise conventions, line monitoring (detect open AND short — the A35 trick), pulse-tested digital outputs that catch a welded interposing relay (mirror contacts, A51).
  • 3Communication ≠ trip path: the SIS may report to SCADA (A14) and accept resets, but writes from the BPCS side must be structurally unable to defeat a trip (read-only gateways, hardwired critical commands). Cyber follows: the SIS is Purdue's most protected zone (A33's OT security inverted-CIA logic).
  • 4Response time budget: sensor lag (A50's τ) + scan + logic + valve stroke must beat the process safety time — the interval from deviation to unrecoverable. Half the PST is the design habit.
  • 5Trip setpoint placement: far enough from the operating envelope to avoid spurious trips, early enough to leave stroke time — a three-way negotiation between operations, LOPA and valve dynamics.
🧠 Quick check

Why do safety PLCs pulse-test their digital outputs (brief microsecond off-pulses while "on"), and what failure would otherwise wait silently?

Tap to reveal ↓ An output transistor (or interposing relay contact) can fail shorted/welded in the ON state — the classic dangerous-undetected failure: everything looks normal, the solenoid stays energised, but the channel can no longer de-energise on demand. The micro-pulse briefly commands OFF and verifies the line actually drops (too fast for the solenoid to react); a stuck output fails the check and raises a fault → λDU becomes λDD. It's the same philosophy as EDM reading mirror contacts in A35/A51 — never trust an actuator you haven't recently seen obey an OFF command.

Who rules where — and the border cases

61511 territory

Reactor trips, tank overfill, furnace/burner management (with its own child standard), compressor protection, HIPPS replacing relief capacity.

13849/62061 territory

Guard doors, light curtains, two-hand controls, robot cells (A35), press brakes — the E-stop chains of A46/A51.

Border: packaged machines in plants

A centrifuge in a chemical plant: its own guarding is machinery-law; its process trip (overspeed on hazardous fluid) may be a SIF in the plant's LOPA. One machine, two frameworks, document both.

Border: burners & furnaces

BMS logic (EN 50156 / NFPA 85) blends both: high-demand flame supervision + low-demand trips. The demand-mode question must be answered per function, not per cabinet.

For the automation engineer the payoff is fluency: you will sit in one meeting where "PL d, Cat 3, EDM" is spoken (A35) and another where "SIL 2, 1oo2, β 5%" is spoken — and you now know they are the same four ideas: quantified target, redundancy, diagnostics, common-cause discipline.

🧠 Quick check

A packaging line's light curtain (opened ~50×/day) and a solvent tank's high-level trip (demand maybe once in 10 years) both "need SIL 2 / PL d". Why would designing the tank trip like the light curtain — relying on its diagnostics and skipping proof tests — be dangerous?

Tap to reveal ↓ The light curtain's safety is self-revealing: 50 demands/day exercise the whole channel; a dangerous fault is discovered within hours by failure-to-clear or by DC, so PFH math with diagnostics suffices. The tank trip gets no natural exercise: a stuck valve or plugged tap sits invisible precisely because demands never come — its integrity depends almost entirely on λDU·TI/2, i.e. on the proof-test contract. Skip the tests and PFD grows linearly with elapsed time toward certainty-of-failure. Demand mode isn't paperwork taxonomy — it decides which physics keeps you honest.

Recap, Failure Modes & Final Quiz

Eight ideas, six ways real plants lose their SIL, five questions.

The eight ideas
  • 1Safety is layered and independent: the SIS is one ring of the onion; sharing hardware between rings punches holes through several at once.
  • 2SIL is a lifecycle property of a SIF — set by LOPA, written in the SRS, built in realisation, and kept alive by proof tests and MOC. Never a device sticker.
  • 3The currency is PFDavg (low demand) or PFH (high demand) — bands a decade apart; picking the wrong column invalidates everything downstream.
  • 4λDU·TI/2 is the heartbeat: dangerous-undetected failures accumulate between proof tests. Diagnostics shrink λDU; testing resets its clock.
  • 5Architectures vote: 1oo2 for safety, 2oo2 for availability, 2oo3 for both — and β common-cause sets the real ceiling beyond 1oo2.
  • 6De-energise to trip: arrange the physics so lost energy = safe state; energise-to-trip demands line monitoring and justification.
  • 7Systematic failures don't obey the math: SRS errors, software bugs and wrong materials need process rigor (SC, validation, LVL), not more redundancy.
  • 8The final element dominates: ~half the loop PFD is the valve; PST, dual solenoids and honest full-stroke tests are where SIL is really bought.
How plants lose their SIL
🔓 The eternal bypass

A trip bypassed for startup… still bypassed in week 6. The LOPA layer exists on paper only. Cure: alarmed, time-limited, logged bypasses reviewed daily — and counted as incidents when overdue.

🎭 Proof-test theatre

Signal injected at the solver, valve never stroked against pressure, tap never challenged. Coverage ~30% while the record says "pass". PFD quietly triples (WE 4 does the math).

👯 Common cause uncounted

Three identical transmitters, one tap, one calibrator, β ≈ 10% — the third device is decoration. Diversity and separation, not multiplication.

📈 Demand-rate drift

LOPA assumed a demand per decade; the aging BPCS now initiates yearly. Each save is logged as success while the SIF burns through its probability budget 10× faster than designed.

✏️ MOC bypass surgery

Bigger pump, new solvent, raised setpoint — installed as "minor works", LOPA sheet untouched (Page 5's story). The SRS now describes a plant that no longer exists.

🧟 Zombie hardware

Devices past certificate useful life, actuators off the bathtub plateau, obsolete logic solver with no spares — λ figures from the FMEDA no longer describe the iron on the pipe.

🧠 Final quiz 1

Rank where each belongs: PFDavg or PFH — (a) reactor high-pressure trip demanded ~once/15 yr; (b) burner flame supervision; (c) batch-reactor charge interlock exercised every batch (3/day).

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) Low demand → PFDavg — classic sleeping trip, proof-test economics. (b) Continuous → PFH — the function IS the control of the hazard; a dangerous failure is immediately hazardous. (c) High demand → PFH — 1000+ demands/yr means faults are revealed by demands themselves faster than any proof test; treating it as low-demand PFD would flatter it absurdly.
🧠 Final quiz 2

Your 1oo1 SIF computes PFD = 3×10⁻³ but the target is SIL 2 with RRF ≥ 500 (PFD ≤ 2×10⁻³). Name the four levers, cheapest first.

Tap to reveal ↓ (1) Shorten TI (test every 8 months: 3×10⁻³ × 8/12 = 2×10⁻³ ✓) — paperwork and labour, no capex. (2) Raise proof-test coverage / add PST — attacks the dominant valve term. (3) Better device / higher DC — swap for lower λDU. (4) Architecture — 1oo2 the weakest subsystem (mind β). In practice you check which subsystem owns the budget first (usually the final element) and pull these levers there — a 1oo2 on the transmitter when the valve owns 70% of PFD is spending money where the problem isn't.
🧠 Final quiz 3

Why does 61511 cap the risk-reduction credit of the BPCS at RRF 10 — even for a beautifully engineered control loop?

Tap to reveal ↓ Because the BPCS is not built or operated to safety-lifecycle rigor: no SRS, no verified PFD, unrestricted online changes (A07 forces/edits), shared components across functions, no proof-test regime, and it is often itself the initiating cause. A claim beyond 10 would rest on evidence the BPCS lifecycle simply doesn't generate. Need more? Build it as a SIF in the SIS, where the evidence machine exists.
🧠 Final quiz 4

A 2oo3 pressure trip shows one transmitter in fault alarm for two weeks. Operations says "still 2oo2, we're fine." What has actually happened to safety and availability?

Tap to reveal ↓ Degraded 2oo3→2oo2 means: safety worsened (2oo2 needs BOTH remaining channels healthy to trip; its PFD is ~λDUTI — an order worse than 2oo3) and spurious immunity relies on both too. The standard's answer: a declared degraded-mode policy with a repair-time clock (MTTR assumptions in the PFD calc — often 72 h): repair within it, or take the compensating measure/trip. Two weeks of "still fine" means the loop has silently been running a full SIL band below its claim.
🧠 Final quiz 5

Explain to a plant manager, in three sentences without jargon, why the SIS that "never does anything" justifies its maintenance budget.

Tap to reveal ↓ "The trip system is insurance we've sized mathematically: it only has to work on the one day in ten years everything else has already failed. Its whole value lives in the probability it works that day — and that probability is bought with the testing and discipline you're funding, decaying automatically when we stop. Cutting its maintenance doesn't save money; it silently cancels the policy while we keep paying the premium in false confidence."
Memory hook

"HAZOP finds it, LOPA sizes it, the SIF closes it — and the proof test keeps the promise."

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems, solved in full.

WE 1 · LOPA to SIL target

Scenario: BPCS loop failure overfills a solvent tank (f = 10⁻¹/yr). Consequence: large spill with ignition potential; tolerable frequency 10⁻⁵/yr. Credited IPLs: independent hi-level alarm with proven 25-min response (×0.1) and a bund (×0.01). Size the SIF.

Mitigated (before SIF) = 10⁻¹ × 10⁻¹ × 10⁻² = 10⁻⁴ /yr Gap to target = 10⁻⁴ / 10⁻⁵ = 10× short → SIF required PFD ≤ 10⁻¹ … i.e. RRF ≥ 10 → SIL 1 If the consequence review had set 10⁻⁶/yr (fatality potential): gap = 100× → PFD ≤ 10⁻² → SIL 2 — one decade of consequence = one SIL.

Discussion: notice what LOPA prevented: a reflexive "make it SIL 3" gold-plate. Also notice the fragility — the alarm credit dies if response time or independence fails (Page 5), instantly promoting the SIF to SIL 2. Targets inherit every assumption upstream.

WE 2 · Loop PFD budget (1oo1 everywhere)

A SIL 2 trip: transmitter λDU = 500 FIT, certified logic solver PFD = 1×10⁻⁴, valve+solenoid λDU = 1500 FIT. Annual proof test (TI = 8760 h). Verify the claim.

Sensor: 5×10⁻⁷ × 8760/2 = 2.19×10⁻³ Logic: 1.0×10⁻⁴ (vendor-calculated, incl. its own testing regime) Valve: 1.5×10⁻⁶ × 8760/2 = 6.57×10⁻³ Loop PFD = 8.9×10⁻³ → inside 10⁻³–10⁻² → SIL 2 band ✓ (barely; RRF ≈ 113) Shares: valve 74%, sensor 25%, logic 1% — the valve owns this loop.

Discussion: "SIL 2 achieved" is true but fragile — RRF 113 vs the band floor of 100 leaves no margin for imperfect testing (WE 4!). The efficient upgrades attack the 74%: PST on the valve, dual solenoids, or 6-month valve-only tests. Upgrading the transmitter would polish 25% of the problem.

WE 3 · 1oo2 with common cause

Two transmitters, each λDU = 500 FIT, TI = 1 yr, β = 10% (same tap, same model). Compute PFD and show what β costs; then with β = 2% (separate taps, diverse models).

x = λDU·TI = 5×10⁻⁷ × 8760 = 4.38×10⁻³ Independent term: x²/3 = (4.38×10⁻³)²/3 = 6.4×10⁻⁶ β term: β·x/2 = 0.10 × 4.38×10⁻³/2 = 2.19×10⁻⁴ PFD ≈ 2.25×10⁻⁴ — the β term is 34× the independent term With β = 2%: 6.4×10⁻⁶ + 4.4×10⁻⁵ = 5.0×10⁻⁵ — 4.5× better, bought with layout & diversity, not hardware

Discussion: the redundant pair without common-cause discipline delivers 2.2×10⁻⁴ (SIL 3 band) — but a naive engineer computing only x²/3 would claim 6.4×10⁻⁶ (SIL 4!), off by 34×. Every serious SIL verification therefore documents its β and the checklist score behind it.

WE 4 · Imperfect proof testing

A valve subsystem: λDU = 800 FIT, annual test with PTC = 60% (unloaded stroke only), full test only at 12-yr turnaround. Compute the honest PFD; then with PST raising effective coverage to 90%.

Perfect-test illusion: λTI/2 = 8×10⁻⁷×8760/2 = 3.5×10⁻³ Honest: PTC·λTI/2 + (1−PTC)·λ·MT/2 = 0.6×3.5×10⁻³ + 0.4×8×10⁻⁷×(12×8760)/2 = 2.1×10⁻³ + 1.68×10⁻² = 1.9×10⁻² → below SIL 1! The uncovered 40% ruins everything. With PST (PTC→0.9): 0.9×3.5×10⁻³ + 0.1×8×10⁻⁷×52560 = 3.2×10⁻³+4.2×10⁻³ = 7.4×10⁻³ → SIL 2 ✓

Discussion: coverage beats frequency (compare: doubling test frequency at PTC 60% only improves the first term — total ~1.8×10⁻², still failing). This single calculation is the strongest argument for partial-stroke testing ever written, and it's three lines long.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Work each before revealing — the reveal is the marking scheme.

Problem 1

A LOPA requires RRF 800 from a SIF. State the SIL and the PFDavg ceiling.

Tap to reveal ↓RRF 800 → PFD ≤ 1/800 = 1.25×10⁻³ → inside 10⁻³–10⁻² is SIL 2… careful: 1.25×10⁻³ IS in the SIL 2 band (10⁻³ ≤ PFD < 10⁻²), so SIL 2 with a verified PFD ≤ 1.25×10⁻³ — tighter than the band floor. The SIL label alone is not the requirement; the RRF is.
Problem 2

λDU = 300 FIT, TI = 2 yr, 1oo1. PFDavg? Which SIL band?

Tap to reveal ↓PFD = 3×10⁻⁷ × 17 520/2 = 2.6×10⁻³ → SIL 2 band (RRF ≈ 380). Note 2 years doubled the 1-yr figure — TI is linear in the formula.
Problem 3

Why can a high-level alarm and the high-level trip never share one transmitter if both are LOPA-credited?

Tap to reveal ↓IPLs must be independent of each other, not just of the initiating cause: one plugged tap or failed sensor deletes both credits simultaneously, so the multiplication f×0.1×PFDSIF is a fiction — the joint failure probability is the single transmitter's, not the product. Credit one, or fit separate devices (ideally diverse — A50's level-tech table).
Problem 4

A plant replaces a 1oo1 trip valve with 2oo2 (both must act) "to improve safety". Evaluate.

Tap to reveal ↓Backwards: 2oo2 doubles dangerous failure exposure (PFD ≈ λDUTI — both valves must be healthy for the trip to succeed… no: for 2oo2-to-act, EITHER failing dangerous kills the function → PFD ≈ 2×(λTI/2) = λTI). It halves spurious trips — that's its actual purpose. For safety, valves in series each capable of isolation (1oo2): PFD drops to (λTI)²/3 + β term. Direction of redundancy must match direction of failure you fear.
Problem 5

Halving TI vs adding an identical second sensor in 1oo2 (β = 10%): which improves PFD more, starting from λDUTI = 4×10⁻³ (1oo1 PFD 2×10⁻³)?

Tap to reveal ↓Halve TI: PFD → 1×10⁻³ (2× better, free-ish). 1oo2 @ β 10%: (4×10⁻³)²/3 + 0.1×2×10⁻³ = 5×10⁻⁶ + 2×10⁻⁴ ≈ 2×10⁻⁴ (10× better — but β-limited). So the second sensor wins on PFD; the shorter TI wins on cost and also improves the valve/logic terms that redundant sensors don't touch. Exam answer: state both, note β cap and that loop-level thinking decides.
Problem 6

Give two legitimate reasons a SIF might be designed energise-to-trip, and the two compensations 61511 expects.

Tap to reveal ↓Reasons: (1) the tripped state itself creates hazard on spurious activation — fire-water pumps, some depressurising systems; (2) extreme spurious-trip cost with demonstrated intolerable consequences (flare capacity, thermal shock). Compensations: line/circuit monitoring (open- and short-circuit detection so loss of the trip path annunciates ≈ converting λDU→λDD) and secure energy (monitored redundant power, alarmed). Plus the justification lives in the SRS, not in folklore.
Problem 7

A safety PLC shows three variables forced for eight months (A07's forces trap). Frame the finding in 61511 terms.

Tap to reveal ↓A force is an unauthorised bypass of a safety function outside MOC and bypass procedure: the as-running application program no longer matches the validated SRS logic — a live systematic failure. Findings: which SIFs are defeated (LOPA layers gone), duration exposure (8 months of unprotected operation), procedure breach (bypass log, alarms, authorisation). Actions: remove under controlled conditions, incident-report the exposure, audit why force alarms didn't escalate.
Problem 8

List six items every SRS entry must contain for one SIF (there are ~a dozen; six earn the marks).

Tap to reveal ↓Any six of: the process hazard addressed; safe state; trip setpoint(s) & logic (incl. voting); required SIL / RRF and demand mode; required response time vs process safety time; proof-test interval & coverage assumptions; bypass/override philosophy; reset behaviour (manual, conditions); action on detected fault (trip or alarm+timer); spurious-trip constraints; environmental/materials requirements; interfaces to BPCS/HMI.
Problem 9

Your 2oo3 sensor group's proof tests are all done on the same day by the same tech with the same calibrator. Which parameter did the scheme just inflate, and what's the fix?

Tap to reveal ↓β — the common-cause fraction now includes calibration-induced simultaneous mis-setting (a systematic common cause: one wrong reference propagates to all three, A50's traceability nightmare). Fixes: stagger tests across the interval (also flattens the PFD sawtooth), rotate technicians/equipment, and compare as-found values between channels as a cross-check — disagreement between "identical" channels is free diagnostics.
Problem 10

A machine builder claims "PL e, therefore SIL 3" for a guard function reused as a process trip on a solvent reactor. Give the two-step audit response.

Tap to reveal ↓(1) Demand-mode audit: PL e was earned in high-demand math (PFH, exercised daily, self-revealing faults). As a process trip it may sleep for years — its integrity now hangs on proof tests it was never assigned; the PFDavg must be computed fresh with λDU and a real TI (Page 11's lesson). (2) Lifecycle audit: a 61511 SIF needs an SRS entry, LOPA-derived target, validation and an operate-phase regime — a machinery DoC doesn't supply these. The hardware may well be capable; the claim must be rebuilt in the process dialect.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations worth owning, the symbols, and the seven classic traps.

Derivation 1 · PFDavg = λDUTI/2 from first principles
Unavailability at time t after a perfect test: probability a DU fault has occurred and not been found: U(t) = 1 − e^(−λDUt) ≈ λDUt (λt ≪ 1) Average over the interval [0, TI]: PFDavg = (1/TI) ∫₀^TI λDUt dt = (1/TI)·λDU·TI²/2 = λDU·TI/2 ∎ The sawtooth picture: U(t) climbs linearly, resets at each test — the average is half the peak. Staggered redundant tests flatten the peaks.
Derivation 2 · 1oo2 independent term (λTI)²/3
Both channels must be dead: U1oo2(t) = (λDUt)² (independent faults) PFDavg = (1/TI) ∫₀^TI (λDUt)² dt = (λDU)²·TI²/3 = (λDUTI)²/3 ∎ Add common cause: a β-fraction behaves as a single 1oo1 channel: PFD1oo2 ≈ (λDUTI)²/3 + β·λDUTI/2 Sanity habit: with λTI ~ 10⁻³–10⁻², (λTI)² is 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁴ — so ANY β above ~1% dominates. Write the β term first in exams.
Derivation 3 · LOPA algebra & SIL allocation
fmitigated = finit · Πi PFDIPL,i target: fmitigated · PFDSIF ≤ ftol → PFDSIF ≤ ftol / (finit·Π PFDIPL) RRFSIF = 1/PFDSIF In decade bookkeeping (all values 10^−n): required decades nSIF = (ntol) − (ninit + Σ nIPL) → SIL = nSIF (1 decade per SIL) This is why LOPA sheets are integer arithmetic — and why a lost IPL credit promotes the SIF by exactly one SIL.

Seven exam & field traps
  • 1"SIL-rated device" ≠ SIL-rated loop. SIL belongs to the SIF; a certificate makes a component eligible, nothing more.
  • 22oo2 is an availability scheme. It doubles dangerous exposure. Match the voting direction to the failure you fear.
  • 3Demand mode decides the column. Weekly-exercised functions don't get PFDavg credit maths; sleeping trips don't get self-revealing-fault credit.
  • 4β eats redundancy. Beyond 1oo2, common cause — not channel count — bounds PFD. Write the β term first.
  • 5Proof-test coverage beats frequency. An untested failure mode accrues PFD over mission time, not test interval (WE 4).
  • 6BPCS credit caps at 10 — once. And never when the BPCS is the initiator. Double-counting the control system is LOPA's cardinal sin.
  • 7The certificate boundary and useful life are load-bearing. Electronics-only FMEDAs, lab mission profiles and 10-year lifetimes quietly exclude your actual installation.
