Page 1 · Foundations

Why Hydraulics? Force Without Compromise

Precise Definition

Hydraulics is the use of a pressurised, near-incompressible fluid (mineral oil, or a fire-resistant / bio fluid) to transmit and control power — converting the mechanical energy of a pump into very high linear or rotary force at the actuator.

Where pneumatics trades efficiency for speed and simplicity, hydraulics trades simplicity for raw, controllable force. The defining property is the opposite of air's: oil is nearly incompressible. That stiffness gives you precise positioning and enormous force density — at the price of leaks, heat, filtration and cost.

Strengths

Huge force in a small package (200–350 bar), stiff & precise positioning, holds load statically, smooth under heavy varying load, high power-to-weight at the actuator.

Weaknesses

Leaks & mess, generates heat (needs cooling), contamination-sensitive, costlier components, fluid disposal, noisier power unit, slower to commission.

Energy density: hydraulics ≈ 200–350 bar · pneumatics ≈ 6–10 bar → same-bore cylinder makes ~30–50× the force of a pneumatic one. Hydraulics wins on force, stiffness and load-holding, not cost or cleanliness.
Where this sits in the plant

Hydraulics is the default for high-force, load-holding, heavy-duty motion: presses, injection-moulding clamps, mobile machines (excavators), steel mills, dies and forming. When force is modest and cleanliness matters you drop to pneumatics; when you need clean, programmable, mid-force motion you move to electric servos.

Page 2 · System Architecture

The Complete Circuit — Power Unit to Work

Micro 13 showed four blocks. A real hydraulic system is a closed loop: oil leaves the tank, is pressurised, does work, and returns — being cleaned and cooled on the way. Every system is built around a HPU (Hydraulic Power Unit): tank, pump, motor, relief valve and filtration.

A real HPU also carries a suction strainer, a cooler (heat exchanger), pressure gauges, check valves and a breather/filler cap with a desiccant.

Relief valve

The safety cornerstone. Sets the maximum system pressure; dumps excess flow to tank so the pump can never over-pressurise the circuit.

Filtration

Suction, pressure and return filters. ~75–80% of all hydraulic failures trace to contaminated oil — this is not optional.

Reservoir

Stores fluid, sheds heat, settles dirt and lets entrained air escape. Rule of thumb: tank ≈ 3–5× pump flow per minute.

Page 3 · The Physics

Pascal's Law — and Where the Power Goes

Micro 13 gave you Pascal's law. Here is the full picture: pressure transmits equally, force multiplies by area, but flow sets speed and the product P × Q is the power that must be paid for — and partly turns into heat.

Pascal: pressure applied to a confined fluid acts equally in all directions Force: F = P · A (pressure × piston area) Speed: v = Q / A (flow ÷ area sets actuator speed) Hydraulic power: P_hyd (kW) = (p[bar] · Q[L/min]) / 600 Heat: any pressure drop with flow ⇒ heat: ΔP·Q becomes thermal

Two ideas govern daily engineering:

THE FORCE MULTIPLIER

Small piston drives a big one

Apply pressure across a small input area; the same pressure on a large output area gives proportionally more force. A 1 cm² input at 100 bar lifts a 100 cm² piston with 100× the force — the hydraulic press. The trade is stroke: the big piston moves 100× slower.

STIFFNESS ≠ RIGIDITY

Oil is "almost" incompressible

Oil's bulk modulus is ~1.5–2 GPa, so it compresses only ~0.5% per 100 bar — but that tiny give, plus entrained air, is exactly the spring that makes servo-hydraulic loops oscillate if mis-tuned.

Worked power example: p = 200 bar, Q = 30 L/min P_hyd = (200 · 30) / 600 = 10 kW of fluid power If a valve drops 30 bar at 30 L/min: heat = (30·30)/600 = 1.5 kW straight to oil. Bulk modulus β ≈ 1.6 GPa → ΔV/V ≈ ΔP/β ≈ 200e5 / 1.6e9 ≈ 1.25% at 200 bar.
The "stiff spring" in numbers

Because oil barely compresses, a hydraulic cylinder holds position under load far better than the springy pneumatic piston — you can clamp a die and walk away. But that same stiffness means trapped pressure has nowhere to go: thermal expansion in a blocked line can spike to dangerous levels, which is why circuits need relief and thermal-relief paths.

Page 4 · The Source

Pumps — Gear, Vane & Piston

A hydraulic pump creates flow, not pressure — pressure only builds when that flow meets a resistance (the load and the relief setting). The pump type sets the pressure ceiling, efficiency, noise and whether the flow can be varied.

As you move gear → vane → piston: pressure capability and efficiency rise, cost rises, and variable-displacement control becomes possible.

TypePressureTraitsTypical use
External gearup to ~250 barCheap, robust, noisy, fixed displacement, dirt-tolerantMobile machines, simple power packs

Fixed vs variable displacement

A fixed pump always delivers the same volume per rev — excess flow dumps over relief as heat. A variable (swashplate) pump tilts to deliver only the flow demanded — the foundation of energy-efficient load-sensing systems.

Pressure vs flow — the key reframing

Pumps are flow sources. Speed = flow ÷ area; pressure rises only to whatever the load needs, capped by the relief valve. Confusing "pump makes pressure" causes most beginner circuit errors.

Cavitation — the pump-killer

If the pump inlet can't draw oil fast enough (clogged suction strainer, cold thick oil, tank too low), pressure at the inlet drops below vapour pressure and bubbles form, then implode on the pressure side — pitting metal and roaring. Keep suction lines short, fat and clean, and warm the oil before full load.

Page 5 · The Valve Language

Valves — Directional, Pressure & Flow

Hydraulic valves split into three families by job: directional (where oil goes), pressure (how hard it pushes), and flow (how fast). Directional valves use the same ISO 1219 ports × positions notation as pneumatics — a 4/3 valve has 4 ports and 3 positions.

The centre condition decides what happens when both solenoids are off — a safety and energy decision, just like the pneumatic 5/3.

Open / tandem centre

P connects to T at centre → pump unloads to tank at low pressure. Saves energy & heat when idle; actuator floats.

Closed centre

All ports blocked → actuator locked, pressure held for other valves. Needed when one pump feeds several circuits.

Float / regen

Special centres connect both work ports together — letting a load free-float, or regenerating flow to speed extension.

Counterbalance — the valve that stops the drop

On a vertical load (a press ram, a boom), a counterbalance valve holds back-pressure on the return so gravity can't run the load away — the cylinder lowers only as fast as the pump feeds it. Forgetting it is a classic and dangerous error: the load "free-falls" the moment the directional valve opens.

Page 6 · The Actuators

Cylinders & Motors — Turning Pressure Into Work

As in pneumatics, a hydraulic cylinder has two areas — and the rod penalty matters even more here, because a regenerative circuit deliberately exploits it. For rotation, hydraulic motors are pumps run backwards.

Same areas as a pneumatic cylinder — but at 200 bar instead of 6, the forces are ~30× higher and the rod-side stiffness is what holds the load.

A_bore = π/4 · D² A_ann = π/4 · (D² − d²) F_extend = p · A_bore F_retract = p · A_ann Worked: D=100mm, d=56mm, p=200 bar = 200×10⁵ Pa A_bore = 0.7854·0.100² = 7.85e-3 m² → F_ext ≈ 157 kN (≈16 tonne) A_ann = 0.7854·(0.100²−0.056²) = 5.39e-3 m² → F_ret ≈ 108 kN Speed: v_ext = Q / A_bore (e.g. 30 L/min ÷ 7.85e-3 m² ≈ 0.064 m/s)

Differential / 2:1

Rod sized so A_bore = 2 × A_ann. Enables clean regenerative fast-extend: rod-side oil joins pump flow.

Telescopic

Nested stages for long stroke in a short retracted length — tipper trucks, cranes.

Hydraulic motor

Gear/vane/piston run in reverse — continuous torque. T = (p · D_m)/(2π), where D_m is displacement per rev.

Regeneration — free speed on extend

In a regen circuit, oil pushed out of the rod side is routed back to join the pump flow entering the cap side instead of going to tank. The cylinder extends faster (effective area = bore − annulus = rod area) but with proportionally less force. Brilliant for the rapid-approach stroke of a press before it switches to full-force pressing.

Page 7 · Stored Energy

Accumulators — The Hydraulic Battery

An accumulator stores pressurised fluid against a compressed gas (usually nitrogen) — a small hydraulic "battery." It is where pneumatics and hydraulics meet: a gas spring backing an oil volume. It does four jobs that a pump alone cannot.

Bladder, piston and diaphragm types exist. Pre-charge pressure (gas only) is set to ~80–90% of minimum working pressure.

Energy storage

Charge slowly, release fast — a small pump can serve a big peak demand (clamp surge, brief high-flow move). Downsizes the pump & motor.

Pulsation & shock damping

Absorbs pump ripple and the pressure spikes when a valve slams shut (water-hammer), protecting seals, gauges and pipework.

Emergency / hold

Holds a clamp or brake applied if the pump stops — a safety reserve. Also maintains pressure against slow leak-down.

Volume compensation

Makes up for thermal contraction and small leakage so the circuit stays "topped up" without the pump cycling.

Gas behaves by Boyle's law (slow) / adiabatic (fast): p₀·V₀ = p₁·V₁ = p₂·V₂ (isothermal, gentle cycling) Usable oil: ΔV = V₀·[(p₀/p₁) − (p₀/p₂)] where p₀ = pre-charge, p₁ = min, p₂ = max working pressure. ⚠ Discharge a hydraulic accumulator before any service — it is stored energy.
Safety: stored energy that bites

An accumulator stays dangerous after the power unit is off — there is high-pressure fluid waiting. Lock-out procedures require discharging the accumulator to tank (a manual dump valve) before opening any fitting. This is the hydraulic equivalent of the pneumatic soft-start/dump rule on A12.

Page 8 · The Real Enemies

Heat & Contamination — Why Systems Die

Hydraulic components rarely fail from over-design. They die from dirty oil and excess heat — the two issues a power unit is built to manage and the two most often neglected.

CONTAMINATION

~75–80% of all failures

Particles a few microns across erode spool clearances, jam valves and score pumps. Cleanliness is graded by ISO 4406 (e.g. 18/16/13). Filters are rated by β-ratio: β₁₀ = 200 means it removes 199 of every 200 particles ≥10 µm. Match filter rating to component sensitivity (servo valves demand the cleanest oil).

HEAT

Every wasted bar is heat

All pressure drop with flow becomes thermal energy. Oil above ~60 °C oxidises faster (life roughly halves per 10 °C rise), thins out, and lets metal touch metal. The cooler, the relief-dumping fixed pump and the reservoir surface all fight this.

75%+

Share of hydraulic failures rooted in fluid contamination — making filtration the highest-ROI maintenance task.

−50%

Approximate fluid & seal life lost for every ~10 °C the oil runs above its rated temperature.

ISO 4406

The three-number cleanliness code (≥4/6/14 µm). Spec it, measure it with particle counters, trend it.

Condition monitoring — the modern edge

Inline particle counters, water-in-oil sensors and oil-temperature probes feed SCADA/OPC-UA dashboards. Trending cleanliness and temperature predicts pump wear weeks before failure — one of the strongest Industry 4.0 use-cases in fluid power, exactly paralleling air-leak monitoring on A12.

Page 9 · Closing the Loop

Proportional & Servo — Hydraulics Goes Programmable

Classic hydraulics is on/off: shift the spool fully one way or the other. Proportional and servo valves break that — the spool moves proportionally to an electrical signal, so flow (and thus speed/position) becomes continuously controllable under closed-loop PID control.

An electro-hydraulic axis: the same closed-loop structure as an electric servo, but with oil delivering the muscle.

PROPORTIONAL

Good control, robust

A proportional solenoid positions the spool roughly in proportion to current. Some hysteresis & deadband, but tough and affordable. Runs ramps, speed profiles and pressure control on presses and mobile machines.

SERVO

Highest precision

A torque-motor / nozzle-flapper pilot gives fast, near-linear, low-hysteresis response with internal spool feedback. Demands the cleanest oil. Used in test rigs, flight controls, precise force/position loops.

Valve flow vs spool & pressure drop (square-root law): Q = K · x_spool · √(ΔP_valve) → flow is linear in spool position but non-linear in pressure. That √ΔP term is why electro-hydraulic loops need careful PID tuning, often with feed-forward to linearise the valve.
Why not just use an electric servo?

For force above roughly a few tonnes, hydraulics still wins on power-to-weight and overload tolerance — a servo-hydraulic press holds 1000 tonnes that no electric motor of that size could. The modern hybrid is the servo-pump (variable-speed electric servo driving a fixed pump): electric-drive efficiency and controllability with hydraulic force. It closes the loop between this deep dive and A09 Servo & Motion.

What actually goes wrong on the floor
⚠️ Slow / weak actuator

Pump worn (internal leakage), relief set too low, or flow control choked. Check case-drain flow and the relief setting before blaming the cylinder.

⚠️ Overheating oil

Fixed pump dumping over relief while idle, blocked cooler, or undersized tank. Add an unloading/open-centre path and clean the cooler.

⚠️ Spongy / drifting position

Air entrained in the oil, or internal valve leakage. Bleed the circuit; on a held load suspect a worn check or counterbalance valve.

⚠️ Noisy pump / cavitation roar

Starved suction: clogged strainer, cold thick oil, low tank, or a sucked-in air leak. Fix the inlet before the pump self-destructs.

Real Industrial Example — full circle to Micro 13

On a 150-tonne injection-moulding machine (the kind of press behind Micro 13), an axial-piston pump on an open-centre circuit feeds a clamp cylinder through a 4/3 proportional valve. An accumulator supplies the brief high-flow surge as the mould slams shut; a counterbalance valve stops the moving platen running away under its own weight; proportional control ramps the clamp force smoothly to protect the tool; a cooler and a β10=200 return filter keep the oil clean and below 55 °C; and a particle counter on OPC-UA flags the day the pump starts shedding metal — before it fails. Quick Check

A press cylinder has a 100 mm bore, 56 mm rod, runs at 210 bar, fed by a 40 L/min pump. (a) Estimate its extend force in tonnes and its extend speed. (b) The machine overheats after an hour of idling between cycles — name the most likely cause and the cheapest fix.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
(a) A_bore = π/4·0.100² = 7.85e-3 m². F = 210e5 Pa × 7.85e-3 = 164,900 N ≈ 16.8 tonne. Speed = Q/A = (40/60000 m³/s) ÷ 7.85e-3 = 6.67e-4/7.85e-3 ≈ 0.085 m/s. (b) A fixed-displacement pump dumping its full flow over the relief valve while idle turns all that power (≈ 210 bar × 40 L/min ≈ 14 kW) straight into heat. Cheapest fixes: switch the directional valve to an open/tandem centre so the pump unloads to tank at low pressure when idle, or add an unloading valve — far cheaper than a bigger cooler.
