🛠Hydraulic Systems

A hydraulic system is not a machine that makes force. It is a heat engine running backwards: every bar of pressure you did not intend to drop becomes a watt you must then remove, and the oil that carries that heat gets thinner as it does so — which opens every clearance in the circuit and makes the next bar worse. One equation, P = Q Δp / 600, governs the entire subject. This module derives it and seven other laws from first principles — compressibility, the √Δp valve law, the intensification ratio, the annular-clearance leak law, the 0.06 °C-per-bar heat signature, the accumulator polytropic, the softest-point stiffness minimum and the contamination feedback model — and walks one Sri Lankan rubber-moulding plant from LKR 1.46 million a month of invisible loss to a twenty-day payback, where 64 % of the money was hiding behind a blocked 6 mm pilot hose.

Four Faults, None of Them a Broken Component, and a Blocked 6 mm Hose That Cost Nine Hundred Thousand Rupees a Month

COLD OPEN — HORANA, SOLID-TYRE MOULDING BAY CORE

Four compression-moulding presses cure solid tyre bands on one shared hydraulic power unit. Each press is a single 320 mm bore / 180 mm rod cylinder on a four-column frame; the HPU carries a 400 L reservoir, a 63 cm³/rev fill pump and a 25 cm³/rev pressure pump, both fixed displacement at 1 450 rpm, an unloading valve, a 50 L bladder accumulator and an air-blast cooler. System pressure 210 bar. The bay runs two eleven-hour shifts, twenty-six days a month: 572 running hours.

Two instruments told the whole story, and neither of them is exotic. A clamp-on power logger on the HPU incomer. A PT100 in the reservoir. The logger said the unit averaged 42.8 kW. The PT100 said the oil sat at 75 °C. Nobody had looked at either number in nine months, because the presses were still making tyres.

The press cycle, derived rather than quoted (page 7 does the arithmetic): A_piston = π/4 × 0.320² = 804.25 cm² A_annulus = π/4 × (0.320² − 0.180²) = 549.78 cm² φ = A_p / A_a = 1.4629 fast approach 300 mm, regenerative 5.0 s slow close 20 mm, full bore 1.1 s pressure build to 210 bar 3.5 s cure dwell 480.0 s decompression + two bumps 14.0 s retract 320 mm 11.6 s eject, de-mould, re-load 17.0 s ------- CYCLE 532.1 s → 3 870 cycles/press/month

Notice what dominates: the press spends 90.2 % of every cycle doing nothing but holding still. A cure dwell is not an idle period — it is a period during which the machine must hold 210 bar and move essentially no oil. What the hydraulic system does during those 480 seconds is the entire economics of the bay, and it is the part nobody watches, because from the outside a press holding pressure and a press wasting 32 kW look and sound identical.

FAULT 1 — THE PUMP NEVER UNLOADS CORE 64.0 % OF THE LOSS

Nine months earlier a burst hose was replaced on the HPU. The fitter, working around a hot manifold, capped a 6 mm pilot line that ran to the unloading valve's remote vent port and never uncapped it. With the vent blocked, the unloading valve can no longer be told to open. The 63 cm³ fill pump therefore delivers its full flow over the system relief valve, at full system pressure, for the whole of every cure dwell.

A capped 6 mm hose. No noise, no alarm, no error code, no leak, no lost production — the presses kept cycling on time. The only outward symptom was that the oil ran hot, and hot oil in a Sri Lankan factory in June is what everyone expects anyway.

FAULT 3 — A CYLINDER DIAGNOSED AS A PUMP CORE 13.7 %

Press 2 could not hold cure pressure. Over the 480-second dwell the gauge sagged from 210 bar towards 180 bar before the accumulator and pump topped it back up, and the moulding specification calls for a minimum of 195 bar throughout. The maintenance contractor's diagnosis was “the pump is worn” and the quotation was LKR 1,100,000 for a rebuilt pump.

A borrowed thermal camera (see A41) settled it in four minutes. The pump body was 6 K above reservoir — normal. The cylinder cap end was 13 K above reservoir, and nothing else in the circuit was warm. That number is not a coincidence; it is a law.

Heat signature of an internal leak (derived on page 14): ΔT = Δp / (ρ c_p) with Δp in Pa ΔT per bar = 10⁵ / (870 × 1900) = 0.0605 K per bar at full system Δp 0.0605 × 210 = 12.7 K measured at the cylinder cap 13 K A component sitting exactly Δp × 0.06 above tank is dropping the whole system pressure inside itself. The piston seal was bypassing. The pump was innocent.

A pressure-decay test confirmed it: with the directional valve centred (closed centre) and the pump isolated, Press 2 fell from 210 bar to zero in 22 s; Press 1 lost 4 bar in the same time. The cost was never the pressure — it was the cure. Bands cured below 195 bar for part of the dwell came out porous at the bead, and the bay's scrap rate on that press ran at 1.8 % against a 0.4 % baseline on the other three.

excess scrap (1.8 − 0.4) % × 7 739 bands/month = 108.4 bands × LKR 1,850 contribution = LKR 200,452 / month and the LKR 1,100,000 pump rebuild that was not bought.

PREDICT FIRST
The oil in this reservoir ran at 75 °C instead of 40 °C. Beyond “seals age faster”, what is the most damaging immediate consequence for the machine's behaviour?
B — and the factor is exactly computable. ISO VG 46 with a viscosity index of 100 has ν = 46 cSt at 40 °C and 12.8 cSt at 75 °C (page 3 does this with the Walther equation). Laminar leakage through a clearance is inversely proportional to dynamic viscosity μ = ρν, so every internal leak path — pump clearances, spool laps, piston seals, pilot orifices — passes μ₄₀/μ₇₅ = 0.0393/0.0106 = 3.7 × the oil it passed cold. That leakage is itself a Δp × Q loss, so it becomes more heat, which lowers the viscosity further. This is a positive feedback loop, and it is why hot hydraulic systems do not merely run hot — they run away. A is true but slow (oxidation roughly doubles per 10 K, so 75 °C does halve oil life — it is a maintenance cost, not a behaviour change). C is wrong: bulk modulus is barely temperature-sensitive compared with viscosity, falling only about 15 % over this range. D is a real risk but a secondary one, and it depends on inlet conditions, not on temperature alone.

The One Equation, and the Two Laws Everybody Violates

P = Q Δp / 600 — WHERE IT COMES FROM CORE

Hydraulic power is mechanical power wearing a disguise. Push a piston of area A with pressure p and it delivers force F = pA. Move it at speed v and it delivers power F v = pAv. But Av is exactly the volumetric flow rate Q. So hydraulic power is p Q, always, everywhere, with no exceptions and no efficiency term hidden inside it.

P = F v = (p A)(Q/A) = p Q [Pa × m³/s = W] In the units engineers actually use: p in bar = 10⁵ Pa Q in L/min = 1/60000 m³/s P [W] = Δp × 10⁵ × Q / 60000 = Δp × Q × 1.6667 P [kW] = Q [L/min] × Δp [bar] / 600 Memorise the divisor. Six hundred. It is the only number in hydraulics you need to be able to use without a calculator.

Now the part that makes it the only equation you need. The same expression computes three completely different things depending on what Δp means:

If Δp means…then QΔp/600 is…Example from page 1
Pressure across the loadUseful work leaving the system91.35 L/min at 210 bar into a moving press = 31.97 kW of pressing
Pressure across a restrictionHeat, deposited in the oil, at 100 % conversion91.35 L/min over a relief valve at 210 bar = 31.97 kW of pure heat
Pressure across an internal leak pathHeat plus lost capacity5.8 L/min of case drain at 210 bar = 2.03 kW, and 5.8 L/min you no longer have

Look at rows one and two. They are numerically identical. A press doing 32 kW of useful work and a relief valve doing 32 kW of nothing draw the same current, make the same noise, and cost the same money. The hydraulic system cannot tell you which one it is doing. That is the whole reason page 1's fault survived nine months, and it is why the diagnostic discipline on page 14 exists.

THE TWO LAWS EVERYBODY VIOLATES CORE

Law 1: flow determines speed. An actuator's velocity is v = Q/A. Nothing else. Not pressure, not the relief setting, not how new the pump is. If a cylinder is slow, either the flow reaching it is less than you think or the area is bigger than you think. Raising the pressure setting cannot make it faster — it can only make it push harder while arriving late.

Law 2: the load determines pressure. A pump does not “make 210 bar”. It makes 91.35 L/min and then pushes that flow at whatever pressure is required to get it somewhere. Put the flow into free space and the pressure is near zero. Put it into a jammed cylinder and the pressure climbs until something gives — which is precisely why a relief valve is not an accessory but a structural necessity. The relief valve does not set pressure; it sets the maximum pressure the load is permitted to demand.

Corollaries you can use in an argument tomorrow: • A pressure gauge reads the load, not the pump's health. • A cylinder that stalls at 210 bar is not short of pressure. It is short of area, or the load is larger than the design. • A cylinder that is slow is short of flow, or has a bigger area than the designer's spreadsheet says. • The relief valve's own cracking pressure is the only place in the whole circuit where pressure is set by hardware. • Pressure travels at the speed of sound in oil (~1 350 m/s). Flow travels at 3–7 m/s. The command arrives 250× before the oil does — which is exactly why page 12 has a shock section.
WHY HYDRAULICS, AND WHEN NOT CORE

Hydraulics is a force-density technology. At 210 bar an actuator develops 2 100 N per square centimetre of piston. Pneumatics at 6 bar develops 60. An electric ball-screw actuator is limited not by pressure but by the Hertzian contact stress in the nut and the thermal limit of the motor winding, which in practice puts it an order of magnitude below hydraulics for the same envelope.

PREDICT FIRST
A 91 L/min fixed pump feeds a cylinder that needs only 60 bar to move its load. The relief valve is set at 210 bar. What pressure does the gauge at the pump outlet read while the cylinder is extending?
B — and this is Law 2 in its most useful form. While the cylinder is moving, the flow has somewhere to go, so pressure rises only to the value that pushes it there: the load pressure (60 bar) plus every Δp between the pump and the load — the directional valve, the lines, the filter. The relief valve is shut and passing nothing. It becomes relevant only at the instant the cylinder reaches the end of its stroke or stalls, at which point the flow has nowhere to go, the pressure climbs in milliseconds, and the relief opens to dump the entire 91 L/min at 210 bar. That is the moment 32 kW of heat switches on, and on a press it is 90 % of the cycle. A is the single most common misconception in the trade; C confuses pressure with flow (wear costs you flow, not pressure); D forgets that pushing a load requires pressure by definition.

The Fluid: Viscosity Is the Hidden State Variable, and Air Is the Hidden Spring

THE FLUID DOES FOUR JOBS AT ONCE CORE

Hydraulic oil is not a working medium with lubrication as a bonus. It is doing four jobs simultaneously, and every property it has is a compromise between them: it transmits power, it lubricates every sliding surface in the pump and the valves, it seals the clearances that make those components work at all, and it carries heat to the cooler. A change that helps one hurts another. Thin oil transmits power with less line loss and cools better, and leaks through every clearance. Thick oil seals and cushions, and starves the pump inlet and burns power in friction.

VISCOSITY UNDER PRESSURE — THE TERM EVERYONE DROPS STRETCH

Oil gets thicker when you squeeze it. The standard first-order model is the Barus relation:

μ(p) = μ₀ · e^(αp) α ≈ 1.5–2.5 × 10⁻⁸ Pa⁻¹ for mineral oil at 210 bar with α = 2.0×10⁻⁸: e^(2.0×10⁻⁸ × 21×10⁶) = e^0.42 = 1.52× at 350 bar: e^0.70 = 2.01×

A 52 % viscosity rise across the pump is not a rounding error, and it partly explains why real leakage is lower than the isoviscous clearance formula on page 5 predicts. It also matters enormously in the elastohydrodynamic contacts inside a piston pump — the slipper on the swash plate, the piston in its bore — where local contact pressures reach 1–2 GPa and the piezoviscous effect is what creates the load-carrying film at all. Without the Barus term, EHL theory predicts that piston pumps cannot work, and yet they do.

Where it bites in practice. If you are modelling a circuit and your leakage predictions are consistently 30–50 % high, you have almost certainly left the pressure term out of μ while keeping the pressure term in Δp. The two effects fight each other, and at high pressure the piezoviscous one wins more often than students expect.
BULK MODULUS — OIL IS A SPRING, AND AIR RUINS IT CORE

“Hydraulic fluid is incompressible” is a teaching lie that has to be retired the moment you care about response, stiffness or shock. Oil compresses about 0.7 % per 100 bar. That sounds negligible until you notice that page 1's press had to squeeze 0.96 litres of oil into a rigid mould before the platen made any pressure at all, and that this took two thirds of a second of the cycle.

READ THE CONVENTION BEFORE YOU USE THAT NUMBER CORE

In the equation above, Vair/Vtotal is the void fraction at the working pressure — the air actually present in the cylinder while it is loaded. That is the quantity control texts use, and it is the right one for stiffness and for ωh. It is not the number you get by looking at foam in the reservoir, because that air is at atmospheric pressure and will shrink as (1/p)1/n on its way in.

Take 1 % of air, and be explicit about where it was measured: 1 % present at 210 bar → β_eff = 1036 MPa (−35 %) 1 % measured at atmosphere, then carried to 210 bar: it shrinks to (1/211)^(1/1.4) = 2.19 % of its volume → void fraction in situ = 0.022 % → β_eff = 1581 MPa (−1.2 %) The same three words, "one per cent of air", give answers a factor of thirty apart. State the pressure the fraction refers to, every time.

Both readings are real and both matter. A system with a continuous suction leak genuinely runs with a percent or so of air in situ, because the pump keeps re-entraining it faster than the reservoir can release it — that is the 35 % case, and it is the one this page is about. A system that was merely bled badly at commissioning has a slug of atmospheric air that compresses away to nothing at working pressure — that is the 1.2 % case, and it will pass every stiffness test you can run at full pressure while still misbehaving on the low-pressure approach stroke.

That pressure dependence is the single most useful non-obvious fact about entrained air, and it explains a family of faults that otherwise look like witchcraft. A machine that is rigid and well-behaved at full pressure but spongy, oscillatory and slow to respond at low pressure is almost never a control problem. It has air in it, and because βair = n·p the air is 10.5 times softer at 20 bar than at 210 — which turns a 35 % stiffness loss into an 85 % one. Tuning the servo loop will not fix it; bleeding the circuit, fixing the suction-side leak that is aerating the oil, and giving the reservoir enough residence time will.

Where the air comes from, in order of frequency: 1. Suction-side air leak — a loose inlet flange or a porous suction hose. Draws air in without leaking oil out, so it is invisible. Suspect it first, always. 2. Return line above the oil level, splashing and entraining. Return must discharge below the surface, at <3 m/s, through a diffuser, on the far side of the baffle. 3. Too little residence time. Rule of thumb: reservoir volume ≥ 3–5 × pump flow per minute. 91 L/min wants 300–450 L. Page 1's plant has 400 L — correct, and one of the few things the original designer got right. 4. Cavitation at the pump inlet (page 5) — makes its own air by boiling the oil, then collapses the bubbles destructively. 5. Vortexing at the suction strainer when the level runs low.
Two-minute diagnosis. Draw a sample into a clear bottle and let it stand for five minutes. Oil that stays milky has entrained air and probably a poor air-release value; oil that clears from the top down had entrained air and is releasing it normally; oil that is milky and stays milky after an hour has water, not air. Cloudy plus a crackling pump equals suction-side leak until proved otherwise.

THE RUNAWAY: WHY HOT SYSTEMS GET HOTTER STRETCH

The balance on this page treated the heat load as a constant. It is not. Internal leakage — through pump clearances, spool laps, seal gaps — is laminar flow through a thin gap, and laminar flow is inversely proportional to dynamic viscosity. So the loop closes:

T ↑ → ν ↓ → Q_leak ↑ → Q_leak·Δp/600 ↑ → T ↑ Quantify it for page 1's oil, VG 46, from 40 to 75 °C: ν: 46.0 → 12.8 cSt leak multiplier 3.70× Write the balance with a temperature-dependent term: Q(T) = Q₀ + Q_leak,₀ · ν₀/ν(T) and Q(T) = k(T − T_air) The system is stable while dQ/dT < k. Because ν(T) falls roughly exponentially, dQ/dT grows with T — so every machine has a temperature above which the balance has no stable solution and the oil climbs until something changes: the cooler catches up on a shift break, a relief valve chatters open, or a seal fails.

This is the honest answer to “why did the machine seem fine for months and then get much worse in the last three weeks”. It did not degrade linearly. It approached the knee of a feedback curve, and on the far side of that knee small extra losses produce large temperature rises. It is the same mathematics as thermal runaway in a power semiconductor (A29) and in a battery cell (A39) — a loss mechanism whose gain rises with the temperature it produces.

Pumps I: Displacement, Three Efficiencies, and the Cube Law That Ends Machines

A PUMP MAKES FLOW, AND ONLY FLOW CORE

Every pump in industrial hydraulics is positive displacement: it traps a fixed volume and moves it, so its output is set by geometry and shaft speed and is almost independent of the pressure it is pushing against. That “almost” is where all of the engineering lives.

Theoretical flow Q_th = V · n V cm³/rev, n rev/min, Q L/min Q_th [L/min] = V × n / 1000 63 cm³/rev at 1 450 rpm = 91.35 L/min Theoretical torque T = V · Δp / (20π) T in N·m, V cm³/rev, Δp bar 63 cm³ at 210 bar = 63 × 210 / 62.832 = 210.6 N·m × 1450 rpm × 2π/60 = 31.98 kW — which is QΔp/600 again, as it must be. The two forms are the same statement.

Real pumps fall short of both, in two independent ways that must never be mixed:

Volumetric efficiency η_v = Q_actual / Q_theoretical Lost to internal leakage. Falls with pressure, falls with temperature, falls with wear. This is a FLOW loss. Hydro-mechanical efficiency η_hm = T_theoretical / T_actual Lost to friction — bearings, seals, viscous shear. Falls with viscosity RISING and with speed rising. This is a TORQUE loss. Overall η_t = η_v × η_hm typically 0.82–0.92 new They move in OPPOSITE directions with viscosity. That is why there is an optimum viscosity band at all (page 3), and why the band is a band and not a limit.
Why this distinction earns its keep. A pump that has lost volumetric efficiency makes the machine slow and hot and draws roughly the same current. A pump that has lost hydro-mechanical efficiency makes the machine hot and draws more current at the same speed. One clamp meter plus one stopwatch on a cylinder stroke separates them in under five minutes, and this is the difference between condemning a pump and finding the real fault, as page 1's fault 3 shows.
THE LEAKAGE LAW — WHY CLEARANCE IS EVERYTHING CORE

Internal leakage in a pump, a valve or a cylinder is laminar flow through a thin gap. For a concentric annular clearance of diameter D, radial gap h and land length L, integrating the Navier–Stokes equations for slow flow between near-parallel plates gives (page 19 derives it):

Q_leak = π D h³ Δp / (12 μ L) Three dependences, in descending order of how much they matter: • h³ — double the clearance, get eight times the leak • 1/μ — halve the viscosity, get twice the leak • Δp — linear, and the least interesting of the three Fully eccentric (the shaft resting on one side) multiplies it by 2.5. That is why a worn journal leaks far more than the average clearance suggests.

Now run page 1's pump through it. Nine pistons of 20 mm diameter in bores 35 mm long, at 210 bar:

NEW, cold h = 15 µm, μ = 0.0393 Pa·s (VG 46 at 40 °C) Q = π(0.020)(15e-6)³(21e6) / (12 × 0.0393 × 0.035) = 0.0162 L/min per piston × 9 = 0.146 L/min WORN, cold h = 30 µm, same oil h³ is 8× = 1.166 L/min WORN, HOT h = 30 µm, μ = 0.0106 Pa·s (VG 46 at 75 °C) and 1/μ is 3.70× on top of that = 4.312 L/min TOTAL FACTOR 29.6× A clearance you cannot see with the naked eye, in oil that is merely warm rather than hot, and the leak past the pistons alone has gone from 0.16 % of delivery to 4.7 %.

This is why case-drain flow is the best single wear measurement in hydraulics and why it must be trended, never spot-checked. A rising case drain means h is growing, and because the dependence is cubic, the trend accelerates. The industry rule — condemn at 10–15 % of rated delivery, investigate above 5 % — exists because by the time you reach 15 % the third power has already run away with you. Always record the oil temperature with the reading, or you will chase a viscosity change and call it wear.

Second, load sensing on a multi-actuator machine holds the pump at the highest demanded load plus margin, so a light actuator working alongside a heavy one still throttles away the difference. The cure is pressure-compensated valve sections or, better, independent metering — four electro-proportional valves per actuator instead of one four-way spool, which decouples inlet from outlet and is the most interesting active area in industrial hydraulics today. THE √Δp LAW — THE MOST-IGNORED LINE ON A DATASHEET CORE

A valve's metering land is an orifice. Flow through a sharp-edged orifice is not proportional to pressure drop — it is proportional to its square root, because the pressure difference becomes kinetic energy and velocity goes as √(2Δp/ρ):

Orifice equation Q = C_d · A · √(2Δp/ρ) C_d ≈ 0.6–0.7 for a sharp edge Catalogue form, which is the same statement: Q = Q_N · √(Δp / Δp_N) A rated flow means NOTHING without its rated pressure drop. Two NG10 valves, both printed “100 L/min”, in a circuit that can spare 10 bar across the valve: Valve A rated 100 L/min at Δp_N = 10 bar Q = 100 × √(10/10) = 100.0 L/min Valve B rated 100 L/min at Δp_N = 35 bar Q = 100 × √(10/35) = 53.5 L/min Same envelope, same bolt pattern, same headline number, 87 % difference in the machine. Manufacturers quote either Δp per metering land (usually 5 bar) or total valve drop (usually 10 bar, sometimes 35 or 70 for fine-metering spools). Read which. Convert before you compare.

Two consequences that decide whether a machine works:

• Speed is expensive in pressure. To double the flow through a given valve you need four times the pressure drop, and that drop is QΔp/600 of pure heat. Doubling the speed of a throttled axis multiplies its heat output by eight. • Available Δp is what is left over. Δp_valve = p_pump − p_load − Δp_lines − Δp_filter − Δp_backpressure On a load-sensing machine this is just the margin — 14–25 bar and no more, ever. Size every valve on an LS machine against the margin, not against the system pressure. Getting this wrong is why retrofitted LS machines come out slow.

COUNTERBALANCE AND THE OVERRUNNING LOAD CORE

An overrunning (aiding) load is one that wants to move in the direction you are driving it — a boom coming down, a platen descending, a winch paying out. The actuator no longer needs pressure to move; it needs resistance, or the load runs away and the supply side cavitates behind it. A counterbalance valve provides that resistance and lets the load descend only as fast as oil is supplied to the other side.

A CBV opens when: p_load + R × p_pilot ≥ p_setting R = pilot ratio, typically 3:1, 4.5:1 or 10:1 Boom cylinder, load-induced pressure 120 bar, CBV set at 1.3 × 120 = 156 bar: R = 3 : 1 p_pilot needed = (156−120)/3 = 12.0 bar R = 4.5 : 1 = (156−120)/4.5 = 8.0 bar R = 10 : 1 = (156−120)/10 = 3.6 bar Low ratio (3:1) needs more pilot pressure, so it wastes more energy — but it is much more stable. High ratio (10:1) is efficient and twitchy: a small pilot change swings the valve a long way, so the load bounces. Use high ratios only with a stiff pilot supply and a compliant load. Back pressure downstream of a CBV is added to its setting (unless it is externally drained). 10 bar of return-line back pressure on a 4.5:1 valve demands about 12.2 bar more pilot — which is why fitting a return filter can stop a boom from lowering, and why the fault always appears the week after a “routine” filter upgrade.

Why a counterbalance valve plus meter-out oscillates. Both devices are trying to control the same variable — the pressure holding the load back — through the same fluid column. The CBV's pilot responds to supply pressure, the supply pressure responds to the throttle, the throttle's flow responds to the CBV. That is a feedback loop with two lags (the pilot volume and the oil column) and almost no damping, and it hunts at a few hertz. Choose one. If the load is genuinely overrunning, use the counterbalance valve and meter the supply side; do not add a return throttle on top of it.

Pressure travels through oil at the speed of sound — about 1 350 m/s in a rigid steel pipe, less in a hose or with entrained air. The oil itself travels at 4–7 m/s. Stop that oil suddenly and its momentum has to go somewhere, and it goes into pressure:

The frame also stores elastic energy and must be let down gently, or the whole machine rings like a bell and the mould and tie rods take the load.
Pump pulsation is the other shock source and it is continuous rather than transient. A pump with z displacement elements at n rev/min excites the circuit at z n/60 Hz: page 1's nine-piston pump at 1 450 rpm pulses at 217.5 Hz, with strong harmonics. That is what you hear as the pump's whine, and if a line's quarter-wave resonance lands on it you get a standing wave, hammering fittings and cracked tube. Cures: an odd number of pistons (which is why nine and seven are standard), a short length of hose acting as a compliance, a dedicated pulsation damper, or moving the pump speed a few per cent.

THE MODEL — AND WHY THE SIMPLE ONE IS WRONG STRETCH CORE ARGUMENT

Write the obvious mass balance first. Particles enter at a rate R per minute; the filter removes a fraction η of everything that passes through it at flow Q_f. At steady state, removal equals ingression:

R = Q_f · η · N → N_ss = R / (Q_f η) Now test it against page 1. The return filter passes the full 91.35 L/min. Swapping β₁₀(c) from 200 to 2 changes η from 0.995 to 0.500, so the model predicts N_new / N_old = 0.995 / 0.500 = 1.99× = 1.0 ISO code The plant actually moved from 18/16/13 to 21/19/15 — three codes at 4 µm(c), a factor of 7.8. The model is not slightly wrong. It is wrong by a factor of four, and understanding why is the whole point of this page.

The missing term is internally generated debris. Wear rate is roughly proportional to the concentration of abrasive particles, so the particles the system already contains create more particles. Ingression is not a constant — it has a term that depends on the very quantity we are solving for:

R_total = R_ext + k · N (k has units of volume per time) Q_f η N = R_ext + k N N_ss = R_ext / (Q_f η − k) This is a completely different animal. It is a hyperbola with a pole, not a straight line. As the filter's cleaning capacity Q_fη approaches the self-generation coefficient k, the steady state runs to infinity — and below it, there is no steady state at all: the machine grinds itself to a stop. Fit k and R_ext to page 1's two measured states: OEM N = 1 800/mL at 4 µm(c) with Q_fη = 90 893 mL/min copy N = 14 000/mL with Q_fη = 45 675 mL/min → k = 39 003 mL/min R_ext = 93.4 million particles/min Now read what that means: with the OEM element margin = 90 893 − 39 003 = 51 890 with the copy element margin = 45 675 − 39 003 = 6 672 The copy element left the system with 13 % of its former cleaning margin. It was not half as good. It was operating a factor of eight closer to the pole, and one more small degradation — a hotter day, a worn wiper seal, a longer service interval — would have tipped it over.
This is the honest answer to a question that embarrasses the industry. Published filtration life-extension tables show benefits far larger than the linear model predicts, and are often presented without derivation, which makes engineers suspicious of them. The feedback term is why they are real. It also explains three field observations that the linear model cannot: that cleanliness improvements accelerate once started; that a machine can be stable for years and then deteriorate rapidly; and that offline (kidney-loop) filtration — which adds to Q_fη without touching the main circuit — is disproportionately effective for its cost.

THE HEAT-SIGNATURE LAW: 0.06 °C PER BAR CORE

When oil passes through a restriction with no work extracted, all the pressure energy becomes heat in that oil. Energy per unit mass in equals temperature rise times specific heat out:

per unit volume: Δp = ρ c_p ΔT ΔT = Δp / (ρ c_p) For mineral oil, ρ = 870 kg/m³, c_p = 1 900 J/(kg·K): ΔT = 10⁵ / (870 × 1900) = 0.0605 K per bar 30 bar → 1.8 K 150 bar → 9.1 K 70 bar → 4.2 K 210 bar → 12.7 K 100 bar → 6.1 K 350 bar → 21.2 K What it does NOT tell you: the flow. The temperature rise of the leaking stream is set by the pressure drop alone — a 0.2 L/min leak and a 20 L/min leak through the same Δp heat their own oil by exactly the same amount. What changes with flow is how much of the component gets hot, and how fast. What it DOES tell you, and nothing else does: a component whose surface sits Δp × 0.06 above the reservoir is dropping the full system pressure inside itself. That is a leak path, and you now know which box it is in, in about four minutes, with a camera you can borrow. See A41.

Page 1's fault 3 in one paragraph: the pump body read 6 K over tank, which is normal churn and case-drain loss for a healthy unit. The directional valve read 3 K. The cylinder cap end read 13 K, and 0.0605 × 210 = 12.7. Nothing else in the circuit was warm, so nothing else was dropping pressure. The LKR 1,100,000 pump quotation was withdrawn on the basis of one thermal image and a hand calculation.

QUANTIFYING IT: THE PRESSURE-DECAY TEST CORE

Thermal imaging localises. The decay test quantifies, and it needs nothing but a gauge and a stopwatch. Isolate the trapped volume, note the pressure, and time the fall. Because laminar leakage is proportional to pressure, the decay is exponential, not linear:

dp/dt = −(β/V) · Q_leak and Q_leak = C·p (laminar) → p(t) = p₀ e^(−t/τ) with τ = V / (β C) So measure τ from any two pressures and a time, then C = V / (β τ) and Q_leak(p) = C · p Page 1, trapped volume V = 54.75 L, β_eff = 1.2 GPa: PRESS 2 210 → 21 bar in 22 s τ = 22 / ln(210/21) = 22/2.3026 = 9.55 s C = 0.05475/(1.2×10⁹ × 9.55) = 4.78×10⁻¹² m³/(s·Pa) Q_leak at 210 bar = 6.02 L/min PRESS 1 210 → 206 bar in 22 s τ = 22 / ln(210/206) = 1 144 s Q_leak at 210 bar = 0.050 L/min A factor of 120 between them. Cross-checks that must all agree, and did: • heat: 6.02 × 210/600 = 2.11 kW inside one cylinder — which is what made the cap end 13 K hot. • make-up: 6.02 L/min × 8 min of dwell = 48 L, against an accumulator holding 6.5 L usable — so the pump HAD to cycle on repeatedly during every cure, which the operators had been reporting for months as “the pump keeps kicking in”. • pressure sag: consistent with the observed droop to 180 bar. Three independent measurements converging on one number is what a diagnosis is. One measurement is an opinion.
Getting β_eff right matters, and here is the trick. You rarely know β_eff for a real machine. But you do not need it in absolute terms if you are comparing two identical machines, because it cancels in the ratio — Press 2 versus Press 1 is a valid comparison whatever β is. And if you need the absolute number, you can measure β_eff on the same machine: record the oil volume the pump has to deliver to raise the pressure from 0 to p with everything blocked, and β = VΔp/ΔV. Page 1's press gave 0.958 L for 210 bar on 54.75 L, which is 1.20 GPa — and that measurement takes one flow meter and one gauge.

COMMISSIONING AND FLUSHING — THE STEP EVERYONE SKIPS STRETCH
A new system is the dirtiest it will ever be. Flushing is not “running it for a while”; it has an engineering criterion: flush at Reynolds > 4 000, ideally > 10 000, in every line because only turbulent flow scours the wall. Since Re = vd/ν, and ν falls with temperature, you achieve it by flushing hot and fast, with the actuators bypassed by jumper hoses and with a very fine offline filter in the loop. A 20 mm line needs v = 4000 × ν/d at 46 cSt (40 °C) v = 9.2 m/s → 173 L/min at 20 cSt (60 °C) v = 4.0 m/s → 75 L/min — which is why you flush hot: it can halve the pump you need to hire. Flush until two consecutive samples an hour apart meet the target class, then change every element and sample again. Budget 8–24 hours for a machine of any size. Then, and only then, connect the servo valves. Fitting a servo valve before flushing is the most expensive twenty minutes anyone saves on a commissioning schedule.

The same logic applies to every intervention afterwards. A hose changed with the port left open for ten minutes in a rubber factory has undone a quarter of a year's filtration. Cap every port the moment it is opened; filter the top-up oil in through a filter cart rather than pouring it through a funnel; keep the filter cart's own hoses capped when not in use. None of this is difficult and all of it is cultural, which is why it succeeds or fails at supervisor level rather than at engineer level.

SIX FAILURE MODES WORTH KNOWING BY NAME CORE
Silent failure to unload

An unloading or venting path blocked, mis-piloted or mis-set. No alarm, no symptom except heat and the electricity bill. Page 1, fault 1. Fit a pressure switch on the unloading line and alarm it.

Intensification

A restricted annulus multiplies pressure by φ. Destroys rod seals, gauges and cylinder ends, and is invisible on the system gauge. Fit a cross-line relief; check with a peak-hold gauge.

Cavitation erosion

Inlet starvation boils the oil; the bubbles collapse against the valve plate and port faces at enormous local pressure. Pitting, then metal in the oil, then everything else. Fix the inlet, never the symptom.

Silt lock

Fine particles pack into a spool clearance and hold the spool. Presents as intermittent, temperature-dependent and unrepeatable, and clears when you strip the valve — so it is diagnosed as “no fault found” and returns. It is a cleanliness fault, not a valve fault.

Bladder over-discharge

An accumulator discharged below its pre-charge slams the bladder onto the oil-port poppet and cuts it. Nitrogen then enters the circuit and the machine goes soft. Check pre-charge annually; fit the poppet guard.

Thermal runaway

Heat thins the oil, thin oil leaks more, leakage makes heat (page 4). Stable for months, then rapid. Watch the trend of reservoir temperature, not its value.

3. A piston pump's case drain has risen from 2.4 L/min to 5.6 L/min over a year. But the oil temperature at the two readings was 42 °C and 61 °C. ISO VG 46, VI 100. How much of the rise is wear?

The machine has a heat problem, and someone who condemned the pump on the raw case-drain numbers would have spent a million rupees on the wrong component and still had a hot machine. 4. A valve is rated 63 L/min at Δp_N = 10 bar. Your circuit needs 50 L/min through it and can spare 3.5 bar. Does it work? What would you need?

5. A 50 L bladder accumulator should be pre-charged to 0.9 × 160 = 144 bar. Two years later it measures 96 bar. The machine uses it between 160 and 210 bar, discharging in about 6 s. What has happened to the usable volume, and is that good news?

So it is bad news twice over — less usable oil and a bladder that now expands through a larger fraction of the shell on every cycle, which is what fatigues it. Check pre-charge annually with the oil side bled to zero.
6. A thermal camera shows a valve block sitting 9 K above the reservoir in a 200 bar system. What is happening inside it?
Tap to revealIt is dropping about 149 bar of the system's 200. Δp = ΔT/0.0605 = 9/0.0605 = 148.8 bar. That is not a valve doing its job — a healthy directional valve drops 3–8 bar and would read 0.2–0.5 K. Either a spool is stuck part-open, a relief inside the block is cracking, or the block contains a pressure-reducing or throttling function nobody told you about. What the temperature does NOT tell you is the flow, so it does not tell you the kilowatts — for that you need to know how much oil is going through, which is where the decay test or a flow meter comes in. Localise thermally, quantify hydraulically.
7. A machine returns 250 L/min through a 32 mm ID line. Is that acceptable? What size should it be?
Tap to revealNo — 5.18 m/s in a return line. It should be 42 mm ID. v = Q/A = (250/60000)/(π/4 × 0.032²) = 5.18 m/s, well above the 2–4 m/s return limit. Required: d = 4.61√(250/3) = 42.1 mm. Two consequences follow, and the second is the one people miss. First, the pressure drop is roughly (5.18/3)1.75 = 2.6 × what it should be, and that back pressure subtracts from actuator force. Second, oil arriving in the tank at 5 m/s entrains air (page 3), which softens the whole machine — so an undersized return line shows up as a control problem on a completely different part of the equipment.
8. Oil flows at 6.2 m/s in a 9 m line at 180 bar. A cartridge valve closes in 8 ms. What peak pressure appears, and would a 25 ms solenoid valve be better?
Tap to reveal253 bar peak, and yes, the slower valve helps a lot. Critical time t_c = 2L/c = 2 × 9/1350 = 13.3 ms. The cartridge closes in 8 ms, which is faster than t_c, so the full Joukowsky rise applies: Δp = ρcΔv = 870 × 1350 × 6.2 = 72.8 bar on top of 180, so 252.8 bar — above most 210 bar component ratings. A 25 ms solenoid closes in about 1.9 t_c, so the reflected wave returns before closure completes and the rise is roughly Δp × t_c/t_close = 72.8 × 13.3/25 = 38.7 bar — about half. Other cures: a bigger line (lower v), a shorter line (lower t_c is worse, so this one is a trap — shortening the line makes t_c smaller and full Joukowsky more likely; what shortening really buys you is a place to put the accumulator), or an accumulator within a metre of the valve.

9. A 60 L/min circuit has a self-generation coefficient k = 56 L/min-equivalent. Its filter is upgraded from β₁₀(c) = 20 to β₁₀(c) = 200. How much cleaner does the oil get? Compare with the naive answer.

The naive balance N ∝ 1/η gives 0.995/0.950 = 1.047 ×, i.e. 0.07 of an ISO code — not worth the money. The feedback model N = R/(Q_fη − k) gives margins of 60 × 0.950 − 56 = 1.00 and 60 × 0.995 − 56 = 3.70, so N falls by 3.70 × = 1.89 ISO codes. The lesson is about position, not about efficiency: this system was sitting almost on top of its pole, where a small change in cleaning capacity produces a large change in cleanliness. The same upgrade on a machine with k = 20 would buy almost nothing (margins 37 and 39.7, ratio 1.07). Ask where the machine sits relative to k before you price a filter upgrade.
10. A 160 mm bore / 90 mm rod cylinder rated 210 bar works in a 160 bar system. A technician fits a needle valve in the rod-side return to slow the extend. What is the worst-case annulus pressure?

The system relief cannot see this and will never open: it protects the P line, and the annulus is a separate, unprotected volume. The gauge on the manifold will read a perfectly innocent 160 bar throughout. 12. Synthesis. A plant manager tells you: “Our injection moulding machine's oil runs at 72 °C, the cycle time has drifted up 6 % in eight months, and we get a hydraulic fault alarm about twice a week. Please quote for a bigger cooler and a new pump.” You have one day on site, a clamp meter, an infrared thermometer, a peak-hold gauge, a stopwatch and a sample bottle. What do you do, and what do you refuse to do?
Tap to revealRefuse to quote for either item on the information given, and say why in one sentence: a cooler treats a symptom and a pump has not been shown to be faulty. Then do this, in order, and it fits inside a day.

Morning — measure, do not open anything. (1) Clamp the HPU incomer and log the whole cycle; look specifically for whether demand falls during clamp-hold and cooling. If it does not, you have page 1's fault 1 and you are already finished. (2) Tape measure on the tank plus the cooler nameplate; predict the oil temperature from the thermal balance (page 4) and compare with 72 °C. The gap in kilowatts is your budget for the rest of the day. (3) Infrared every component: anything sitting Δp × 0.06 above tank is a leak path (page 14). (4) Draw a bottle sample.

Afternoon — quantify what the morning found. (5) Case-drain flow with a bucket and stopwatch, recording oil temperature (problem 3 — at 72 °C the raw number will look alarming and probably is not). (6) Decay test on each cylinder. (7) Peak-hold gauge on the annulus port of any cylinder with a return throttle (problem 10). (8) Read the relief setting at full flow, not statically.

The 6 % cycle drift is the clue that should organise everything else. It is almost certainly viscosity: at 72 °C, VG 46 is at 14.0 cSt and every clearance in the machine is leaking about 3.4 × what it did at 40 °C, so the machine is genuinely slower and will get slower still. That makes it a consequence of the heat, not an independent fault — which is why fixing the heat has to come first and why re-measuring afterwards is mandatory. What you quote at the end of the day is a measurement report with three costed options and their assumptions, not a cooler. If the client insists on the cooler anyway, put the thermal balance in the quotation so that the decision is recorded as theirs.

DERIVATION 1 — THE ANNULAR CLEARANCE LEAK LAW STRETCH
Claim: Q = π D h³ Δp / (12 μ L) Take the gap between piston and bore. Because h << D, unwrap it into a flat slot of width b = πD, height h, length L. The flow is slow and steady, so inertia vanishes and the Navier–Stokes equations reduce to a balance of pressure gradient and viscous shear in one dimension: dp/dx = μ d²u/dy² (Stokes flow between plates) dp/dx is constant along the land, = −Δp/L. Integrate twice: u(y) = −(Δp/2μL)(y²) + C₁y + C₂ No-slip at both walls: u(0) = u(h) = 0, giving C₂ = 0 and C₁ = Δp h/(2μL). So the profile is the parabola u(y) = (Δp / 2μL) · y(h − y) Integrate across the gap and multiply by the width: Q = b ∫₀ʰ u dy = b(Δp/2μL)[h·h²/2 − h³/3] = b Δp h³ / (12 μ L) and with b = πD: Q = π D h³ Δp / (12 μ L) ∎ Three readings of the same result. • The h³ comes from integrating a parabola across the gap: one power of h from the area, two from the velocity scale. It is not empirical and it does not have exceptions. • Eccentricity. If the piston rests on one side, the gap varies as h(1 + εcosθ). Integrating h³ around the circumference gives a factor (1 + 1.5ε²), so a fully eccentric piston (ε = 1) leaks 2.5× the concentric value. This is why a worn or side-loaded component leaks far more than its average clearance suggests. • Validity. The result assumes laminar flow, which needs Re = ρuh/μ < ~1000. For h = 30 µm and Δp = 210 bar in hot oil, u ≈ 20 m/s and Re ≈ 45 — comfortably laminar. It also assumes the gap does not change under pressure, which is false for a thin-walled bore: real pumps grow their clearances slightly with pressure, which is one reason measured η_v falls faster than this formula predicts.
DERIVATION 2 — REGENERATION, AND WHY THE RECIPROCAL IS EXACT STRETCH
In a regenerative circuit the annulus discharge is fed back to the piston side, so both chambers sit at supply pressure p. FORCE. Pressure acts on A_p one way and A_a the other: F = p A_p − p A_a = p (A_p − A_a) = p A_rod — the net force is the pressure acting on the ROD's cross- section alone, which is why a regenerating cylinder is effectively a rod-area ram. SPEED. The pump supplies Q; the annulus returns v·A_a to the piston side; the piston side must swallow v·A_p: Q + v A_a = v A_p → v = Q / (A_p − A_a) = Q / A_rod RATIOS, against normal extend (v₀ = Q/A_p, F₀ = pA_p): v/v₀ = A_p/(A_p − A_a) = φ/(φ − 1) F/F₀ = (A_p − A_a)/A_p = (φ − 1)/φ Their product is exactly 1, so P = Fv is unchanged — which it must be, because the pump is delivering the same Q at the same p. Regeneration does not create power; it re-gears it. Page 1's press: φ = 1.4629 speed × 1.4629/0.4629 = ×3.1605 force × 0.4629/1.4629 = ×0.3164 and 3.1605 × 0.3164 = 1.0000 ∎ Design note. The ratio is set by the rod diameter alone. A 2:1 area ratio (d = 0.707D) gives exactly 2× speed at ½ force, which is why 2:1 cylinders are made specifically for regenerative presses — the switch-over is then a clean halving.
DERIVATION 3 — WHERE A DIFFERENTIAL CYLINDER IS SOFTEST STRETCH
Two oil columns act as springs in parallel through the piston. With the rod extended by x from the cap end, stroke L, and ignoring dead volumes: V₁ = A_p x V₂ = A_a (L − x) k(x) = β(A₁²/V₁ + A₂²/V₂) = β(A_p²/(A_p x) + A_a²/(A_a(L−x))) = β( A_p/x + A_a/(L−x) ) Minimise: dk/dx = β( −A_p/x² + A_a/(L−x)² ) = 0 A_a x² = A_p (L−x)² x/(L−x) = √(A_p/A_a) = √φ x_min / L = √φ / (1 + √φ) ∎ Check the symmetric case: φ = 1 gives x/L = ½, and then k = β(A/(L/2) + A/(L/2)) = 4βA/L = 4βA²/V_t, the textbook form. Page 1's press: φ = 1.4629, √φ = 1.2095 x_min/L = 1.2095/2.2095 = 0.5474 → 493 mm of 900 Second derivative is positive there, so it is a minimum, and because the curve is shallow near it the practical rule “design for mid-stroke and add 10 %” is safe. But note the asymmetry direction: the soft point is always on the ROD side of centre, never the cap side, because the annulus is the smaller area and needs more length to match the piston's stiffness. On a 2:1 cylinder it is at 0.586 L.
DERIVATION 4 — THE HEAT SIGNATURE, AND WHY IT IGNORES FLOW STRETCH
Take a control volume around any restriction — an orifice, a leaking seal, a relief valve. Steady flow, no shaft work, no heat crossing the boundary in the short term, negligible change in height and (for a liquid) negligible change in kinetic energy between inlet and outlet. The steady-flow energy equation reduces to constant enthalpy: h₁ = h₂ → u₁ + p₁/ρ = u₂ + p₂/ρ For a nearly incompressible liquid, internal energy depends only on temperature: Δu = c_pΔT. So c_p ΔT = (p₁ − p₂)/ρ ΔT = Δp / (ρ c_p) ∎ Mineral oil: ρ = 870 kg/m³, c_p = 1900 J/(kg·K) ΔT = 10⁵/(870 × 1900) = 0.0605 K per bar Note what is absent from the result: the mass flow rate. It cancelled, because both the energy dissipated and the mass it is dissipated into are proportional to it. A 0.1 L/min leak and a 100 L/min leak through the same Δp heat their own fluid by exactly the same amount. What flow DOES control is the observable: how much heat per second arrives at the component's mass, hence how quickly and how far its surface temperature rises above the surrounding metal, and how strong the signal is against convection to the air. So: • the temperature RISE OF THE STREAM tells you Δp • the SURFACE temperature of the component tells you whether the flow is big enough to overcome that component's own cooling • neither tells you the flow — for that, page 14's decay test, or a flow meter. Practical corollary: a very small leak at high Δp may be invisible to a thermal camera even though its stream is 12.7 K hot, because there is not enough of it to warm the casting. Thermal imaging finds leaks that matter; it does not prove the absence of leaks that do not.
