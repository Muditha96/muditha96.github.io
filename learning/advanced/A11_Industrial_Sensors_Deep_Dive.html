The Sensor Was Right. The Line Stopped Anyway.

MONDAY MORNING, A COCONUT MILK LINE NEAR KATUNAYAKE

Every Monday, for the first forty minutes of the shift, the filler underfills. The checkweigher rejects about 3% of packs. By ten o'clock the problem has vanished. It comes back the following Monday.

The plant blames the level sensor in the buffer tank — a capacitive probe that tells the filler when to top up. Maintenance tests it on the bench: it reads perfectly. They replace it anyway. The fault returns. They replace it again, with a different brand. The fault returns.

Nothing is broken. The sensor is doing precisely what it was designed to do. The probe's switch point depends on the dielectric constant of what surrounds it, and coconut milk that has stood cold over the weekend has a different dielectric constant — and a different foam layer — from warm product circulating mid-shift. The calibration was set on a Friday afternoon, in warm foamy product. On Monday morning the switch point has quietly moved by about nine millimetres, and nine millimetres of tank is forty minutes of underfilled packs.

Three shifts, two new sensors, one contractor visit, and roughly LKR 400 000 of rejected product — spent because nobody read the sentence in the datasheet about dielectric constant. That sentence is on page 9 of this module.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Before you read anything else — on that line, which single action would have found the fault fastest?

B. The fault is time-varying and condition-dependent, so any test that takes a snapshot — a bench test, a shift calibration, a spot comparison — will find nothing, which is exactly what happened three times. Only a continuous record spanning the condition change can show the switch point moving. This is the single most useful habit in sensor troubleshooting: a sensor that tests good but behaves badly is telling you the fault lives in the conditions, not the device.

D is the runner-up and would have worked if the second sensor used a different physical principle — two capacitive probes would have drifted together and confirmed each other's error beautifully. That is a real and expensive trap: redundancy only reveals a fault if the redundant element fails differently.

If you picked A or C, you are in good company — those are the two things the plant actually did.

The Sensing Chain

A measurement is not a device. It is a chain of five stages, and the number that arrives in your controller carries the errors of all of them.

process transduce condition transmit acquire error injected
THE THESIS OF THIS MODULE CORE

The sensing element is usually not the weakest link. The chain is.

Engineers buy accuracy at stage 2 — a better probe, a tighter tolerance class — and then destroy it at stages 3, 4 and 5 with a badly chosen filter, an unshielded cable routed beside a drive, or an analogue input card scanned once a second. In the worked example on page 16 you will see a chain where the sensing element contributes 74% of the total variance, which makes it the right thing to improve; you will also see, in the problem set, a chain where improving the sensor by a factor of two improves the measurement by 4%. Knowing which situation you are in is the entire skill, and it takes about ten minutes with a spreadsheet.

💼

This is the single most valuable habit in the module. “Which term dominates?” is a question you can ask about an error budget, a cost breakdown, a downtime Pareto or a safety calculation — and the engineer who asks it consistently gets noticed.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A Pt100 transmitter is specified at ±0.1 °C. It is wired to an analogue input card specified at ±0.1% of a 0–16 mA span, scaled to 0–200 °C. What does the card contribute, in °C?

B — ±0.2 °C, twice the sensor's error. The card's specification is a percentage of its own span, not of the temperature:
0.1% × 16 mA = 0.016 mA, and the scaling is 200 °C / 16 mA = 12.5 °C per mA, so 0.016 × 12.5 = 0.2 °C.

This is the most common and most expensive mistake in specifying a measurement chain: paying for a premium transmitter and then feeding it into a standard input card that doubles the error before the number reaches the controller. It is also why every specification must be converted into the same engineering unit before anything is compared. Percentages of different spans cannot be compared, added or ranked — and yet they are, constantly.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A pressure transmitter is quoted as ±0.25% of span, ranged 0–10 bar. Your process normally runs at 1 bar. What is the error as a percentage of what you are actually reading?

B — 2.5% of reading. 0.25% × 10 bar = 0.025 bar, and 0.025 / 1.0 = 2.5% of the value you care about. The transmitter is ten times worse than its datasheet appears, purely because it is over-ranged by a factor of ten.

This is the argument for ranging a transmitter to the process, not to the pipe rating. It is also why “% of reading” devices (Coriolis meters, many analysers) are worth their premium on wide-turndown duties, and why over-specifying the range “to be safe” is one of the most common ways engineers make a measurement worse while believing they are making it more robust.

The same arithmetic, in a different costume, is what breaks orifice-plate flow measurement below about 30% of range — page 10.
COMBINING ERRORS — RSS, NOT ADDITION STRETCH

Independent errors do not all reach their maximum at the same instant in the same direction. Adding them arithmetically assumes they do, which is pessimistic to the point of being useless. Because variances add, independent contributions combine in quadrature — the root-sum-square:

uc = √( u₁² + u₂² + u₃² + … ) ← independent (random) terms Systematic terms that always act the same way — a known installation effect, an uncorrected head of liquid — are added arithmetically, or better, corrected for.

The property that makes RSS so useful is dominance. Suppose one term is three times another. Then

√(3² + 1²) = 3.162 against 3.0 alone The smaller term added just 5.4%. It is, for practical purposes, invisible — and money spent improving it is money wasted.

This single result governs how you should spend an instrumentation budget: find the largest term and attack that one. Halving the second-largest contributor typically improves the total by a percent or two. Halving the largest can transform it. You will use this again in the LOPA arithmetic of A63, in reliability chains, and in every cost breakdown you are ever asked to review — it is the same mathematics each time.

💼

An error budget on one A4 page — every contribution in the same unit, ranked, with the RSS at the bottom — is the most persuasive document a young instrument engineer can bring to a meeting. It converts “I think the sensor is bad” into “the sensor is 74% of our variance and here is what fixing it buys.”

Uncertainty, Traceability & Calibration

What separates a number you can defend from a number you merely believe.

UNCERTAINTY IS NOT ERROR CORE

Error is the difference between your reading and the true value — and you can never know it, because you do not know the true value. Uncertainty is the size of the interval within which the true value plausibly lies.

The internationally agreed framework (the GUM — Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement) divides contributions into two types by how they were evaluated, not by their nature:

TypeEvaluated byTypical sources here
Type AStatistical analysis of repeated observations — you took twenty readings and computed the standard deviationRepeatability of the sensor, process noise, operator variation
Type BAny other means — a calibration certificate, a datasheet limit, engineering judgement, a handbook valueReference standard uncertainty, resolution, drift since last calibration, temperature effects

Both are converted to standard uncertainties (equivalent to one standard deviation) before combining. A datasheet limit of ±a quoted as a rectangular distribution becomes u = a/√3; a certificate quoted at k = 2 becomes u = U/2. Only then may they be combined by RSS. The expanded uncertainty is U = k·uc, almost always with k = 2, giving roughly 95% confidence — and a result without its k value is not a result.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Your calibrated reference thermometer has an expanded uncertainty of ±0.5 °C (k = 2). You are using it to verify a transmitter whose tolerance is ±1.0 °C. Is that acceptable?

B. The test uncertainty ratio is TUR = tolerance / expanded uncertainty of the measurement = 1.0 / 0.5 = 2:1. Standard practice (and most quality systems) requires at least 4:1; below that, a significant fraction of the pass/fail decisions you make are decided by your own reference's uncertainty rather than by the device under test. At 2:1 you will fail good instruments and pass bad ones at a rate you cannot quantify without applying guard-banding.

C is tempting and wrong: repeat readings shrink the Type A component only. The reference's certificate uncertainty is Type B — a systematic offset you cannot average away. Taking a thousand readings with a biased ruler gives you a very precise wrong answer.

D matters, but traceability is a separate requirement: an unbroken chain of comparisons back to a national standard, each link with a stated uncertainty. Traceable but inadequate is still inadequate.
THE CALIBRATION THAT ACTUALLY TELLS YOU SOMETHING CORE

A single-point check tells you almost nothing. The standard industrial practice is a five-point calibration, up and down: 0, 25, 50, 75, 100% of span rising, then falling. Five points expose linearity; the return leg exposes hysteresis; doing it twice exposes repeatability.

Record as-found data before adjusting anything, and as-left data after. The as-found record is the valuable one, and it is the one most often skipped:

  • 1It tells you whether the interval is right. As-found consistently inside tolerance → the interval can be extended, saving money. As-found outside → shorten it, or change the device.
  • 2It bounds the damage. If a transmitter is found 1.8 °C high, every batch made since the last calibration was made with that error. Without as-found data you cannot say how much product is affected — which in food, pharma or export manufacturing is a recall question, not an engineering one.
  • 3It is the evidence. An auditor asking “how do you know your process ran within specification?” is asking for as-found records, not for a sticker on the instrument.

Calibrate the loop, not just the sensor. Injecting a known resistance at the transmitter proves the transmitter; it proves nothing about the wiring, the input card, the scaling in the PLC or the number on the operator's screen. The only honest test walks the whole chain: apply a known physical input at the sensor and read the value on the HMI.

💼

Export-oriented plants in Sri Lanka — apparel, tea, rubber, food — are audited against ISO 9001 and customer standards that require exactly this evidence. An engineer who can set up a defensible calibration programme, with intervals justified by as-found data rather than by habit, is solving a commercial problem, not just a technical one.

The ramp result is the one that surprises people. A sensor with τ = 12 s watching a process ramping at 2 °C/min reads (2/60) × 12 = 0.4 °C low, permanently — not while it settles, but for as long as the ramp continues. No calibration will find this, because in steady state the sensor is perfect.

WHERE THE TIME CONSTANT ACTUALLY COMES FROM STRETCH

For a thermal sensor, τ is a ratio of stored energy to transferred power:

τ = m cp / (h A) m cp = thermal mass of everything that must change temperature h A = heat transfer coefficient × area available to transfer it

Every practical consequence follows from that one expression:

Thermowells dominate

A bare 1.5 mm thermocouple has τ of order 1–3 s. The same element in a heavy stainless well can be 20–90 s — the well's mass is now in the numerator.

Fluid velocity matters

h rises with flow. The same sensor is several times faster in fast-moving liquid than in stagnant air — so a datasheet τ without stated conditions is meaningless.

Air gaps are fatal

A sensor not touching the bottom of its well, or a well without heat-transfer paste, has a tiny h. This one installation detail routinely multiplies τ by five.

Fouling is a slow ambush

A coating adds mass and thermal resistance, so τ grows quietly over months. The loop degrades without any component failing — exactly the fault diagnosed in A63.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A temperature sensor has τ = 12 s. Your process oscillates with a period of 60 seconds. How much of that oscillation's amplitude does the sensor show you?

B — 62%, lagging by 51.5°.
ω = 2π/60 = 0.1047 rad/s  →  ωτ = 1.257
AR = 1/√(1 + 1.257²) = 0.62    phase = −arctan(1.257) = −51.5°

Two consequences, both serious. First, the real oscillation is 1.6× bigger than your trend shows — the product is worse than the chart. Second, in a closed loop that 51.5° of phase lag is subtracted directly from your phase margin, so the sensor has quietly consumed most of a typical 60° budget and the loop will oscillate at a gain that the process alone would have tolerated easily.

A is the instinct almost everyone has — “60 is much bigger than 12, so it is fine”. The correct comparison is not period against τ, it is ωτ against 1, and ω brings a factor of 2π with it. That factor of 6.28 is where the intuition fails.

Inductive & Capacitive Sensing

The two workhorses of factory automation — one sees metal only, the other sees almost anything, and that difference is the source of both their value and their trouble.

oscillating field eddy currents electrostatic field dielectric target
INDUCTIVE — HOW IT WORKS CORE

An LC oscillator drives a ferrite-cored coil, producing an alternating field in front of the face. A conductive target entering that field has eddy currents induced in it; those currents dissipate energy as heat in the target, which appears to the oscillator as extra loss — a drop in the tank circuit's Q. The oscillation amplitude falls, a comparator with deliberate hysteresis detects the fall, and the output switches. No contact, no moving parts, no wear, and typically tens of millions of operations.

Because the mechanism is energy loss in the target, sensing distance depends on the target's conductivity and permeability. The datasheet's headline figure assumes mild steel; everything else is reduced:

Note the counter-intuitive ordering: the better conductors sense at shorter range, because eddy currents in a good conductor stay near the surface and dissipate less energy at these frequencies. Ferrous targets add magnetic hysteresis loss on top, which is why steel wins.

THE FOUR SENSING DISTANCES — AND THE ONLY ONE YOU MAY DESIGN TO STRETCH
Sn nominal — the catalogue figure. Mild steel, standard target, 20 °C, ideal. Sr real — this individual unit: Sn ±10% Su usable — across the full temperature and voltage range: Sr ±10% Sa assured — guaranteed to operate: 0 … 0.81 × Sn The standard target is a square of mild steel, 1 mm thick, with a side equal to 3 × Sn (or the sensor face diameter, whichever is larger). A smaller target reduces range further.

Design to Sa, then apply the material factor, then leave margin. For an 8 mm flush sensor detecting a stainless steel flag: Sa = 0.81 × 8 = 6.48 mm, then × 0.7 = 4.5 mm. Setting the gap at 3 mm gives comfortable margin for mounting tolerance, thermal growth and vibration. Setting it at 7 mm — “the datasheet says 8” — produces a sensor that works on the bench, works at commissioning, and starts missing targets on a hot afternoon three months later.

Hysteresis of 3–20% of Sn is built in deliberately: the release point is further away than the operate point. Without it, a vibrating target sitting exactly at the switch point would chatter the output at hundreds of hertz. Flush (shielded) sensors can be embedded in metal but have shorter range; non-flush (unshielded) sensors reach further but need a specified clear zone around the face — mounting one flush in a steel bracket is a classic self-inflicted permanent-on failure.

CAPACITIVE — AND THE MONDAY-MORNING FAULT, EXPLAINED CORE

A capacitive sensor makes one plate of a capacitor its face and lets the target — or the environment — be the other. Anything entering the fringing field changes the capacitance:

C = ε₀ εr A / d εr = relative permittivity of whatever is in the field A = effective plate area, d = separation

Because εr is in the equation, the sensing distance depends on what the target is made of — not merely on whether something is there:

Now the coconut milk line. Water's relative permittivity falls appreciably as temperature rises — roughly 80 near 20 °C down towards the low 60s at 70 °C — and a product's effective εr shifts further with fat content, solids and entrained air in foam. The probe's switch point sits where the total capacitance crosses a threshold, so a lower εr means the product must come closer to trip it. Calibrated in warm foamy product on Friday, the threshold corresponded to one level; in cold, settled, denser product on Monday it corresponded to a level roughly nine millimetres different. Nothing failed. The physics simply did what the equation says it does.

The fixes, in order: use a sensor with automatic compensation or a different principle entirely (guided-wave radar does not care about εr nearly as much — page 9); calibrate in the worst-case product condition rather than the convenient one; or move the measurement to a point where the product condition is controlled. The fix that does not work is buying a more accurate capacitive sensor.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A conveyor carries stainless steel cans at 0.8 m/s. Each can presents a 30 mm target. Your inductive sensor responds in 1 ms. The PLC scans the input every 20 ms. Will every can be counted?

B — and it will miss cans intermittently, which is worse than missing them consistently.
target present = 0.030 m / 0.8 m/s = 37.5 ms
to guarantee capture: T ≤ W/2 = 18.75 ms, but the scan is 20 ms → FAILS

The sensor's own 1 ms response was never the constraint — and that is the point. The sampling stage of the chain (page 2, stage 5) sets the limit, and it does so by the same Nyquist argument that governs analogue signals. At 20 ms the count will be right most of the time and wrong occasionally, producing the most expensive kind of fault: one that is intermittent, blamed on the sensor, and “fixed” by replacing hardware.

Three real fixes: a 10 ms task, a latching or high-speed counter input that captures the edge in hardware regardless of scan, or a physically longer target flag. C is a common misconception — ordinary PLC inputs sample the state at the scan boundary, they do not remember pulses in between.

Photoelectric & Ultrasonic

Sensing across a gap. Light gives you range and precision; sound gives you indifference to colour and transparency. Both are ruined by the same thing — the medium in between.

through-beam retro-reflective diffuse ultrasonic
EXCESS GAIN — THE ONE NUMBER THAT PREDICTS RELIABILITY CORE

Range is the specification everyone reads. Excess gain is the one that decides whether the sensor still works in six months:

excess gain = light received at the detector / light needed to switch 1× threshold — works only in a clean laboratory 1.5× clean air, light dust 5× light dust, mild condensation, some misalignment 10× factory air, regular airborne dust 50× heavy dust, steam, oil mist — wash-down food plant

Excess gain falls roughly with the square of distance, so a sensor used at half its rated range has around four times the margin. Design for excess gain, not for range: a through-beam sensor rated at 20 m used at 4 m in a dusty mill will outlive a diffuse sensor rated 400 mm used at 380 mm, and cost less in call-outs than the difference in purchase price.

For diffuse sensors, contrast ratio matters as much: the received signal from the object divided by that from the background. Matt black cardboard returns roughly 10% of what white paper returns, so a diffuse sensor set up on a white product will simply not see a black one. Background suppression (BGS) sensors solve this by triangulating on the angle of return rather than its intensity, giving a sharp distance cut-off almost independent of colour — and they are the correct default for anything on a conveyor with a fixed background behind it.

ULTRASONIC — THE FOUR CONSTRAINTS STRETCH

Ultrasonic sensing times an echo: d = c·t/2. Simple, non-contact, indifferent to colour and transparency — and constrained in four specific ways that you must check before specifying one:

1 · The speed of sound moves

c ≈ 331 + 0.6T m/s in air. From 20 °C to 50 °C, c goes 343 → 361 m/s — a 5.2% range error if uncompensated. Every serious sensor has a temperature probe; check that it is measuring the air in the path, not the sensor's own housing.

2 · The blind zone

The transducer must stop ringing before it can listen, so nothing can be measured within roughly 5–15% of the range. Mount high enough that a full vessel never enters it — a level sensor that reads “empty” when the tank is full is this fault, and it has caused overflows.

3 · Beam angle and false echoes

The beam is a cone, typically 5–15°. Anything inside it — a ladder, an agitator shaft, a weld seam, a tank wall — can return a stronger echo than the product. Map the cone before mounting; use a stilling well if the vessel is crowded.

4 · Foam, dust and vacuum

Foam absorbs ultrasound and returns nothing; heavy dust scatters it; and sound needs a medium, so a vacuum service is impossible. These are the cases where radar earns its price — page 9.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A diffuse photoelectric sensor reliably detects white cartons at 400 mm. Production switches to a matt black carton of identical size. What happens?

B. A diffuse sensor switches on returned intensity, and matt black returns of the order of 10% of what white returns. The excess gain that was comfortable at 400 mm on white collapses below the switching threshold on black, and detection becomes marginal — usually intermittent, which is far harder to diagnose than an outright failure.

The reflex fix is to turn up the sensitivity potentiometer. That works for a week and then the sensor starts seeing the conveyor belt behind the product, and you have swapped a missed-detection fault for a permanently-on fault.

The correct answers: a background suppression sensor (switches on distance, so colour barely matters), or a through-beam arrangement (the object's colour is irrelevant when it simply blocks the beam). This is precisely why BGS should be your default on any conveyor where the product can change — and product always changes.

The most measured quantity in industry, and the one with the most ways to be quietly wrong.

RTD — THE PRECISION CHOICE CORE

Platinum's resistance rises with temperature in a way that is stable, repeatable and very nearly linear. The international standard (IEC 60751) defines the Callendar–Van Dusen relation:

t ≥ 0 °C : R(t) = R₀[ 1 + A t + B t² ] t < 0 °C : R(t) = R₀[ 1 + A t + B t² + C(t − 100) t³ ] A = 3.9083×10⁻³ B = −5.775×10⁻⁷ C = −4.183×10⁻¹² α = 0.003851 Ω/Ω/°C (the European / IEC curve) Pt100 checkpoints: 0 °C → 100.00 Ω 100 °C → 138.51 Ω 150 °C → 157.33 Ω 200 °C → 175.86 Ω Sensitivity near 0 °C: 0.391 Ω per °C

That last number is the key to two of the three classic RTD errors, because it converts every stray ohm into degrees.

Lead resistance

A two-wire Pt100 adds the cable's resistance directly to the element's. Just 0.39 Ω of loop resistance = 1 °C of error — about 16 m of 1.5 mm² twin cable. Use 3-wire (cancels matched leads) or 4-wire (true Kelvin, cancels them completely).

Lead mismatch

3-wire only cancels if the two measurement legs are equal. A 0.05 Ω mismatch — one crimp resistance, one different cable length — leaves 0.05/0.391 = 0.13 °C of uncorrectable error. This is why 4-wire is specified for reference-grade work.

Self-heating

The excitation current heats the element: P = I²R. At 1 mA in a Pt100 at 100 °C, P = 0.14 mW — negligible. At 5 mA, P = 3.46 mW, and at a typical 0.3 °C/mW in still air that is +1.0 °C of pure self-inflicted error. Worse in air than in flowing liquid, so it changes with process conditions.

Tolerance classes

IEC 60751: Class AA ±(0.10 + 0.0017|t|), Class A ±(0.15 + 0.002|t|), Class B ±(0.30 + 0.005|t|). At 150 °C that is ±0.36, ±0.45 and ±1.05 °C. Buying Class A instead of B is usually the cheapest accuracy you will ever buy.

THERMOCOUPLES — AND THE ONE IDEA THAT EXPLAINS EVERY MISTAKE CORE

A thermocouple does not measure temperature at its tip. Two dissimilar conductors joined at both ends generate an emf proportional to the temperature difference between the junctions (the Seebeck effect) — and, importantly, the emf is generated along the length of wire that lies in the gradient, not at the junction itself.

So every thermocouple reading is: Thot = f(measured emf) + Tcold junction, and the cold junction must be measured by another sensor — usually a thermistor or RTD on the terminal block. An error of 1 °C in the cold-junction measurement is an error of 1 °C in your reading, at any process temperature. That single sentence accounts for most thermocouple trouble in industry.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A type K thermocouple runs 40 m from a furnace to the panel. To save money the contractor uses ordinary copper cable for the last 30 m, joined in a junction box that sits in the hot end of the plant. The reading is wrong. Roughly how wrong, and why?

B — and the error moves with the weather and the time of day, which makes it maddening to diagnose.

The copper joint has accidentally created a new cold junction inside the hot junction box. The instrument still applies cold-junction compensation using the temperature at the panel terminals, but the thermocouple pair actually ends in the junction box. The reading is therefore in error by approximately the difference between those two locations. A junction box at 55 °C and a panel at 25 °C gives roughly 30 °C of error.

Thermocouple resistance is almost irrelevant to accuracy (A is wrong) because the instrument's input impedance is very high — it measures emf, not current. And this is a bias, not noise (D is wrong): the reading is smooth, plausible and consistently wrong, which is the worst combination.

The rule: the thermocouple alloys must continue unbroken all the way to the point where cold-junction compensation is performed. Use type K extension wire, respect the polarity, and put any junction box in a thermally quiet place.
THERMISTORS, INFRARED AND THE THERMOWELL STRETCH

Thermistors are semiconductor devices whose resistance falls steeply with temperature (NTC). Sensitivity is enormous — several percent per degree against the RTD's 0.39% — giving excellent resolution and cheap electronics, but the relationship is strongly non-linear and the usable range is narrow (typically −50 to +150 °C). The standard model is Steinhart–Hart:

1/T = A + B·ln(R) + C·(ln R)³ (T in kelvin) or the simpler beta form: R = R₀ · exp[ β (1/T − 1/T₀) ]

They dominate exactly where their weaknesses do not matter: cold-junction compensation on thermocouple terminals, motor winding protection (PTC types), battery packs, HVAC and appliances.

Infrared measures emitted radiation, so it is the only option for moving, rotating, live-electrical or unreachable targets — and its dominant error is emissivity, exactly as in thermography. Set ε for the surface, distrust shiny metal, and remember that the reading is an average over the spot size, which grows with distance. A41 covers this in full.

The thermowell deserves its own paragraph because it is a mechanical component that decides a measurement. Beyond the dynamic penalty already covered, two design rules matter: insertion depth must be enough that conduction along the well to the flange does not drag the tip towards ambient — a rule of thumb is at least 10 times the well diameter into the fluid, and never less than the pipe's boundary layer; and wake frequency must be checked, because a well in flowing fluid sheds vortices and can be excited into resonance and snap off. That calculation is standardised in ASME PTC 19.3 TW, and on high-velocity steam service it is not optional.

Pressure & Level

One sensing element, several jobs — and the page that finally solves the coconut milk problem properly.

THE PRESSURE ELEMENT ITSELF CORE

Almost all industrial pressure transmitters are a diaphragm whose deflection is converted to an electrical signal by one of three effects: piezoresistive (silicon strain elements diffused into the diaphragm — high output, strongly temperature dependent, therefore always compensated); capacitive (the diaphragm is one plate — excellent stability and overrange tolerance, the workhorse of DP transmitters); or resonant (a vibrating element whose frequency shifts with stress — outstanding stability, frequency output immune to analogue drift, and expensive).

Four reference types, and confusing them is a classic commissioning error: gauge (relative to atmosphere, vented), absolute (relative to vacuum), differential (between two ports) and sealed gauge (relative to a sealed reference at manufacture — drifts with altitude and weather). A gauge transmitter with a blocked vent reads the weather; it is a real and surprisingly common fault.

Two specifications matter more than accuracy on real plants. Overrange / burst pressure: the DP cell measuring a few hundred millibars across an orifice must survive full line pressure on one side when a manifold valve is operated in the wrong order — which is why the equalising valve must be opened before either isolating valve is closed. And static pressure effect: a DP cell's zero shifts by a specified amount per bar of line pressure, so a cell calibrated on the bench at atmospheric pressure will read differently at 40 bar. Both belong on the datasheet checklist.

DP LEVEL — WET LEGS, ELEVATION AND SUPPRESSION STRETCH

Hydrostatic level is Δp = ρgh. On an open tank with the cell at the bottom this is trivial. On a closed tank the vapour space pressure must be subtracted, so a reference leg runs to the top — and if that leg fills with condensate (a wet leg), it applies a constant negative bias that must be calibrated out.

Tank 3.0 m tall · product SG 1.03 · wet leg filled to 3.5 m with SG 1.0 Δp = ρproduct g h − ρleg g hleg EMPTY: 0 − 3.5 = −3.50 mH₂O (−343 mbar) ← LRV FULL : (1.03 × 3.0) − 3.5 = −0.41 mH₂O (−40 mbar) ← URV SPAN = 3.09 mH₂O = 303 mbar, with the zero elevated by −3.50 mH₂O

Two consequences worth remembering. First, the transmitter never sees a positive pressure in normal operation — a technician who “corrects” the negative zero has just destroyed the calibration. Second, if the wet leg loses fluid, the reading rises and the tank appears fuller than it is, which is the dangerous direction. Fill the leg with a low-volatility fluid, check it on a schedule, or eliminate it with a remote-seal (capillary) transmitter.

The fundamental limitation of every hydrostatic method: it measures ρh, not h. If density changes — temperature, concentration, entrained air, a different product grade — the level reading changes even though the level did not. On a plant that makes several products in one tank, DP level is systematically wrong by the density ratio unless it is compensated with a temperature or density input.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A batching system doses syrup by volume using a turbine meter, calibrated in litres. Production complains that winter batches taste different from summer batches. What is happening?

B — the classic volume-versus-mass error, and it costs food and beverage plants real money every year.

A recipe is a statement about mass (or moles). A volumetric meter delivers litres, and litres of a liquid whose density varies with temperature contain different masses. Syrup's density change over a 15 °C seasonal swing is easily a percent or more — enough to shift sweetness, viscosity and yield measurably, and enough to put a nutritional declaration out of tolerance.

There is a further and nastier effect with turbine meters specifically: their calibration factor is viscosity dependent, and syrup viscosity changes far more steeply with temperature than its density does. So the winter batch is wrong twice over — once because the litres carry more mass, once because the meter is no longer counting litres correctly.

Fixes, in ascending order: temperature-compensate the volumetric reading (cheap, needs a good density model); dose by weight using load cells on the vessel (robust, and page 12's dead-load caution applies); or use a Coriolis meter and measure the mass directly — which is exactly why Coriolis dominates food, pharmaceutical and custody-transfer dosing despite the price.

That last line is a real design gate. A high-resolution encoder on a fast shaft can exceed a standard PLC high-speed counter (often 100–200 kHz) and will simply lose counts — silently, and cumulatively, so the position error grows all shift and resets only at homing.

Resolution is not accuracy. A 4096-count encoder resolves 0.088°, but its accuracy also depends on disc eccentricity, bearing play, coupling wind-up and thermal growth of the mechanism. On a machine with a 20:1 gearbox and a toothed belt, the encoder is usually the most accurate component in the chain by a wide margin — and the position at the tool is still out by millimetres.

Signal type decides cable length

Open-collector or push-pull outputs are fine for a few metres. Beyond that use RS-422 line-driver (differential) outputs — common-mode noise from drives is rejected, and runs of tens of metres become reliable. Retrofitting this after a noise problem means new cable and often a new encoder.

Absolute removes a whole class of accident

Single-turn absolute knows the angle; multi-turn also counts revolutions, mechanically or with a battery. No homing sequence means no chance of homing into a fixture, and no lost position after a power dip — often worth more than the price difference on a machine that stops frequently.

Digital interfaces

SSI, BiSS and EnDat send the position as a data word with error checking, so a corrupted reading is detected rather than believed. On a safety-related axis this matters far more than resolution.

Where encoders lose

Heat, shock, oil mist and washdown. In those places a resolver — effectively a rotating transformer with no electronics in the sensor — survives where an optical encoder will not, at lower resolution and with a resolver-to-digital converter needed at the drive.

A cut-to-length machine uses an incremental encoder. Over one shift the cut lengths drift progressively longer. Overnight the machine is powered off; the next morning it starts perfectly, then drifts again. Most likely cause?

Two mechanisms produce it. Count rate exceeding the counter input — the encoder is genuinely producing more edges per second than the module can register at peak speed; the cure is a lower-PPR encoder, a faster counter, or ×1 rather than ×4 decoding.

C is worth testing but would not reset overnight in that pattern. The deeper lesson: an incremental system's position is a running total of a long series of assumptions. Force, Strain & Load Cells

FROM STRAIN TO MILLIVOLTS CORE

A bonded foil strain gauge is a thin metal grid glued to a surface. Stretch the surface and the grid gets longer and thinner, so its resistance rises. The proportionality is the gauge factor:

GF = (ΔR/R) / ε ε = strain = ΔL/L (dimensionless, quoted in µε) metal foil gauges: GF ≈ 2.0 semiconductor gauges: GF ≈ 100–200 (huge output, poor temperature stability) Typical working strain in a steel load cell: 1000 µε = 0.001 → ΔR/R = 2 × 0.001 = 0.002 = 0.2% On a 350 Ω gauge that is a change of just 0.7 Ω.

Detecting a 0.2% change in a resistance, repeatably, to a fraction of a percent of itself, is not something you do with an ohmmeter. That is what the bridge is for:

quarter bridge (1 active arm): Vout/Vex = ¼ · GF · ε → 0.5 mV/V full bridge (4 active arms): Vout/Vex = GF · ε → 2.0 mV/V which is exactly why almost every industrial load cell in the world is specified as 2 mV/V. It is not a convention — it is 1000 µε of steel through a full bridge of GF-2 gauges.

At 10 V excitation, 2 mV/V means a full-scale output of 20 mV. Everything about load cell wiring follows from that number: the signal is tiny, so the cable is screened and twisted, the excitation must be stable (or ratiometric measurement used, where the ADC references the excitation itself and supply drift cancels), and sense leads (the six-wire configuration) measure the excitation at the cell so that voltage drop in a long cable does not appear as a weight error.

LOAD CELLS IN A REAL VESSEL STRETCH

Four 500 kg cells under a mixing vessel give a system capacity of 2000 kg. Suppose the vessel's own tare weight is 600 kg and it doses 100 kg additions of an ingredient.

System capacity = 4 × 500 = 2000 kg Combined error, say ±0.02% of full scale = 0.0002 × 2000 = ±0.4 kg On the 900 kg batch: 0.4/900 = 0.04% — excellent On a 100 kg addition: 0.4/100 = 0.4% — ten times worse On a 10 kg minor: 0.4/10 = 4.0% — useless

This is the dead-load penalty, and it is the single most common weighing design error. The cells are sized for the vessel; the accuracy that matters is on the smallest addition. Fixes: size the cells as close to the maximum gross load as safety allows; dose minor ingredients from a separate, small-capacity weigh hopper; or use loss-in-weight feeders for the small components. No amount of calibration improves a system whose resolution is set by a tare it must carry all day.

Four mechanical rules matter as much as the electronics:

  • 1Every attached pipe is a load path. Rigid pipework carries force into or out of the vessel, and the cells cannot tell that force from product. Use flexible connections, and route them so thermal expansion does not push on the vessel.
  • 2Restrain horizontally, not vertically. Stay rods and check rods stop the vessel moving sideways under agitation or wind without taking vertical load. Getting this wrong shows up as a weight reading that changes when the agitator starts.
  • 3Creep and temperature. A load cell under constant load drifts slowly (creep), and its zero and span move with temperature — compensated in good cells, but check the coefficients if the vessel sits in the sun in Colombo.
  • 4Shunt calibration for verification. Switching a precision resistor across one bridge arm simulates a known load, letting you verify the electronics and cabling without lifting test weights — useful, but note it does not verify the mechanical installation, which is where most weighing faults actually live.

Where weighing is used for trade — selling a product by weight — the whole system falls under legal metrology (OIML R76 / ISO and national weights-and-measures regulations), with defined accuracy classes, a minimum number of verification scale intervals, and mandatory periodic verification by an approved body. That is a compliance requirement, not an engineering preference, and it must be identified at the specification stage.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A weigh vessel reads correctly at rest, but the reading jumps by several kilograms whenever the agitator starts. The cells and electronics test perfectly. Where is the fault?

The separating test: run the agitator drive with the vessel isolated from the mechanical disturbance — or, more practically, start the VFD with the motor's mechanical output uncoupled or the vessel empty and quiescent. If the reading jumps with no mechanical excitation, it is electrical. If it only jumps when the vessel is actually being stirred, it is a load path. A second, faster test: watch the raw millivolt signal on a scope. Electrical interference looks like noise or a step at switching frequency; a mechanical load path produces a smooth, repeatable offset that tracks the disturbance.

If electrical: screened twisted cable bonded at one end only, separated from the drive's motor cable, sense leads used, and the drive's own EMC installation checked (screened motor cable with 360° glands — the same discipline as the bearing-current problem in A63).
If mechanical: flexible pipe connections, correctly designed stay rods that take only horizontal load, and a check that no ladder, conduit or insulation is bridging the vessel to the floor.

WHY CURRENT, NOT VOLTAGE CORE

Four properties, and no competing analogue standard has all of them:

Immune to cable resistance

Current is the same at every point in a series loop. Voltage drop in the cable changes nothing the receiver sees — a voltage signal would be attenuated by the same drop.

Live zero

4 mA means “zero, and I am alive”. 0 mA means broken. A 0–10 V signal cannot distinguish minimum reading from a cut wire — a distinction that has prevented real accidents.

Powers the device

A two-wire transmitter takes its supply from the same loop that carries its signal. Two conductors do everything, which halves the field wiring.

Low impedance = noise immunity

A current loop's low impedance makes induced noise voltages far less significant than they would be in a high-impedance voltage circuit.

The loop voltage budget is the calculation that must be done before ordering anything, and the one most often skipped:

Vsupply(min) ≥ Vtransmitter(min) + I·(Rbarrier + Rsense + Rcable + Rindicators) With 22.8 V, a 12 V transmitter, a 300 Ω barrier and a 250 Ω sense: 22.8 − 12 − 6.0 − 5.0 = −0.2 V → FAILS before any cable Reduce the sense resistor to 100 Ω: 22.8 − 12 − 6.0 − 2.0 = +2.8 V → Rcable(max) = 2.8/0.020 = 140 Ω → with 1.5 mm² cable at 24.2 Ω/km loop: 5.8 km Without the barrier and with 250 Ω: 290 Ω allowed → 12 km.

Selection, Installation & Diagnostics

Everything so far was physics. This page is the part that decides whether the installation survives its first monsoon.

THE SEVEN QUESTIONS, IN ORDER CORE
  • 1What decision does this measurement drive? Control, alarm, protection, record, or curiosity. A measurement with no decision attached should not be installed — and one that drives a trip has an entirely different specification from one that drives a trend.
  • 2What range does the process actually use? Not the pipe rating, not the vessel design pressure. Range to the process (page 3) or accept a tenfold loss of accuracy.
  • 3How fast must it tell me? Compare the required response with the process time constant (page 5). This question eliminates more candidate devices than accuracy ever does.
  • 4What varies that I am not trying to measure? Density, dielectric constant, temperature, viscosity, conductivity, colour, foam. This is the question that would have saved the coconut milk line, and it is the one most often skipped.
  • 5What is the environment? Temperature, washdown, vibration, dust, corrosion, hazardous area classification — and, in Sri Lanka, humidity, salt air near the coast, monsoon ingress and lightning.
  • 6Who maintains it, with what, and how quickly? A device that needs a proprietary handheld from an agent in another country is a poor choice however good its datasheet. Local spares and local skills are engineering criteria.
  • 7How will I know it has failed? The diagnostics question — and the subject of the rest of this page.

Three installation rules cause more failures than any component defect: cable entry orientation (always form a drip loop so water runs away from the gland, not into it); thermal siting (a transmitter in direct afternoon sun can sit 20–30 °C above ambient, well outside its compensated range — a simple sunshade is one of the highest-return items in an instrument budget); and vibration (mount transmitters on a bracket, not on the pipe, and use flexible impulse lines — a rigid capillary on a vibrating line will fatigue and fail).

DIAGNOSTICS — THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN WRONG AND KNOWN-WRONG STRETCH

A dumb sensor has two states as far as the control system is concerned: a plausible number, or an implausible one. A smart sensor can tell you why. NAMUR NE 107 standardises this into four status signals that every modern device should report, and the value of the scheme is that each status has a different audience:

And it must be routed to the right person: sending “maintenance required” to the operator's alarm list is how a plant ends up with the alarm flood problem, while sending “failure” only to a maintenance database is how a loop keeps controlling on a dead sensor. The commonest failure of smart instrumentation is not technical — it is that the diagnostics were bought and never configured.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A high-temperature trip on a dryer uses two independent sensors, voted 1oo2 (either one trips). One is an RTD, one is a thermocouple, both in the same thermowell. Is this good redundancy?

B — the technology diversity is excellent and the installation throws it away.

The sensing elements are diverse, so they will not both drift the same way or fail in the same electrical mode: that part is well designed. But they share a single mechanical path to the process. If the thermowell fouls, both read slow. If it is installed too shallow, both read low. If the well cracks, floods or is fitted without the tip touching, both are affected identically. And if someone withdraws the well for maintenance, both sensors leave the process together.

Common cause is what redundancy analysis is actually about. Duplicating a device attacks random failure; only separation and diversity of the whole path attacks common cause — and as A63 shows numerically, once the common-cause term dominates, further duplication buys almost nothing.

The fix: two separate thermowells, at different points on the vessel, on different flanges, with different cable routes and, ideally, different maintenance schedules so the same technician does not have both out at once. Also note that C is a trap in the opposite direction: calibrating both on the same day by the same person with the same reference creates a common cause rather than removing one.

Recap, Failure Modes & Recall

Eight ideas, six ways sensors betray you, and three questions to check that it stuck.

THE EIGHT IDEAS CORE
  • 1A measurement is a chain, not a device. Five stages, each adding error; the controller cannot tell them apart.
  • 2Convert every specification into the same engineering unit before comparing anything. Percentages of different spans are not comparable numbers.
  • 3Errors combine in quadrature, so the largest term dominates. Find it, attack it, and ignore anything a third of its size.
  • 4A sensor is a dynamic element. τ = mcp/hA; the ramp lag is Rτ for ever; you see 1/√(1+(ωτ)²) of a cycling process.
  • 5Know the physical effect and you can predict the failure mode. Capacitive sensing depends on εr; hydrostatic level depends on ρ; ultrasonic depends on the speed of sound; diffuse photoelectric depends on colour.
  • 6Ask what varies that you are not trying to measure. Every classic sensor fault in this module is that question, unasked.
  • 7Sampling is part of the sensor. A 1 ms proximity switch on a 20 ms scan is a 20 ms sensor — and T ≤ W/2 or you will miss events.
  • 8Redundancy without diversity is decoration. Two sensors in one thermowell fail together, and the common-cause term sets the floor.
SIX WAYS SENSORS BETRAY YOU
⚠ It tests good and behaves badly

A snapshot test cannot find a condition-dependent fault. If the bench test passes but the plant misbehaves, log the output continuously across the condition change — the fault lives in the conditions, not the device.

⚠ It is accurate and too slow

A fouled well, a heavy thermowell, an over-generous filter. Steady-state calibration is perfect; every transient and every ramp is wrong, and the control loop pays the phase margin.

⚠ It measures the wrong physical quantity

Volume when the recipe means mass; ρh when you wanted h; εr-weighted level when the product changed. The number is right; it is an answer to a different question.

⚠ It is over-ranged

±0.25% of a 10 bar span is ±2.5% of a 1 bar reading. The device meets its specification perfectly while being useless, and the datasheet defends it.

⚠ The signal is corrupted after the sensor

Earth loops, unshielded runs beside drive cables, a wrong extension wire, a shield bonded at both ends. The measurement was correct when it left the transmitter.

⚠ It fails silently

An energise-to-trip switch, a sensor with no diagnostics, or NE 107 status that was bought and never configured. The most dangerous sensor is not a wrong one — it is a dead one that still looks plausible.

Q1 · Name the three numbers you would extract from any temperature sensor datasheet before agreeing to buy it — and say why each one, not the others.
Tap to reveal ↓ (i) The tolerance expression, with the temperature dependence spelled out — e.g. Class A ±(0.15 + 0.002|t|), because at your actual operating temperature this may be twice the headline figure. (ii) The time constant, with the conditions it was measured in — a τ quoted in flowing water is a different device from the same τ quoted in still air, and this number decides whether the loop can be controlled. (iii) The self-heating coefficient and recommended excitation current (for an RTD) or the cold-junction compensation accuracy (for a thermocouple) — because each is a whole degree of error that no calibration will find.

Notice what is not on the list: resolution, display digits, and the accuracy of the transmitter considered separately from the element. All three chosen numbers are ones that change with your installation.
Q2 · A colleague says: “the analyser is only accurate to 2%, so there is no point controlling this loop tighter than 2%.” Is that right?
Tap to reveal ↓ No — it confuses accuracy with repeatability. If the analyser's 2% is largely a fixed bias, then the loop can still hold the process to the analyser's repeatability, which may be 0.2%. The product will be consistently 2% away from where you think it is, but it will be consistent — and consistency is what a control loop delivers and what variability-reduction money buys.

The practical consequence is important and slightly counter-intuitive: for closed-loop control, repeatability matters more than accuracy; the bias can be removed later by an occasional laboratory correction (the inferential-with-lab-bias architecture). For custody transfer, regulatory declaration or a safety trip, the opposite holds — accuracy and traceability are the whole point.

So the correct reply is a question: “is the 2% bias or scatter?” The datasheet usually distinguishes them, and if it does not, that is itself informative.
Q3 · Without looking back: why does a 60-second process oscillation get attenuated to 62% by a sensor with a 12-second time constant, when 60 seconds seems comfortably slower than 12?
Tap to reveal ↓ Because the comparison is not period against τ — it is ωτ against 1, and ω carries a factor of 2π.
ω = 2π/60 = 0.105 rad/s → ωτ = 1.26 → AR = 1/√(1+1.26²) = 0.62

The −3 dB point sits at ωτ = 1, which corresponds to a period of 2πτ = 75 s — so a 60 s oscillation is already faster than the sensor's corner frequency. The rule of thumb worth carrying: a first-order sensor starts significantly attenuating anything with a period shorter than about 6τ, and by the time the period reaches τ you are seeing under a sixth of the truth.

If you got this without looking, you have the single most transferable result on the page — it is the same mathematics as a filter, a thermowell, a damped gauge and an analogue input's smoothing.

WE4 · THE DEAD-LOAD PENALTY IN A WEIGH VESSEL STRETCH

Given: four 500 kg cells under a vessel of 600 kg tare, 2 mV/V at 10 V excitation, combined system error ±0.02% of full scale. Batches are 900 kg gross, including a 100 kg minor addition and a 10 kg additive. Find: the signal levels and the accuracy of each addition.

System capacity = 4 × 500 = 2000 kg Full-scale output = 2 mV/V × 10 V = 20 mV (the whole signal) Signal at 900 kg = 20 × 900/2000 = 9.0 mV Combined error = 0.0002 × 2000 = ±0.4 kg (constant, in kg) on the 900 kg batch : 0.4/900 = 0.04% ✓ excellent on the 100 kg minor : 0.4/100 = 0.4% ≈ borderline on the 10 kg additive: 0.4/10 = 4.0% ✗ unusable

Note that the error is fixed in kilograms and therefore grows without limit as a percentage of smaller additions — the identical structure to the over-ranged pressure transmitter on page 3. The vessel's 600 kg tare consumes 30% of the capacity before any product is added, and buys nothing.

The design response: dose the 10 kg additive from a separate small weigh hopper (a 25 kg-capacity system at the same 0.02% gives ±0.005 kg = 0.05%); size the main cells as close to the maximum gross as safety and the manufacturer's overload rating allow; and specify the accuracy requirement per addition in the URS rather than as a single figure for the vessel. Also verify the signal level: 9 mV full working signal means the electronics, the cable screening and the excitation stability all matter, and a 24-bit ADC's specification should be read as noise-free resolution, not as raw bits.

P1  [6 marks] · THE TWO-WIRE RTD

A Pt100 is wired two-wire over 40 m of 0.5 mm² copper (resistivity 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m). Calculate the error in °C and state the fix.

Tap to reveal ↓ R per conductor per metre = 0.0172/0.5 = 0.0344 Ω/m
loop = 2 × 40 m = 80 m → R = 80 × 0.0344 = 2.75 Ω
error = 2.75 / 0.391 Ω/°C = +7.0 °C

Seven degrees, reading high, permanently — on a sensor whose element is accurate to ±0.45 °C. The lead resistance is fifteen times the element's own tolerance, which is why two-wire RTD connections are acceptable only for very short runs or for rough indication.

Fix: three-wire (cancels matched leads, leaving only the mismatch — a 0.05 Ω difference leaves 0.13 °C), or four-wire for reference-grade work. Better still: mount a head-mounted transmitter on the sensor and send 4–20 mA or HART down the 40 m, so cable resistance becomes irrelevant (page 13). That is usually cheaper than the extra cores.
P2  [6 marks] · COLD-JUNCTION ARITHMETIC

A type K thermocouple system has a cold-junction compensation error of 2 °C. (a) What is the reading error at a process temperature of 100 °C? (b) At 1000 °C? (c) Express the 2 °C as a voltage.

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) 2 °C. (b) 2 °C. (c) about 82 µV.

The answer to (a) and (b) is the point of the question: a thermocouple measures the difference between its junctions, and the instrument adds the cold-junction temperature to recover an absolute value. An error in that addition is a constant offset in kelvin, independent of the process temperature. It is 2% of the reading at 100 °C and 0.2% at 1000 °C — which is why thermocouples look relatively better at high temperatures and why they are a poor choice for precise work near ambient.

(c) 2 °C × 41 µV/°C ≈ 82 µV — a voltage so small that a poor terminal connection, a temperature gradient across the terminal block, or noise from a nearby drive cable can produce it. Keep the terminal block isothermal, keep it out of draughts and sunlight, and never let the alloy pair end anywhere except at the compensation point.
P3  [8 marks] · ENCODER SPECIFICATION

A ball-screw axis has a 10 mm pitch and is driven at up to 3000 rpm. A 2500 PPR incremental encoder is fitted to the motor shaft, feeding a counter module rated at 200 kHz. (a) Linear resolution? (b) Count rate at full speed? (c) Verdict and fix.

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) counts/rev = 2500 × 4 = 10 000
    resolution = 10 mm / 10 000 = 0.001 mm = 1 µm

(b) 3000 rpm = 50 rev/s
    count rate = 50 × 10 000 = 500 000 counts/s = 500 kHz

(c) The counter is rated 200 kHz. It will lose counts above about 1200 rpm — silently, cumulatively, and only at high speed, so the machine will position perfectly during slow commissioning moves and drift during production. This is exactly the shift-long drift diagnosed on page 11.

Fixes, in order of preference: (i) use a counter or drive input rated above 500 kHz — most servo drives handle this natively, so feed the encoder to the drive rather than to a PLC card; (ii) reduce to ×1 decoding (125 kHz, 4 µm resolution) if 4 µm is adequate — and 4 µm almost certainly is, given that the ball screw's own accuracy and thermal growth exceed it; (iii) fit a lower-PPR encoder. Note the wider lesson: 1 µm resolution on this axis was never real. Screw pitch error, backlash, coupling wind-up and thermal expansion all exceed it, so the specification bought a counting problem in exchange for a precision that the mechanics cannot deliver.
P4  [8 marks] · ULTRASONIC LEVEL IN A HOT SHED

An ultrasonic level sensor spans 6 m in a silo. It is calibrated on a 25 °C morning; by afternoon the headspace reaches 45 °C. Quantify the error and give two fixes.

Tap to reveal ↓ c ≈ 331 + 0.6T

Two hundred millimetres, appearing and disappearing daily with the weather, on a silo whose whole span is six metres. If that silo drives a re-order point or a batch, the plant will conclude the level sensor is “unreliable”.

Fix 1 — temperature compensation. Most sensors have it built in; verify that the temperature element is sensing the air in the sound path and not the sensor's own housing, which sits in the roof and reads its own solar gain rather than the headspace.
Fix 2 — change the principle. Radar or guided-wave radar times an electromagnetic wave, whose speed is unaffected by air temperature, humidity, dust or vapour. On a hot, dusty silo this is the durable answer.

Also creditable: a fixed reference target at a known distance lets the sensor self-calibrate its speed of sound continuously — elegant, cheap, and occasionally the right answer.
P5  [8 marks] · WHERE TO SPEND THE MONEY

A measurement chain has four independent contributions, in the same unit: 0.80, 0.30, 0.25 and 0.20. (a) Combined uncertainty? (b) Effect of halving the largest? (c) Effect of halving the second-largest instead? (d) The rule.

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) uc = √(0.64 + 0.09 + 0.0625 + 0.04) = √0.8325 = 0.912

(b) halve 0.80 → 0.40:
    uc = √(0.16 + 0.09 + 0.0625 + 0.04) = 0.594 → −34.9%

(c) halve 0.30 → 0.15:
    uc = √(0.64 + 0.0225 + 0.0625 + 0.04) = 0.875 → −4.1%
(d) The rule: improvements are worth roughly the square of the term's share. Halving the dominant term bought eight and a half times more improvement than halving the next one — and the second-largest term is not a small contributor by any casual standard; it is 37% of the largest.

Two practical corollaries. Anything below about a third of the dominant term is invisible: at 0.25 against 0.80, eliminating it entirely improves uc by only 4%. And a chain is only as good as its worst element, so the correct procurement question is never “can we afford a better transmitter?” but “which line of the budget is largest, and what does halving it cost?”

This is the same mathematics that governs reliability chains, cost breakdowns, Pareto analysis and the safety-integrity arithmetic in A63, where the final element was 65.6% of a SIF's unavailability and therefore the only sensible thing to improve.
P6  [8 marks] · THE MINOR ADDITION

A 3000 kg weighing system with ±0.02% of full-scale combined error is used to dose a 20 kg ingredient. (a) Error as a percentage of the addition? (b) Two ways to fix it, with numbers.

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) error = 0.0002 × 3000 = ±0.6 kg
    on 20 kg → ±3.0% — unacceptable for almost any recipe.

Fix 1 — a dedicated small weigh hopper. A 50 kg-capacity system at the same 0.02% gives ±0.01 kg = ±0.05% on the 20 kg addition — a sixtyfold improvement for the price of a small hopper and three cells.
Fix 2 — loss-in-weight dosing from a small feeder, where the measurement is made on a vessel whose total mass is close to the material being dispensed rather than on the receiving vessel's full tare.

Also creditable and worth stating: a Coriolis mass flowmeter dosing directly into the vessel, which measures the added mass itself (0.1–0.2% of reading, so ±0.04 kg on 20 kg) and is entirely unaffected by the receiving vessel's tare. Not creditable: recalibrating the existing cells, which cannot change a limit set by full-scale resolution.
P7  [8 marks] · VORTEX LOW-FLOW CUT-OFF

A DN80 vortex meter has a bluff body of 22.4 mm (0.28 D), Strouhal number 0.2, and a minimum reliably detectable shedding frequency of 5 Hz. (a) Minimum measurable velocity and flow? (b) If the maximum duty is 100 m³/h, what turndown does that imply, and what does it mean?

Tap to reveal ↓ f = St · v / d → v = f d / St = 5 × 0.0224 / 0.2 = 0.56 m/s
A = π/4 × 0.08² = 0.005027 m²
Qmin = 0.56 × 0.005027 × 3600 = 10.1 m³/h

(b) Against a 100 m³/h maximum this is a turndown of about 10:1, and below 10.1 m³/h the meter reads zero — not “inaccurate”, but literally nothing, because no vortices are being counted.

Two consequences that matter more than the number. First, if the plant ever turns down below 10% — night shift, start-up, a single line running out of four — the flow totaliser stops accumulating and the plant's material balance quietly goes wrong. Second, the cut-off depends on the fluid: the shedding must produce enough pressure amplitude for the sensor to detect, so a low-density gas has a much higher cut-off velocity than a liquid. Always ask the vendor for the cut-off at your fluid and conditions, not the catalogue figure.

If a wide turndown is needed: a magnetic meter (conductive liquids, 50:1+) or Coriolis (100:1+) rather than a bigger vortex meter — oversizing a vortex meter makes the low-flow problem worse, since a larger bore means a lower velocity for the same flow.
P8  [8 marks] · A NOISE DIAGNOSIS

A 4–20 mA temperature signal is stable at night and noisy whenever a nearby 55 kW VFD-driven fan runs. The cable is a screened twisted pair, screened at both ends. Identify the likely mechanism and give a diagnostic sequence.

Tap to reveal ↓ Most likely: a conductive (common-impedance) earth loop, created by bonding the screen at both ends. With two earth connections at different potentials — and a VFD injecting high-frequency common-mode current into the earth system — current flows along the screen, and the resulting voltage appears in series with the signal.

Diagnostic sequence, in order:
1. Disconnect the screen at the field end and observe. If the noise falls dramatically, the diagnosis is confirmed and the cure is already applied — bond at the control panel only.
2. Measure the potential difference between the two earth points with the fan running. Anything more than a few hundred millivolts is a finding in itself and indicates an earthing system problem beyond this loop.
3. Check the VFD's own installation: screened motor cable, 360° gland terminations at both drive and motor, and a proper high-frequency bonding path. A drive installed without these is a broadband transmitter, and it will affect more than one instrument.
4. Check cable routing — parallel runs with the motor cable, shared trays or shared conduit. Separate them; cross at 90° where they must meet.
5. Only then consider a galvanic isolator or an isolated analogue input, which treats the symptom robustly but leaves the earthing fault in place for the next instrument to find.

Do not start by adding filtering. It would mask the symptom, cost phase margin (page 5), and leave a plant-wide earthing defect undiscovered.
P9  [8 marks] · WHICH FLOWMETER?

Choose and justify a meter for each duty, in one line each: (a) 200 m³/h of raw water for a plant balance; (b) steam to a dryer, 0–2500 kg/h, 8 barg; (c) dosing 20–200 kg/h of a variable-density syrup into a batch; (d) compressed air to a whole factory for energy monitoring; (e) 50 m³/h of fuel oil, existing pipe, no shutdown available.

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) Electromagnetic. Water is conductive, there is no pressure loss, no obstruction to foul, 50:1 turndown, and short straight runs are acceptable. It is almost always the right answer for water and aqueous slurries.

(b) Vortex — steam is a clean, high-temperature gas with no moving parts tolerated; a vortex meter handles the temperature and gives 10–20:1. Provided the pipe does not vibrate and the low-flow cut-off is checked at steam density (P7). Pressure and temperature compensation is needed for mass flow. An orifice plate is the cheaper, traditional alternative and is acceptable if the duty stays in a narrow band.

(c) Coriolis — the density is variable, so volume is the wrong quantity (page 10), and a 10:1 dosing range with high accuracy at the low end demands % of reading. This is the duty Coriolis exists for.

(d) Thermal mass — it measures mass directly, so no pressure and temperature compensation is needed, turndown is wide, and insertion versions fit large pipes cheaply. Compressed air monitoring is its home ground.

(e) Clamp-on ultrasonic (transit-time) — fuel oil is clean and non-conductive (so magnetic is out) and no shutdown is available (so nothing in-line can be fitted). Check the straight run and the pipe wall condition; accept 1–2% rather than 0.2%.

Full marks require the reason, not the name. The reasons here are, in order: conductivity, temperature and cleanliness, variable density, mass-versus-volume, and the impossibility of cutting the pipe.
P10  [12 marks] · SYNTHESIS — SPECIFY A MEASUREMENT PACKAGE

A tea factory is instrumenting a fluid-bed dryer. Required: inlet air temperature (control), product bed temperature (control and high-temperature trip), airflow (control), and a level indication on a covered feed hopper containing dusty leaf. Specify each — principle, key numbers, installation caution — and state one thing you would refuse to do.

Tap to reveal ↓ Inlet air temperature — Pt100 Class A, 4-wire or head transmitter, thin thermowell. Range to the process (say 0–250 °C, not 0–600). Air has a low heat transfer coefficient, so τ is long: use a small-diameter well, spring-loaded tip, and site it in the duct where velocity is highest. Excitation ≤ 1 mA to keep self-heating under 0.05 °C.

Bed temperature — two independent measurements. One Pt100 for control; a separate sensor in a separate well, on a different flange with a different cable route, for the trip — because a shared well is a common-cause failure (page 14). The trip sensor should be de-energise-to-trip and have a defined proof test interval. Diversity of technology is a bonus; diversity of path is the requirement.

Airflow — averaging pitot or thermal mass insertion probe. Large duct, low pressure, so an orifice is impractical. Check straight run; if it does not exist, use a multi-point averaging element or accept a repeatable-but-inaccurate measurement, which is adequate for control (Q2, page 15) but not for an energy declaration.

Hopper level — guided-wave radar, or load cells if the hopper is small enough to weigh. Dusty leaf rules out ultrasonic (dust scatters, and the headspace temperature swings) and makes capacitive unreliable (leaf moisture changes εr seasonally — the coconut milk lesson in a different costume). A simple vibrating-fork level switch at the low point is a cheap and robust addition for the “call for refill” function.

What I would refuse: using the control sensor's signal for the high-temperature trip, however good the sensor. It is the same measurement, the same well, the same wire and the same card — so the protection layer is not independent of the thing it protects against, and it may not be credited as a separate layer. Also refuse: a diffuse photoelectric sensor for anything in a dusty tea plant, and any capacitive level measurement on a product whose moisture varies with the weather.

Marking: 2 marks per measurement for principle plus a number plus an installation caution; 4 marks for the refusal, provided it is justified by independence or by a physical property that varies.

Derivations & Traps

Four results used throughout the module, derived rather than quoted — because a formula you can rebuild is one you will still trust when the number looks wrong.

DERIVATION 1 · WHY A FULL BRIDGE GIVES EXACTLY 2 mV/V STRETCH
The bridge is two dividers compared against each other: Vout/Vex = R₁/(R₁+R₂) − R₄/(R₃+R₄) QUARTER BRIDGE — one active arm, R₁ = R + ΔR, rest = R: Vout/Vex = (R+ΔR)/(2R+ΔR) − ½ = [2(R+ΔR) − (2R+ΔR)] / [2(2R+ΔR)] = ΔR / (4R + 2ΔR) and since ΔR ≪ R, → Vout/Vex ≈ ¼ · ΔR/R = ¼ · GF · ε Note the 2ΔR left in the denominator: the quarter bridge is slightly non-linear, and that term is the non-linearity. FULL BRIDGE — opposite pairs strained oppositely: R₁ = R₃ = R + ΔR R₂ = R₄ = R − ΔR Vout/Vex = (R+ΔR)/(2R) − (R−ΔR)/(2R) = ΔR/R = GF · ε — EXACTLY, no approximation NUMBERS: GF = 2, ε = 1000 µε = 0.001 → Vout/Vex = 0.002 = 2 mV/V

Three things fall out of the algebra free. The full bridge is four times the output of a quarter bridge, so the signal-to-noise ratio improves by the same factor. It is exactly linear, because the ΔR terms cancel in the denominators rather than surviving as they do in the quarter bridge. And because all four arms sit on the same steel at the same temperature, a uniform temperature change adds the same fractional resistance to every arm and cancels completely — which is why load cells work outdoors in Colombo without a temperature sensor anywhere near them. The 2 mV/V on every load cell datasheet is not a convention; it is 1000 µε of working strain through a GF-2 full bridge.

DERIVATION 2 · HOW THREE WIRES CANCEL A CABLE STRETCH
Let the three leads have resistances r₁, r₂, r₃. The instrument drives a known current through the RTD via leads 1 and 3, and measures TWO voltages: VA = I · (r₁ + RRTD + r₃) (across the whole path) VB = I · (r₂ + r₃) (across the return path only) VA − VB = I · (RRTD + r₁ − r₂) IF r₁ = r₂ → the reading is RRTD exactly RESIDUAL ERROR when they differ by δ: ΔR = δ → ΔT = δ / 0.391 Ω/°C δ = 0.05 Ω (one crimp, one extra metre) → 0.13 °C δ = 0.50 Ω (different cable types) → 1.28 °C

The assumption is visible in the algebra: the two measurement legs must be identical. Three-wire cancellation therefore fails if the leads are different lengths, different cross-sections, or if one has a corroded joint — and it fails silently, producing a plausible offset. That is precisely why 4-wire (true Kelvin) connection exists: it measures the voltage across the element with a separate pair carrying no current, so lead resistance disappears from the measurement altogether and no matching assumption is required.

The practical instruction: use three cores of the same cable, terminate them identically, and never extend one leg with a different type. If the sensor is more than a few metres away and accuracy matters, the better answer is usually neither three nor four wires — it is a head-mounted transmitter that converts to 4–20 mA or HART at the sensor, after which cable resistance is irrelevant (page 13).

DERIVATION 3 · THE SENSOR AS A FILTER STRETCH
A first-order sensor obeys: τ dy/dt + y = x(t) (a) SINUSOIDAL INPUT x = X ejωt, and in steady state y = Y ejωt: τ(jω)Y + Y = X → Y/X = 1/(1 + jωτ) amplitude ratio = |Y/X| = 1/√(1 + (ωτ)²) phase = −arctan(ωτ) at ωτ = 1 : AR = 1/√2 = 0.707 (−3 dB), phase = −45° → the corner PERIOD is 2πτ — for τ = 12 s that is 75 s (b) RAMP INPUT x = R·t. Try y = R(t − a): τ·R + R(t − a) = R t → τR − Ra = 0 → a = τ so y(t) = R(t − τ) — the reading is permanently Rτ behind, and lags the truth by exactly τ seconds in time.

Why (b) is the more dangerous result. The sinusoidal case is at least visible — the trend looks smaller than reality and someone eventually notices. The ramp case produces a smooth, plausible, steady reading that is constantly wrong by Rτ and returns to correct the moment the ramp stops, which is exactly when anyone checks it. A calibration performed at steady state will pass it every time.

Both results are the same equation seen two ways, and both explain why the same τ appears in the tuning arithmetic of a control loop: a phase lag of −arctan(ωτ) at the crossover frequency is subtracted straight from the phase margin. A 12 s well on a loop crossing over at 0.1 rad/s has spent 50° of a 60° budget before the controller has done anything at all.

DERIVATION 4 · WHY QUADRATURE, AND WHY THE BIGGEST TERM WINS STRETCH
For independent random variables, variances add: Var(X₁ + X₂ + …) = Var(X₁) + Var(X₂) + … Standard uncertainty is a standard deviation, so: uc² = u₁² + u₂² + … → uc = √(Σ ui²) More generally, for y = f(x₁, x₂, …), the GUM law of propagation of uncertainty gives: uc²(y) = Σ (∂f/∂xi)² · u²(xi) — the partial derivative is the SENSITIVITY COEFFICIENT, and it is what converts a bar of pressure into a millimetre of level, or a milliamp into a degree. THE DOMINANCE PROPERTY — take b = a/3: √(a² + (a/3)²) = a√(1 + 1/9) = 1.054 a A term one third the size of the largest adds 5.4%. A term one fifth adds 2.0%. One tenth adds 0.5%.

Two rules for professional life follow, and they generalise far beyond instrumentation. First, anything below about a third of the dominant term can be ignored — not approximately ignored, but genuinely, because improving it cannot move the answer by more than a few percent. Second, improvement is worth roughly the square of a term's share, which is why halving the largest of four contributions in P5 bought 35% while halving the second bought 4%.

Note the one condition the derivation needs: independence. Correlated errors do not combine this way — if two transmitters share a power supply, a calibration reference or an ambient temperature, their errors move together and the correct treatment adds a covariance term (or, in the extreme, adds them arithmetically). This is the same structural warning as common-cause failure in redundancy: the mathematics of independence is generous, and independence is exactly the assumption that installations quietly violate.
