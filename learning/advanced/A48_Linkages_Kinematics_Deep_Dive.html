Mechanisms, Links & Kinematic Pairs

Before motion comes vocabulary: what moves, how it is joined, and what that joint permits.

Definition

A mechanism is a set of rigid links connected by joints (kinematic pairs) so that a defined input motion produces a defined output motion. It transforms motion — rotary to linear, fast to slow, one path to another — while carrying force along the way.

A machine is a mechanism that also transmits significant power to do work; the kinematics are identical, only the force scale differs. Kinematics studies motion (position, velocity, acceleration) without reference to the forces causing it; kinetics/dynamics adds the forces. This module is mostly kinematics — the geometry that must be right before any force calculation means anything.

Kinematic pairs — joints classified by freedom
PairSymbolDOFMotion allowedExample

Lower pairs (R, P, H, C, S) touch over a surface — durable, low wear, the backbone of linkages. Higher pairs touch on a line or point (cams, gear teeth) — compact and versatile but higher contact stress (Hertzian — cross-ref A20, A19).

Links, chains & the ground

A kinematic chain becomes a mechanism when one link is fixed (the ground/frame). The four-bar shown: ground (1), crank (2), coupler (3), rocker (4) — the archetype every later page builds on.

🧠 Quick Check 1

Why do designers favour lower pairs (pins, sliders) over higher pairs (cams, gears) wherever the motion allows?

Tap to reveal Lower pairs make contact over an area, so the same force gives far lower contact stress, less wear, easier lubrication and long life. Higher pairs (line/point contact) concentrate load into Hertzian contact stresses — compact and able to produce arbitrary motion laws (a cam can do anything), but they wear faster and need harder materials and better lubrication. Use a linkage of lower pairs when it can do the job; reach for a cam or gear only when you need a motion a linkage can't easily give.
Page 2 / 16 · Mobility

Mobility — Counting Degrees of Freedom

How many inputs does a mechanism need? One formula answers it — and its exceptions teach the rest.

Gruebler / Kutzbach criterion

Mobility (DOF) is the number of independent inputs needed to fully position a mechanism. Count links and the freedom each joint removes.

Planar: M = 3(n − 1) − 2j₁ − j₂ n = total links (including ground) · j₁ = lower pairs (1-DOF: R, P) · j₂ = higher pairs (2-DOF) Spatial: M = 6(n − 1) − Σ(6 − fi) fi = DOF of joint i

The logic: each free planar body has 3 DOF; grounding one removes a body; every 1-DOF joint takes away 2 freedoms, every 2-DOF joint takes away 1. M = 1 means one motor/input fully drives it — the target for most machines. M = 0 is a structure (truss); M < 0 is over-constrained (statically indeterminate).

Worked counts

Four-bar

M=1

n=4, j₁=4, j₂=0 → 3(3) − 8 = 1. One input → determinate motion.

Slider-crank

M=1

n=4, j₁=4 (3R+1P) → 1. Same mobility, different geometry.

Five-bar

M=2

n=5, j₁=5 → 3(4) − 10 = 2. Needs TWO inputs (e.g. a 2-motor SCARA-like).

When Gruebler lies — and why that matters
  • 1
    Redundant constraints: a parallel link that duplicates an existing constraint (e.g. the third rail on a locomotive coupling, or a parallelogram's extra bar) makes the formula predict M=0 while the real mechanism moves. The link is there for load/stability, not kinematics — subtract redundant constraints before trusting the count.
  • 2
    Idle DOF: a roller follower that spins on its own pin adds a DOF that doesn't affect output motion. Gruebler over-counts; recognise and discount it.
  • 3
    Special geometry (paradoxes): exact dimensions can grant motion the generic count forbids (a parallelogram in a singular alignment). Kinematics of the specific dimensions overrides the generic formula.

These aren't academic footnotes: a redundant-constraint mechanism binds if the extra bar isn't made to exact length, and an over-constrained (M≤0) assembly that "should" be rigid can jam or stress on tolerance — the daily reality of fixture and machine design.

🧠 Quick Check 2

A parallelogram linkage (crank, coupler, rocker all sized so opposite sides are equal) has M=1 by Gruebler. Add a fifth bar parallel to the coupler to steady it. What does Gruebler now say, and what actually happens?

Tap to reveal Adding the parallel bar adds one link and two pins: ΔM = 3(1) − 2(2) = −1, so Gruebler drops M from 1 to 0 (a structure). But because the geometry is exactly parallel, the extra bar duplicates a constraint already present — it's redundant — and the mechanism still moves with M=1. The catch: it only works if the fifth bar is made to precise length; a small error over-constrains it and it binds. This is the classic Gruebler paradox and a real manufacturing warning.

The Four-Bar & Grashof's Law

The most useful mechanism ever devised — and the one inequality that predicts its behaviour.

Grashof's law

For a four-bar, whether any link can fully rotate depends on a single inequality between the shortest (s), longest (l) and the other two (p, q) link lengths.

Grashof condition: s + l ≤ p + q If TRUE → at least one link makes full revolutions (Grashof / Class I) If FALSE → no link fully rotates — all are rockers (non-Grashof / Class II) Special case s + l = p + q → change-point (folds through a singularity)
The four Grashof types — decided by which link is grounded
Ground link is…ResultInput→OutputUse
adjacent to shortestCrank-rockercrank rotates → rocker oscillatesoscillating fan, wipers, pumps
the shortest linkDouble-crank (drag-link)both side links rotate fullycontinuous drive coupling
opposite the shortestDouble-rockerboth side links oscillatesteering, rocking platforms
(non-Grashof, any)Triple-rockerno full rotationlimited-swing links

This is the profound part: the same four bars give completely different machines depending on which link you bolt down — the four inversions (Page 4). Grashof tells you which link can be the crank; grounding chooses the behaviour.

🧠 Quick Check 3

Links 2, 4, 5, 8 (cm). You need a motor to drive a continuously-rotating input and get an oscillating output. Is it possible, and how do you ground it?

Tap to reveal Check Grashof: s+l = 2+8 = 10; p+q = 4+5 = 9. Since 10 > 9, this is non-Grashof — no link can fully rotate, so a rotating motor input is impossible whatever you ground; you'd only get rocker-rocker motion. To get crank-rocker you need s+l ≤ p+q (e.g. change the 8 to a 6: 2+6=8 ≤ 4+5=9 ✓) then ground a link adjacent to the shortest (the 2 cm link).
Page 4 / 16 · Inversions

Kinematic Inversions

Fix a different link, get a different machine — the same chain, four personalities.

The idea of inversion

An inversion is created by grounding a different link of the same kinematic chain. The relative motion between links is unchanged — but the absolute motion, and hence the machine, transforms completely.

A chain with n links has n possible inversions. They are not new mechanisms — the link lengths and joints are identical — yet each does a different job. Recognising that a puzzle mechanism is "just an inversion of the slider-crank" instantly imports everything you know about it.

Inversions of the slider-crank

The Whitworth and crank-shaper quick-return mechanisms — where the cutting stroke is slow and the return fast — are slider-crank inversions; we quantify their time ratio on Page 10.

Inversions of the four-bar

Grounding each of the four links of a Grashof chain yields the crank-rocker, drag-link and double-rocker of Page 3 — plus the double-slider chain gives the elliptical trammel, Scotch yoke (pure sine motion) and Oldham coupling (connects parallel offset shafts). One chain, a family of couplings and motion converters.

Scotch yoke

Slider-crank cousin giving exact simple-harmonic motion x = r·cosθ. Used in valve actuators & test rigs.

Oldham coupling

Double-slider inversion transmitting torque between parallel, laterally-offset shafts.

Elliptical trammel

Two sliders in perpendicular slots; any coupler point traces an ellipse.

🧠 Quick Check 4

A Scotch yoke and a standard slider-crank both convert rotation to reciprocation. Why does the Scotch yoke give pure sinusoidal motion while the slider-crank does not?

Tap to reveal The Scotch yoke's slot decouples the crank pin's transverse motion, so the slider position is exactly x = r·cosθ — pure SHM. The slider-crank's finite connecting rod adds a second-harmonic term: x ≈ r·cosθ + (r²/2L)·cos2θ. The rod's obliquity distorts the sinusoid (and creates the classic 1×/2× inertia forces engine balancers must handle). Shorter rod (small L/r) → more distortion. The Scotch yoke trades that rod for a sliding slot — cleaner motion, but higher wear in the slot.
Page 5 / 16 · Slider-Crank

The Slider-Crank

The mechanism that powers the industrial world — piston to crankshaft, exactly analysed.

Geometry
Piston position from crank centre (θ from dead-centre): x = r·cosθ + √(L² − r²·sin²θ) exact x ≈ (L + r) − r[(1−cosθ) + (r/4L)(1−cos2θ)] binomial approx (n = L/r) The (r/4L)cos2θ term is the second harmonic — the source of secondary inertia force.
Velocity & acceleration of the piston
With ω = crank speed (rad/s), n = L/r: v ≈ r·ω·(sinθ + (sin2θ)/(2n)) a ≈ r·ω²·(cosθ + (cos2θ)/n) Primary inertia force ∝ cosθ (once per rev) · Secondary ∝ cos2θ/n (twice per rev)

These two harmonics are why engines need balancing: the primary force can be balanced by counterweights, but the secondary (2×) needs Lanchester balance shafts. A longer rod (bigger n) shrinks the secondary term — smoother, but a taller, heavier engine. Every engine is this trade.

Offset (eccentric) slider-crank

Offsetting the slider axis from the crank centre by e makes the mechanism a natural quick-return — the forward and return strokes take unequal crank angles, giving different average speeds. Small offsets also reduce side-thrust on one side of the piston. The trade is asymmetric wear and a slightly more complex position equation (add the offset term inside the square root).

🧠 Quick Check 5

Two engines have rod/crank ratios n = 3 and n = 5. Which has larger secondary (2×) inertia forces, and what's the design cost of fixing it?

Tap to reveal The secondary acceleration term is (cos2θ)/n, so the n = 3 engine has larger secondary forces (1/3 vs 1/5 — about 67 % more). Fixing it means either a longer rod (raise n → taller, heavier, lower-revving engine) or adding balance shafts spinning at 2× crank speed (weight, cost, friction). Short-rod engines are compact but rougher; this is exactly why inline-4s over ~2 L usually get balance shafts.

Position Analysis — Vector Loops

The systematic method: write the linkage as a closed chain of vectors and solve.

The vector-loop equation

Model each link as a position vector R = L∠θ. Going around a closed mechanism and back to the start, the vectors must sum to zero — the loop-closure equation. Split into real (x) and imaginary (y) parts and solve for the unknown angles.

Four-bar (links 1 ground, 2 crank, 3 coupler, 4 rocker): R₂ + R₃ − R₄ − R₁ = 0 x: a·cosθ₂ + b·cosθ₃ − c·cosθ₄ − d = 0 y: a·sinθ₂ + b·sinθ₃ − c·sinθ₄ = 0 Given θ₂ (input) → two equations, two unknowns (θ₃, θ₄).
Freudenstein's equation

Eliminating the coupler angle gives the elegant closed form relating input θ₂ and output θ₄ directly:

K₁·cosθ₄ − K₂·cosθ₂ + K₃ = cos(θ₂ − θ₄) K₁ = d/a, K₂ = d/c, K₃ = (a² − b² + c² + d²)/(2ac) Solve for θ₄ via the half-angle/tangent substitution → two roots = the two assembly configurations ("open" and "crossed" circuits).

The two solutions are physical: a four-bar can be assembled two ways (open or crossed) for the same link lengths, and it cannot jump between them without disassembly. Choosing the right branch — and staying on it through the motion — is a real synthesis constraint (Page 11).

Coupler point & the coupler curve

Any point rigidly attached to the floating coupler traces a coupler curve — often a complex figure-eight or D-shape. These curves are the secret weapon of linkage design: with the right geometry a coupler point can trace an approximate straight line, a dwell, or a specific path, doing with four bars what would otherwise need a cam. The Hrones–Nelson atlas catalogues thousands of them.

🧠 Quick Check 6

Freudenstein's equation gives two values of θ₄ for a given input. What do they represent physically, and can a running mechanism switch between them?

Tap to reveal The two roots are the two assembly configurations — the "open" and "crossed" circuits — the same bars can be pinned together in either mirror arrangement. A running mechanism cannot jump between them without passing through a change-point (singular) configuration or being disassembled; away from singularities each circuit is a separate continuous branch. Designers must pick the branch that keeps the transmission angle healthy (Page 9) throughout the cycle.

Velocity Analysis & Instant Centres

Two roads to velocity: differentiate the loop, or find the point a body is momentarily rotating about.

Method 1 — differentiate the vector loop
Differentiate the loop-closure once w.r.t. time (ω = dθ/dt): x: −a·ω₂·sinθ₂ − b·ω₃·sinθ₃ + c·ω₄·sinθ₄ = 0 y: a·ω₂·cosθ₂ + b·ω₃·cosθ₃ − c·ω₄·cosθ₄ = 0 Given ω₂ → solve the linear pair for ω₃, ω₄: ω₄ = a·ω₂·sin(θ₂−θ₃) / [c·sin(θ₄−θ₃)] ω₃ = a·ω₂·sin(θ₂−θ₄) / [b·sin(θ₃−θ₄)]

Clean, exact and programmable — the method behind every kinematics solver. Note the sin(θ₄−θ₃) in the denominator: when coupler and rocker align (θ₄ = θ₃), it goes to zero and ω₄ blows up — a singularity / change-point (Page 9).

Method 2 — instant centres of rotation

At any instant, a rigid body in planar motion is rotating about a single point — its instant centre (IC). Velocities are then just IC = pivot: v = ω × r from that point.

Number of ICs: N = n(n − 1)/2 (n = number of links) Four-bar: N = 4·3/2 = 6 instant centres Primary ICs = the pin joints; secondary ICs found by Kennedy's theorem.
Kennedy's (three-centres) theorem

Kennedy: the three instant centres of any three bodies in relative motion lie on one straight line. This lets you locate the elusive secondary ICs by intersecting lines — and once you have the IC of the coupler relative to ground (I₁₃), the coupler's angular velocity and every point's velocity follow instantly. It's the graphical shortcut that makes hand analysis of complex linkages tractable.

Mechanical advantage from ICs
MA = (output force)/(input force) = (ω_in/ω_out) = (r_out lever)/(r_in lever) As a linkage approaches a toggle, the output lever → ∞ (ω_out → 0) → MA → ∞ This is why toggle clamps and rock crushers can multiply force enormously near lock-up.
🧠 Quick Check 7

In velocity analysis of a four-bar, ω₄ has sin(θ₄−θ₃) in its denominator. What physically happens when coupler (3) and rocker (4) become collinear?

Tap to reveal sin(θ₄−θ₃) → 0, so the equation predicts ω₄ → ∞ for finite ω₂ — a singular / change-point configuration. Physically the mechanism is at a dead-centre / toggle: the output link momentarily can move very fast for tiny input (or, viewed as force, gives enormous mechanical advantage — ω_out→0 means MA→∞). It's the basis of toggle clamps and quick-return; but if it's unwanted it means loss of control of the output branch — a design flaw to avoid within the working range.

Acceleration & the Coriolis Term

Differentiate twice — and meet the acceleration component that trips up every student.

Acceleration of a point on a rotating link
For a point at radius r on a link with ω, α: a = a_normal + a_tangential a_normal = ω²·r (centripetal, points to centre) — exists even at constant speed a_tangential = α·r (along motion, from angular acceleration) |a| = r·√(ω⁴ + α²)

Differentiating the four-bar velocity equations again gives a linear pair for α₃, α₄ in terms of the known ω's and input α₂ — the same machine, one more derivative. This is what sizes bearing loads, inertia forces and vibration in high-speed mechanisms.

The Coriolis acceleration

When a point slides along a link that is itself rotating (a block in a rotating slot — Whitworth, shaper, any sliding pair on a moving body), a fourth acceleration term appears: Coriolis.

a_Coriolis = 2·ω × v_rel magnitude = 2·ω·v_slip Full: a_B = a_A + a_rel + ω×(ω×r) + α×r + 2ω×v_rel normal tangential Coriolis

Direction: rotate the sliding-velocity vector 90° in the direction of ω. Miss it and your acceleration (and therefore force/stress) analysis of any quick-return or governor is simply wrong. It only appears with the combination of sliding and rotation — a slider on the fixed frame has no Coriolis; a slider on a spinning arm does.

Why it matters in practice

Force spikes

Coriolis + normal + tangential can peak far above the "obvious" value → sized bearings & pins.

Vibration

High-speed mechanisms' inertia forces drive shaking couples — the input to balancing.

Governors & pumps

Sliding-on-rotating parts (fly-ball governors, vane pumps) live on the Coriolis term.

🧠 Quick Check 8

A block slides outward at 0.5 m/s along an arm rotating at 20 rad/s. What is the Coriolis acceleration, and does it exist if the arm is NOT rotating?

Tap to reveal a_Cor = 2·ω·v_rel = 2×20×0.5 = 20 m/s², directed perpendicular to the slide (rotate v_rel 90° in the sense of ω). If the arm is not rotating (ω = 0), Coriolis is zero — it requires the combination of sliding motion and rotation of the frame it slides on. A piston sliding in a fixed cylinder has no Coriolis; the same block in a spinning slot does. Forgetting it is the classic acceleration-analysis error.
Page 9 / 16 · Force Transmission

Transmission Angle & Toggle

The single geometric number that decides whether a linkage pushes smoothly or jams.

Transmission angle μ

The transmission angle μ is the angle between the coupler and the output link (rocker). It governs how much of the coupler force does useful work on the output versus wasted radial load on the bearings.

Useful output force ∝ sin μ · (coupler force) Ideal: μ = 90° (all force drives the output) Rule of thumb: keep 40° ≤ μ ≤ 140° (i.e. |90°−μ| ≤ 50°) μ → 0° or 180°: output force → 0, bearing load → ∞ → mechanism jams / stalls
Toggle positions & dead centre

When the crank and coupler become collinear the mechanism is at a toggle (dead-centre): the transmission angle is at an extreme, mechanical advantage spikes (Page 7), and the output momentarily stalls. Two faces of the same coin:

Toggle exploited

Toggle clamps, riveters, rock crushers, knee-locks, switchgear over-centre springs — huge force from small input near lock-up, and the joint self-holds.

Toggle avoided

A drive linkage that hits dead-centre loses control of the output and may not restart — keep the whole working cycle away from μ extremes.

🧠 Quick Check 9

A toggle clamp gives huge clamping force and holds itself shut with no continuous input. Explain both effects from the transmission angle.

Tap to reveal Near the toggle the driving links are nearly collinear, so mechanical advantage → very high (ω_out→0 ⇒ MA→∞): a modest handle force becomes a large clamping force. Self-holding comes from going slightly over-centre: any reaction from the workpiece now tries to push the linkage further past dead-centre against a stop, not back toward release — the joint is locked by geometry, needing no holding force. The same physics runs circuit-breaker mechanisms and knee-lock stands.

The Classic Special Mechanisms

A toolbox of named linkages — each solves a recurring motion problem elegantly.

Quick-return mechanisms

A cutting tool should return faster than it cuts (idle time is wasted time). Quick-return linkages give an asymmetric stroke: slow forward, fast return. The time ratio is set by the crank angles of the two strokes:

Time ratio Q = α/β α = crank angle for cutting stroke, β = for return (α+β=360°) Whitworth & crank-shaper: Q from the geometry of the driving crank & slotted lever Offset slider-crank: offset e creates the asymmetry directly
Straight-line mechanisms
MechanismTypeNote
WattApproximateCoupler midpoint traces a figure-8; near-straight over a short span. Historic (steam beam engines).
ChebyshevApproximateSimple 4-bar giving a good straight segment.
Roberts / HoekenApproximateHoeken gives near-constant-velocity straight run — popular in walkers & pick-and-place.
Peaucellier–LipkinExact8-bar inversor — mathematically perfect straight line (1864). Elegant but complex.
Scott-RussellExact*Exact via a slider; a linkage-with-slider solution.

Before precise linear guides, generating a straight line from pin joints was a famous engineering problem (guiding a piston rod). Today approximate straight-line linkages still shine where you want a straight stroke plus a clean return without rails — walking robots, film advance, packaging.

Motion-copying & intermittent motion

Pantograph

Parallelogram linkage that scales & copies a path (engraving, drawing, some robot arms). Output = input path × fixed ratio.

Geneva mechanism

Converts continuous rotation to precise intermittent indexing — film projectors, indexing tables, cap fillers. n slots → 1/n turn per cycle with dwell.

Ratchet & pawl

One-way intermittent motion & holding — winches, tensioners, some indexers.

Toggle press

Over-centre force multiplication for punching, clamping, moulding.

🧠 Quick Check 10

You need an indexing table that stops precisely at 6 stations while the drive motor runs continuously. Which classic mechanism, and what's the key design number?

Tap to reveal A Geneva mechanism (external Geneva) with 6 slots: the driving pin enters each slot to rotate the driven wheel 1/6 turn (60°), then the locking arc holds it dead still for the rest of the cycle — precise intermittent indexing from continuous input, no clutch or servo. Key numbers: number of slots n = number of stations, and the driving-crank radius sets the ratio of motion time to dwell time. (A servo-indexer, A09, is the modern programmable alternative — but the Geneva is cheap, rugged and self-locking.)

Linkage Synthesis

Analysis finds motion from a given linkage. Synthesis is the reverse — and the real design job.

The three synthesis tasks
TaskGoalExample
Function generationOutput angle = f(input angle) at precision pointsa linkage computing y = log x; steering ratio
Path generationA coupler point follows a specified pathfilm-advance, mixing, pick-and-place curve
Motion (rigid-body) generationThe coupler passes through prescribed positions AND orientationshatchback hinge, bucket levelling, container tilt
Precision points & two/three-position synthesis

You can only satisfy a finite number of exact (precision) points; between them the linkage deviates (structural error). More precision points → more equations → but a four-bar has limited free parameters, so there's a ceiling (Freudenstein's equation gives 3 independent constants → up to 3 precision points for function generation without extra freedoms; up to 5 with all free choices — Burmester theory).

Two-position: connect the two desired coupler poses; the moving pivot lies on the perpendicular bisector of its two locations → freedom to choose along it. Three-position (Burmester): the two bisectors' intersection fixes the pivot circle; the centre-point & circle-point curves give ALL valid pivots.

Graphical Burmester construction and analytical (complex-number / matrix) methods both live here; modern practice runs it in software (MotionGen, GIM, SAM, Linkage) that also checks Grashof type, branch and transmission angle automatically.

The four checks every synthesised linkage must pass
  • 1
    Grashof / rotatability: can the intended input actually rotate the way you need (Page 3)?
  • 2
    Branch (circuit) defect: do all precision points lie on the same assembly circuit? If not, the mechanism can't reach them all without disassembly (Page 6).
  • 3
    Order defect: does it reach the points in the required sequence, not out of order?
  • 4
    Transmission angle: stays within ~40–140° across the whole working range (Page 9) — else it jams under load.

A synthesis that nails the geometry but fails a branch or transmission-angle check is a common, expensive trap — the linkage works on paper and binds on the bench.

🧠 Quick Check 11

Your synthesised four-bar hits all three required coupler positions in simulation but physically won't reach the third without "popping" the assembly. Which defect, and how is it detected?

Tap to reveal A branch (circuit) defect — the third precision point lies on the other assembly circuit (the crossed vs open configuration), so the running mechanism, locked to one circuit, cannot reach it without disassembly or passing through a change-point. Detect it by checking the sign of the transmission-angle/coupler configuration at each precision point: if it flips, the points are on different branches. Cure: re-synthesise choosing pivots (from the Burmester curves) that keep all points on one branch, or accept a different linkage type.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

Ten ideas, six ways linkages fail, and the memory hook.

The ten ideas
  • 1
    Mechanism = links + kinematic pairs; lower pairs (area contact) beat higher pairs (point/line) where motion allows.
  • 2
    Mobility M = 3(n−1) − 2j₁ − j₂. Aim for M=1; beware redundant constraints & idle DOF.
  • 3
    Grashof: s+l ≤ p+q decides whether any link can fully rotate.
  • 4
    Inversions: grounding a different link of one chain gives a different machine.
  • 5
    Slider-crank: x = r·cosθ + √(L²−r²sin²θ); the 2× harmonic drives engine balancing.
  • 6
    Position: vector-loop closure → Freudenstein; two roots = two assembly circuits.
  • 7
    Velocity: differentiate the loop, or use instant centres + Kennedy's three-centres line.
  • 8
    Acceleration: normal ω²r + tangential αr + Coriolis 2ωv when sliding on a rotating link.
  • 9
    Transmission angle μ: keep 40–140°; toggle = MA spike, output stall, self-lock.
  • 10
    Synthesis (function/path/motion) must pass Grashof, branch, order & transmission-angle checks.
Six classic linkage failures
🔒 Bad transmission angle

μ strays near 0/180° in the working range → huge bearing loads, binding, stall. The #1 linkage design sin. Keep 40–140°.

🪤 Dead-centre lock

Drive linkage reaches an unwanted toggle → output loses control & may not restart. Design the cycle away from change-points (or add a flywheel/offset).

🔀 Branch defect

Synthesised points on different circuits → mechanism can't reach them all. Simulation lies if branch isn't checked.

🧩 Over-constraint

Redundant links (M≤0 by Gruebler) bind unless made to exact length → tolerance-sensitive jamming in fixtures & frames.

〰 Unbalanced inertia

High-speed 1×/2× shaking forces (slider-crank harmonics) → vibration, bearing & mount fatigue. Balance or slow down.

🧯 Coriolis ignored

Sliding-on-rotating force underestimated → under-sized pins/bearings in quick-returns & governors fail early.

Q1 — Links 3, 4, 5, 6. Classify (Grashof?) and, if a rotating input is possible, name the mechanism when a link adjacent to the shortest is grounded.

Tap to reveal s+l = 3+6 = 9; p+q = 4+5 = 9 → s+l = p+q: change-point mechanism (borderline Grashof). A link can rotate but the mechanism passes through singular (folded) configurations each cycle — usable but needs care (flywheel/guide) to pass dead-centres. Grounding a link adjacent to the shortest gives a crank-rocker-type motion, subject to those change-points. (Change 6→5 and it becomes cleanly Grashof: 3+5=8 < 4+5.)

Q2 — A five-bar planar chain with all revolute joints: how many inputs to control it, by Gruebler?

Tap to reveal n=5, j₁=5, j₂=0 → M = 3(5−1) − 2(5) = 12 − 10 = 2. Two independent inputs (two motors) are needed — the basis of 2-DOF planar parallel manipulators & five-bar pick-and-place arms.

Q3 — In a Whitworth quick-return, why must Coriolis acceleration be included when finding the ram acceleration?

Tap to reveal Because the driving block slides along the slotted lever while that lever rotates — the exact sliding-on-rotating condition that generates Coriolis (2ωv_slip). Omitting it understates the true acceleration and the resulting inertia force on the slot and pins, under-sizing them. Quick-returns are the textbook home of the Coriolis term.

Q4 — A four-bar's transmission angle drops to 12° at one point in its cycle under heavy load. Predict the symptom and two fixes.

Tap to reveal Symptom: near-toggle → output force collapses (∝ sin12° ≈ 0.21) while bearing/pin reaction soars → the mechanism binds, stalls or the motor over-currents at that point, with rapid wear. Fixes: (1) re-proportion the links (longer coupler/rocker or different ground length) to raise the minimum μ above ~40°; (2) restrict the working range to avoid the bad zone; (3) add a flywheel to carry inertia through it, or change the assembly circuit. Prevention is a min-μ check across the whole cycle at synthesis time.

Q5 — Why can the same four link lengths build a crank-rocker, a drag-link, and a double-rocker?

Tap to reveal Because those are inversions — the behaviour depends on which link is grounded, not on the lengths alone. In a Grashof chain, grounding a link adjacent to the shortest → crank-rocker; grounding the shortest → drag-link (double-crank); grounding the link opposite the shortest → double-rocker. Same bars, same relative motion, three machines. It's the most economical idea in mechanism design.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals, fully worked.

WE 1 — Mobility of a compound mechanism

Given: a mechanism has 6 links, 7 revolute joints, and 1 cam (higher) pair. One of the revolutes carries a roller follower with an idle spin. Find the mobility.

M = 3(n−1) − 2j₁ − j₂ = 3(6−1) − 2(7) − 1(1) = 15 − 14 − 1 = 0 → Gruebler says "structure" Correct for the idle DOF (roller spin adds 1 real DOF not counted as output): Effective M = 0 + 1(idle) → the mechanism actually MOVES with M = 1 useful DOF

Discussion: raw Gruebler gives 0, which would mean no motion — but the roller follower's free spin is an idle degree of freedom that the formula miscounts. Recognising idle DOF (and redundant constraints) is exactly why the count is a starting check, not the final word. Always sanity-check a "structure" result against physical intuition.

WE 2 — Grashof classification & type

Given: four-bar links a=40, b=120, c=80, d=100 mm; the crank (a) is the input, ground is d. Classify and name the mechanism.

shortest s = 40 (=a), longest l = 120 (=b); other two p,q = 80,100 s + l = 40 + 120 = 160; p + q = 80 + 100 = 180 160 ≤ 180 → Grashof (Class I) — a link can fully rotate Shortest link a is a SIDE link (adjacent to ground d) → grounding a link adjacent to the shortest → crank-rocker: crank a rotates fully, rocker c oscillates ✓

Discussion: exactly the recipe for an oscillating output from a rotating motor (wiper, agitator). Had we grounded link a (the shortest) instead, the same bars would give a drag-link (both cranks rotate). Grashof + choice-of-ground fully determines the behaviour.

WE 3 — Slider-crank velocity & acceleration

Given: r = 50 mm, L = 200 mm (n = 4), crank at θ = 90°, ω = 200 rad/s constant (α=0). Find piston velocity and acceleration.

v ≈ rω(sinθ + sin2θ/2n) = 0.05×200×(sin90° + sin180°/8) = 10×(1 + 0) = 10 m/s a ≈ rω²(cosθ + cos2θ/n) = 0.05×200²×(cos90° + cos180°/4) = 2000×(0 + (−1)/4) = 2000×(−0.25) = −500 m/s²

Discussion: at θ=90° the primary acceleration term (cosθ) vanishes, so the acceleration is entirely the secondary (2×) harmonic — a pure −500 m/s² from the cos2θ/n term. This is the acceleration engine designers must not forget: it exists even at mid-stroke and scales with ω², so it quadruples if you double the speed.

WE 4 — Transmission angle of a four-bar

Given: a=40, b=100, c=90, d=120 mm; crank at θ₂=60°. Find the transmission angle μ.

Diagonal from ground pivot O₂ to coupler-rocker pin (via crank): p² = a² + d² − 2ad·cosθ₂ = 40² + 120² − 2(40)(120)cos60° = 1600 + 14400 − 9600×0.5 = 16000 − 4800 = 11200 → p = 105.8 mm μ from triangle (coupler b, rocker c, diagonal p), law of cosines: cos μ = (b² + c² − p²)/(2bc) = (10000 + 8100 − 11200)/(2×100×90) = 6900/18000 = 0.3833 → μ = 67.5° ✓ (within 40–140°, healthy)

Discussion: 67.5° is comfortably in the good band, so the linkage transmits force efficiently at this position. To certify the design you'd sweep θ₂ 0–360° (the interactive on Page 9 does this) and confirm the minimum μ over the whole cycle never dives below ~40°.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Work each before revealing.

Problems 1–10

P1 — A planar mechanism: 8 links, 10 revolute joints, no higher pairs. Mobility?

Tap to reveal M = 3(8−1) − 2(10) = 21 − 20 = 1. A single-DOF eight-bar — one motor drives it (e.g. many mechanical presses & walking linkages).

P2 — Links 25, 55, 40, 50 mm. Grashof? If so, which link should be the crank?

Tap to reveal s+l = 25+55 = 80; p+q = 40+50 = 90 → 80 ≤ 90 → Grashof. The shortest link (25) is the only one that can fully rotate, so it must be the crank; ground a link adjacent to it for a crank-rocker, or ground the 25 itself for a drag-link.

P3 — Slider-crank r=60, L=240 mm. Find piston displacement from TDC at θ=90° (exact).

Tap to reveal x(θ) from crank centre = r·cosθ + √(L²−r²sin²θ) = 0 + √(240²−60²) = √(57600−3600) = √54000 = 232.4 mm. At TDC (θ=0): x = 60+240 = 300 mm. Displacement from TDC = 300 − 232.4 = 67.6 mm.

P4 — Instant centres of a 6-link mechanism: how many?

Tap to reveal N = n(n−1)/2 = 6×5/2 = 15 instant centres. (Primary ones at the joints; the rest located by Kennedy's theorem — the reason six-bar hand analysis is tedious and software wins.)

P5 — A block slides at 0.8 m/s along a link rotating at 15 rad/s. Coriolis acceleration?

Tap to reveal a_Cor = 2ωv = 2×15×0.8 = 24 m/s², perpendicular to the slide in the sense of ω. Add it vectorially to the normal (ω²r) and tangential (αr) terms for the true acceleration.

P6 — A crank-rocker's output rocker swings 40°. Its instant-centre lever ratios give ω_out/ω_in = 0.25 at one instant. Mechanical advantage there?

Tap to reveal MA = ω_in/ω_out = 1/0.25 = 4. Output force is 4× input force (ignoring friction) at that instant — but note MA varies through the cycle, peaking near toggle. Design force capacity for the worst (highest-load) position, not the average.

P7 — Whitworth quick-return: cutting stroke uses 220° of crank rotation, return 140°. Time ratio and what it means for productivity?

Tap to reveal Q = α/β = 220/140 = 1.57. The cutting stroke takes 1.57× as long as the return, so the tool spends more time cutting and returns quickly — the return is ~64 % of the cutting time, cutting idle time and raising strokes/min vs a symmetric mechanism.

P8 — Why does a parallelogram four-bar keep the coupler at constant orientation, and where is that used?

Tap to reveal Opposite links are equal, so the coupler stays parallel to the ground link at all times — it translates in a curved path without rotating. Uses: level-luffing cranes, parallel-lift platforms, drafting machines, some robot arms, and camera/tool-levelling rigs. (Beware its change-point at full extension where it can flip to the anti-parallelogram branch — a branch defect.)

P9 — A four-bar synthesised for 3 precision points reaches points 1 and 2 fine but only reaches point 3 after "popping" the assembly. Name and cure the defect.

Tap to reveal Branch (circuit) defect — point 3 sits on the opposite assembly circuit. Cure: re-select the moving/fixed pivots from the Burmester centre-point & circle-point curves so all three poses lie on one branch, or add link(s)/choose a different type. Detect by checking that the sign of the transmission angle (configuration) is consistent at all precision points.

P10 — An offset slider-crank has offset e = 20 mm, r = 50, L = 180. Qualitatively, what does the offset buy and cost?

Tap to reveal Buys: a built-in quick-return (unequal forward/return crank angles) and reduced piston side-thrust on the major-force side. Costs: asymmetric wear, a slightly more complex position relation (offset term inside the root), and the two dead-centres are no longer at 0/180° so stroke geometry shifts. It's the simplest way to get quick-return without a slotted-lever mechanism.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations, a symbol glossary, and seven traps.

Derivation 1 — Gruebler from body freedoms
Each free planar rigid body has 3 DOF. With n links (one grounded): free DOF = 3(n − 1) Each 1-DOF joint (R or P) allows 1 relative motion, so removes 3−1 = 2 DOF. Each 2-DOF higher joint removes 3−2 = 1 DOF. → M = 3(n−1) − 2j₁ − j₂ Spatial analogue: bodies have 6 DOF, joint of freedom f removes (6−f): M = 6(n−1) − Σ(6 − fi)
Derivation 2 — slider-crank position & the 2× harmonic
Geometry: x = r·cosθ + L·cosφ, with L·sinφ = r·sinθ (same height at the pin) cosφ = √(1 − (r/L)²sin²θ) → x = r·cosθ + L√(1 − (r/L)²sin²θ) Binomial expand the root (r/L = 1/n small): √(1 − k²sin²θ) ≈ 1 − ½k²sin²θ, and sin²θ = (1−cos2θ)/2 → x ≈ L + r·cosθ − (r²/4L)(1 − cos2θ) The (r²/4L)cos2θ term is the second harmonic → 2× inertia force. Differentiate twice → a ≈ rω²(cosθ + cos2θ/n) (primary + secondary).
Derivation 3 — Freudenstein's equation
Vector loop (a crank, b coupler, c rocker, d ground): a·cosθ₂ + b·cosθ₃ = d + c·cosθ₄ a·sinθ₂ + b·sinθ₃ = c·sinθ₄ Isolate the b (coupler) terms, square & add to eliminate θ₃: b² = a² + c² + d² − 2ad·cosθ₂ + 2cd·cosθ₄ − 2ac·cos(θ₂−θ₄) Divide by 2ac and group constants: K₁cosθ₄ − K₂cosθ₂ + K₃ = cos(θ₂−θ₄) K₁=d/a, K₂=d/c, K₃=(a²−b²+c²+d²)/2ac. Tangent half-angle → two roots (2 circuits).

Seven exam & design traps
  • 1
    Gruebler is a first guess: subtract redundant constraints and idle DOF before trusting M — paradox mechanisms move when the formula says "structure."
  • 2
    Grashof needs the two shortest identified correctly: it's s+l vs p+q, not "input vs output."
  • 3
    Two Freudenstein roots = two circuits: pick and keep one branch; don't mix precision points across circuits.
  • 4
    Coriolis only with sliding-on-rotating: present in slotted-lever/quick-return & governors; absent for a slider on the fixed frame.
  • 5
    Transmission angle is measured coupler-to-output, and you must check the minimum over the whole cycle, not one position.
  • 6
    Toggle is friend and foe: exploit it for force/self-lock; avoid it in drive linkages that must keep output control.
  • 7
    Short rods are rough: small n magnifies the 2× harmonic → more vibration; a design trade, not a free lunch.
