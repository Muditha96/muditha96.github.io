What Parametric Feature-Based CAD Really Is

SolidWorks is not a drawing program — it is a history-recording geometry compiler.

Definition

A parametric, feature-based, history-recording CAD system builds a part as an ordered recipe of features — each driven by dimensions and relations (the parameters). Change any parameter and the software re-executes the recipe (rebuild) to produce updated geometry. The model is the recipe, not the shape.

This idea was commercialised by Sam Geisberg's Pro/ENGINEER (PTC, 1988) and brought to the Windows mass market by SolidWorks (Jon Hirschtick's team, 1995). Every mainstream MCAD tool since — Inventor, Solid Edge, Creo, Fusion, Onshape, FreeCAD — follows the same paradigm, so everything in this module transfers. The competing paradigm, direct modeling, edits faces of the final shape with no history; modern packages blend both (Synchronous Technology, Fusion direct edits).

Under the hood — B-rep and the kernel

The shape itself is stored as a Boundary Representation (B-rep): a watertight shell of faces (trimmed analytic or NURBS surfaces), joined at edges, meeting at vertices, with topology (which face borders which) kept separate from geometry (the surface equations). The Parasolid kernel (Siemens-owned; also inside NX, Solid Edge, Onshape) performs the actual Booleans, fillets and offsets. When SolidWorks says a fillet "failed", that is Parasolid reporting it cannot construct a valid watertight B-rep for the requested radius.

Part file = feature history (recipe, parametric) + B-rep result (evaluated shape) Rebuild (Ctrl-B): history ── executes in order ──▶ new B-rep Neutral export (STEP): keeps B-rep only — the recipe is gone forever
The modeling pipeline

The parametric pipeline: sketch → constrain → feature → tree. The tree is the design story; the solid is just its current output.

Paradigm

1988

Pro/E introduces parametric history modeling; SolidWorks (1995) makes it mainstream on Windows.

Kernel

Parasolid

Booleans, fillets, offsets — the geometry engine shared with NX, Solid Edge and Onshape.

Key mindset

recipe

You are programming geometry. Edit the recipe, never fight the result.

The Sketch: States, Colours & Degrees of Freedom

Every solid starts as a 2D sketch on a plane — and every sketch is a small constraint-satisfaction problem.

Sketch fundamentals

A sketch lives on a plane or planar face. The three default planes (Front, Top, Right) intersect at the origin — the single most valuable free constraint in the system: anchor your first sketch to it, and the whole part inherits a meaningful coordinate frame (which later becomes your drawing views, mates and CNC datums — see A40). Sketch entities are lines, arcs, circles, ellipses, splines, points and construction geometry (reference-only centrelines and circles that guide but do not bound material).

Degrees of freedom — the arithmetic of sketching

Each entity carries degrees of freedom (DOF) in the sketch plane. Each relation or dimension removes DOF. The sketch state is pure bookkeeping:

Circle3centre (x, y) + radius
Arc5centre (x, y) + radius + two angles
Ellipse5centre + two semi-axes + orientation
Spline (n pts)2n + tangency handlesevery control point floats
Remaining DOF = Σ entity DOF − Σ (relations + dimensions) > 0 → under-defined (blue) = 0 → fully defined (black) conflicting → over-defined (red/yellow)

Relations & Dimensioning Discipline

Geometry first, numbers second — let shape rules do the work before any dimension is placed.

Geometric relations — the vocabulary
RelationActs onRemovesDesign meaning
Coincidentpoint ↔ point/edge2 (pt-pt) / 1 (pt-curve)"these connect"
Horizontal / Verticalline, 2 points1"aligned with the sheet"
Parallel / Perpendicular2 lines1"stay square"
Tangentcurve ↔ curve1"blend smoothly"
Concentric2 circles/arcs2"share a centre"
Equallines / radii1"one size rules them all"
Symmetric2 entities + centreline2"mirror about the middle"
Midpointpoint ↔ line2"always centred"
Collinear / Coradiallines / arcs2"same infinite line / same circle"

Order of preference: relations before dimensions. A rectangle held square by two parallel + one perpendicular relation and centred by a midpoint relation needs only two dimensions (width, height) — and it can never be accidentally skewed. The Fix relation is the sketching equivalent of hard-coding a magic number: it defines nothing about intent and breaks the moment anything moves.

Driving vs driven — who commands whom

A driving dimension commands geometry: change 80 → the line becomes 80. A driven (reference) dimension — shown in grey parentheses — merely reports a value that other constraints already determine. Trying to place a driving dimension on already-determined geometry triggers the over-defined dialog; accept "driven" when you only want to watch a value (and pair it with a sensor to alarm when it drifts out of range).

Dimension what you would inspect. If the drawing (or the QC bench, or the mating part) cares about the bolt-circle diameter, dimension the PCD — not two arbitrary hole-centre offsets that happen to imply it. Sketch dims should mirror functional dims (→ A21 GD&T).
Sketch hygiene — the professional checklist
  • 1
    Anchor to the origin. First sketch: one vertex, centre or midpoint coincident with the origin. A floating first sketch poisons every downstream feature.
  • 2
    Keep sketches simple. One feature's worth of geometry per sketch. A 60-entity mega-sketch that feeds five features is unmaintainable; five small sketches tell a story.
  • 3
    Fully define — always. Blue geometry moves when a colleague breathes on it. Black geometry moves only when a parameter says so.
  • 4
    Symmetry via centrelines. Model symmetric parts symmetric about the default planes — mirrored features stay linked, and the mass centre sits on the origin where dynamics wants it (→ A48).
  • 5
    No sketch fillets for big blends. Corner rounds belong in a fillet feature (editable, reorderable, suppressible) — not baked into sketch geometry where they multiply DOF and fail silently.
  • 6
    Use construction geometry generously. Centrelines, reference circles (PCDs), guide diagonals — free intent, zero material.
🧠 Quick check

A rectangle sketched from four separate lines has 16 DOF. You apply: 4 coincident corner relations, 1 horizontal, 1 vertical relation, and anchor one corner to the origin. How many dimensions complete it?

Tap to reveal ↓ Coincidents: 4 × 2 = 8. Horizontal + vertical: 2 (the other two edges follow through the corner coincidences). Origin anchor: 2. Total removed = 12, leaving 16 − 12 = 4… but H/V on two adjacent edges also implicitly squares the opposite edges through the corner chain, which the solver counts — in practice the status bar reads 2 DOF remaining: width and height. Two driving dimensions finish the job. Moral: relations did 14 of 16; numbers did 2.
Page 4 / 16 · Feature Toolbox I

Sketched Features: Extrude, Revolve, Sweep, Loft

Four verbs turn 2D profiles into 3D material — added (boss) or removed (cut).

The big four

Each verb exists as Boss (add material) and Cut (remove). Pick the verb that matches how the part is made — it usually matches how it should be modelled.

Extrude end conditions — small menu, big intent
End conditionBehaviourIntent it encodes
Blindfixed depth number"exactly this thick"
Through Allpierces every body, forever"always a through-hole, however thick the part gets" — robust
Up To Nextstops at the next face met"pocket floor follows the wall"
Up To Surface / Bodystops on a chosen face"lands on that boss, wherever it moves"
Offset From Surfacestops short of a face by a value"leave 2 mm of floor" — drill-depth intent
Mid-Planehalf depth each side of sketch"stay symmetric about this plane"

End conditions are design intent in disguise. A clearance hole cut Blind 12 mm silently becomes a blind pocket when the plate grows to 15 mm; cut Through All it survives every thickness change. Choosing Mid-Plane on the first boss keeps the part symmetric about a default plane for free.

Sweep & loft — the sculptural pair

Sweep = profile + path (+ optional guide curves, twist control). The profile must sit on the path (a pierce relation is the classic setup). Watch for self-intersection: a 10 mm-radius profile swept around a 8 mm-radius bend folds into itself and Parasolid rejects it — the same rule as minimum bend radius in pipe (→ A12/A13 plumbing). Loft = two or more sections skinned smoothly (+ guides, start/end tangency). Loft quality lives or dies on consistent section vertex counts and aligned connectors — mismatched connectors twist the skin. Use Boundary Surface when curvature continuity (C2) with neighbours matters.

Rule of thumb

90%

of machine parts are extrudes + revolves. Sweeps and lofts are the seasoning, not the meal.

Sweep killer

rpath < rprofile

Path bend radius tighter than the profile half-width ⇒ self-intersection ⇒ failure.

Loft killer

connectors

Twisted connector lines between sections ⇒ a corkscrewed skin. Drag them straight.

Page 5 / 16 · Feature Toolbox II

Applied Features: Fillet, Shell, Draft, Holes & Patterns

Features that act on existing solid geometry — no sketch required, but plenty of ordering rules.

Fillet & chamfer — last but not least

Fillet types: constant-size, variable-size, face fillet (between two face sets, can heal messy corners), full-round (tangent to three faces — caps ribs beautifully). Fillet order rules that professionals follow:

  • 1
    Structural fillets early, cosmetic fillets last. A cast rib blend that carries load is design geometry; a 0.5 mm edge-break is finishing. Suppressing late cosmetic fillets should never break anything.
  • 2
    Larger radii before smaller. Big blends consume edges the small ones would otherwise cling to; reversed order leaves slivers and failures.
  • 3
    Fillet after shell if the inside must stay sharp; fillet before shell if the inside must follow the outside round. Same two features, opposite products — see page 6.
  • 4
    Never reference fillet-created edges in sketches or mates — they evaporate the moment the fillet is edited, taking your references with them (the classic rebuild-error factory).
Shell, draft & rib — the moulding trio

Shell hollows the body to a wall thickness, removing selected faces. Draft tilts walls by an angle (1–3° typical) so a moulded or cast part releases from its tool — apply draft before shell so inner and outer walls stay parallel. Rib grows a stiffening wall from an open sketch up to the part body, with built-in draft and direction options. Together they are the injection-moulding feature set: draft → shell → ribs → fillets is the canonical order (→ mould-tool thinking pairs with A18's DfAM rules, where draft is irrelevant but wall thickness still rules).

Moulding order: Draft (1–3°) → Shell (t = const) → Ribs (0.5–0.7·t) → Fillets (≥0.5·t) Rib thinner than wall ⇒ avoids sink marks on the show surface (cooling shrinkage)
Hole Wizard & patterns — manufacturing-aware features

The Hole Wizard places standards-correct holes (drill sizes, counterbores for DIN 912 caps, countersinks, tapped holes with correct minor diameters and thread callouts) positioned by a sketch. It writes the callout into the feature, so drawings annotate themselves — hand-sketched circles never do. Advanced Hole stacks multi-step holes. Prefer wizard holes for anything a tap or standard fastener touches.

PatternDriverClassic use
Linear1–2 directions, spacing, countgrids of holes, cooling slots
Circularaxis + count (+ equal spacing)bolt circles, impeller blades
Curve / sketch-drivena curve or point sketchholes along a cam profile, decorative arrays
Mirrorplane/facesymmetric halves — link, don't remodel

Pattern the feature, not the geometry: pattern the wizard hole and every instance stays a proper hole (suppressible, callout-aware). "Geometry patterns" (faces only) rebuild faster but drop feature intelligence — a deliberate trade, not a default.

🧠 Quick check

A plastic enclosure needs 2 mm walls, 1.5° draft, internal ribs and rounded edges. In what order do the features go — and why does draft come first?

Tap to reveal ↓ Draft → Shell → Rib → Fillet. Draft first, so when Shell offsets the drafted outer walls inward, the inner walls inherit the same 1.5° and the wall stays a constant 2 mm everywhere (draft after shell tapers the thickness). Ribs at ~1.2–1.4 mm (0.6–0.7 × wall) to dodge sink marks; fillets last so they can be tuned or suppressed without disturbing the structure.
Page 6 / 16 · The Tree

Design Intent & the FeatureManager Tree

The tree is a program: parent–child references are its variables, feature order is its control flow.

What design intent actually means

Design intent is the set of behaviours you want the model to keep when it changes: what stays symmetric, what stays centred, what follows what, what stays equal. A model has good intent when the obvious edit produces the intended update — and nothing else moves.

Intent lives in five places: sketch relations ("slot stays centred"), dimension scheme ("wall measured from the datum face, not the far side"), end conditions ("hole is Through All"), feature order ("shell happens after all bosses"), and references ("rib sketch uses the Front plane, not face F0038 that a fillet might delete"). Ask the CSWP-style question of every choice: "if the length doubles tomorrow, what should happen?" — then constrain so that it does.

Parent–child: the dependency graph

Every feature that references another becomes its child. Deleting or failing a parent cascades — the yellow/red tree of sorrow.

Feature order changes the answer

Rebuild executes the tree top-down, so the same features in different order give different parts. The canonical example: Fillet then Shell — the shell offsets the filleted outer skin, so the inside corners are rounded with rin = rout − t. Shell then Fillet — the shell hollows a sharp box first, then the fillet rounds only the outer edges, leaving sharp (stress-raising, hard-to-mould) inner corners. Drag the rollback bar up the tree to travel back in time — insert features mid-history, diagnose which step broke, or just read how the designer thought.

Fillet(R6) → Shell(2) ⇒ inner radius = 6 − 2 = 4 mm (smooth inside — mouldable) Shell(2) → Fillet(R6) ⇒ inner corner = sharp (stress raiser, tool trap) Same two features. Different program. Different part.
Tree craftsmanship
  • 1
    Rename features ("Mount_Boss", "Vent_Slots") — a tree of Cut-Extrude17s is write-only code.
  • 2
    Group with folders once the tree passes ~20 features (Cosmetic, Machining, Mould).
  • 3
    Reference upstream, stable geometry — planes, origin, first-boss faces — never late fillet faces or pattern instances.
  • 4
    Test intent by torture: double the main dimension, halve it, rebuild (Ctrl-Q forces full re-execution). If something unexpected moves, the intent is wrong — fix the constraint, don't nudge the result.

Reference Geometry, Multibody & the Master Model

Planes, axes and skeleton sketches are the load-bearing walls of a robust model.

Reference geometry — the stable scaffolding
ReferenceDefined byUse it for
Planeoffset / angle / 3 points / mid-surface / tangentsketch platforms that survive any geometry edit
Axis2 planes / cylinder / 2 pointscircular patterns, revolve centrelines, mate targets
Pointintersections, arc centres, along-curvecurve-driven patterns, weld locators
Coordinate systempoint + axis directionsexport origins (STL, STEP), mass-prop reporting, CNC datums (→ A40 G54 thinking)

A sketch placed on a reference plane can never lose its host; a sketch placed on a model face dies with that face. The rule: faces for convenience, planes for permanence.

Skeleton / layout sketch technique

For parts with several interacting features (or assemblies with several parts), professionals draw one layout ("skeleton") sketch at the top of the tree holding the critical dimensions — centre distances, envelope, mounting PCD — and hang every downstream feature off it. Change the skeleton, the whole part follows coherently. This is horizontal modeling: many features referencing one parent, instead of a fragile daisy-chain where feature 12 references feature 11 references feature 10 (vertical modeling — one failure cascades through everything).

Vertical (fragile): Sk1 → F1 → F2 → F3 → F4 (chain: any break kills the rest) Horizontal (robust): Layout sketch ─┬→ F1 ├→ F2 ├→ F3 (siblings: failures stay local) └→ F4
Multibody parts & the master model

A part file may hold several disjoint solid bodies (uncheck "Merge result"). This unlocks two power workflows. (1) Tool bodies: model a punch as its own body, then Combine-Subtract it from the main body — the die cavity stays linked to the punch. (2) Master model: design a whole product (e.g., an enclosure) as one multibody part where the split lines, bosses and interfaces are decided once, then Save Bodies / Insert Part pushes each body into its own part file for detailing. Downstream parts stay linked: move a split line in the master, every derived part updates. This is top-down design without in-context assembly spaghetti — the interfaces live in one file that one person owns.

🧠 Quick check

Why does a rib sketched on the Front plane survive edits that a rib sketched on a boss face may not?

Tap to reveal ↓ The Front plane is origin geometry with no parents — nothing the user edits can delete it. A boss face is owned by the boss feature: change the boss's end condition, add a draft, or let a fillet consume that face, and the face ID vanishes or mutates; the rib's sketch loses its host and the dreaded "sketch plane missing" error appears. Faces for convenience, planes for permanence.

Equations, Global Variables & Configurations

One model, many parts: capture the rules once, generate the family forever.

Global variables & equations

Any dimension can be driven by an equation referencing global variables or other dimensions. The equation manager evaluates top-down each rebuild. Name variables like code: "WallT", "BoreD", "HoleN".

"WallT" = 3 ' global variable (mm) "BoreD" = 40 "FlangeD" = "BoreD" * 2.2 ' rule of thumb captured as a RULE "PCD" = ("BoreD" + "FlangeD") / 2 ' bolt circle centred in the flange ring "HoleN" = int("PCD" / 25) + 4 ' more holes as the flange grows D1@LPattern1 = 360 / "HoleN" ' equal angular spacing — always closes

Equations turn tribal rules ("flange ≈ 2.2 × bore", "one bolt per 25 mm of PCD") into executable engineering. The Modify dialog accepts arithmetic too (type = 100/3 into any dimension) — and IF() syntax handles piecewise rules: "RibT" = iif("WallT" > 2.5, 0.6 * "WallT", "WallT").

Configurations & design tables

Configurations are named variants inside one file — different dimension values, suppressed features, different materials. A design table (embedded Excel) drives configurations in bulk: rows = configurations, columns = dimensions/suppression states. This is how a single HexBolt.sldprt serves M6×20 through M16×80: one recipe, forty rows. Derived configurations nest (e.g., "M10" → "M10-machined", "M10-blank") for as-cast vs as-machined states of the same part.

Page 9 / 16 · Engineering Data

Materials, Mass Properties & Sensors

A CAD model is an engineering database — if you feed it a material and ask it questions.

Assigning material — more than a colour

Applying a material from the library (or a custom one with your supplier's datasheet values) attaches density, elastic modulus, yield strength, thermal properties to the body. Density powers mass properties; the elastic set powers simulation; the appearance is the least important part. For costed projects, custom materials with local supplier densities (e.g., the actual 6063-T5 extrusion you can buy in Colombo, not generic "Aluminium") keep BOM mass and cost honest.

Mass properties — free engineering answers
Tools ▸ Evaluate ▸ Mass Properties returns, per body or part: m mass = ρ · V V, A volume, surface area x̄, ȳ, z̄ centre of mass (in any coordinate system you pick) Ixx, Iyy, Izz moments of inertia — the J your servo sizing needs (→ A09) principal axes & principal moments

This is the bridge from CAD to machine design: reflected inertia for motor sizing (A09), centre-of-mass for robot payload checks (A35 effective mass), buoyancy volumes, casting weights for quoting. Override with care: purchased components (motors, gearboxes) whose internals you did not model should carry overridden mass and COM from the datasheet so assembly-level numbers stay true.

Sensors & design validation

Sensors watch a quantity — mass, a driven dimension, an interference, a simulation result — and flag when it leaves a range. A sensor on mass ("alert if > 1.2 kg") turns weight budgets from a release-day surprise into a live constraint. Pair with Design Checker or simple macros for drawing-standard audits. The theme of this whole page: the model should answer questions and raise alarms, not just look like the part.

Servo sizing

Izz

Rotor-axis inertia straight from Mass Properties — no hand integration (→ A09 inertia matching).

Weight budget

sensor

Mass sensor alarms the moment a rib or boss pushes the part over budget.

Honest BOM

override

Bought-out parts carry datasheet mass/COM overrides — assembly totals stay real.

🧠 Quick check

Your motor bracket model reports m = 0.42 kg, but the machined part weighs 0.55 kg. List three legitimate causes before you blame the scale.

Tap to reveal ↓ (1) Wrong material/density assigned — generic steel 7850 vs the actual 8000 kg/m³ stainless, or worse, the default "unspecified" material. (2) Model vs manufactured geometry differs — suppressed features, un-modelled weld fillets, or the machinist left stock where the model shows a pocket. (3) Configuration mismatch — you weighed the "machined" config's number against an "as-cast" physical part (or vice versa). Only after checking density, geometry and configuration do you question the scale.

Files, Templates & Export Formats

What survives when your model leaves home — and what dies at the border.

File discipline

Templates (.prtdot/.asmdot/.drwdot) carry units (MMGS!), drafting standard (ISO), materials, custom properties — set once per company, never fight defaults again. Custom properties (PartNo, Description, Material, Mass linked as "SW-Mass") auto-fill title blocks and BOMs. Pack and Go collects a part with all its references (drawings, design tables) for travel — e-mailing a lone .sldprt whose design table lives on your D: drive is the classic broken-reference generator. Rename/move files only via Pack and Go, SOLIDWORKS Explorer or PDM — Windows Explorer renames silently sever drawing↔model links.

Neutral formats are a one-way door: STEP in another CAD system is "dumb solid" — editable only by direct-modeling tools, its recipe unrecoverable. Send STEP to suppliers (they can't quietly re-parameterise your design); keep native + Pack and Go for anyone who must EDIT the intent.

STL export settings matter: the default coarse tessellation shows facets on a Ø50 cylinder. For FDM at 0.2 mm layers, deviation ≈ 0.02–0.05 mm and angle ≈ 5–10° is the sane window; finer only bloats the file (→ A18's slicer does the real resolving).

Drawings hand-off (preview)

Drawings deserve (and will get) their own module — but the part-side rule belongs here: model dimensions should be reusable as drawing dimensions (Insert Model Items). If your sketch dims match the functional/inspection dims (page 3), drawings become annotation, not re-derivation — and with MBD/PMI on AP242, tolerances can ride the 3D model with no drawing at all (→ A21 for what those tolerances must say).

Modeling Strategy, Performance & Quality

Anyone can model a part once. Professionals model parts that survive change, colleagues and years.

The resilient modeling recipe
  • 1
    Plan the first feature. The base feature should be the part's "core idea" (the block, the turned blank, the main plate) — oriented so default planes are the part's natural symmetry/datum planes.
  • 2
    Model the way it's made. A turned shaft = one revolve (reads like the lathe op); a machined block = boss + cuts (reads like the mill ops); a moulding = draft→shell→rib→fillet. Manufacturing-shaped trees are self-documenting and CAM-friendly (→ A40).
  • 3
    Stable references only — origin, planes, layout sketch, base-feature faces. Late features are consumers, never landlords.
  • 4
    Fully define every sketch; name every important feature; fold cosmetic work into a folder that can be suppressed wholesale for simulation/CAM configs.
  • 5
    Torture-test before release: Ctrl-Q rebuild, double the driving dims, check mass sensor, run Verification on Rebuild once (slower but catches invalid B-rep the fast check misses).

🧠 Quick check

A colleague's 180-feature model takes 40 s per rebuild while they detail cosmetic fillets at the tree's end. What single action gives them their afternoon back?

Tap to reveal ↓ Drag the Freeze bar below the last structural feature (~feature 150). Everything above it is locked out of rebuild computation, so each cosmetic-fillet edit re-executes only the last handful of features — rebuilds drop from 40 s to well under a second. (Then run Performance Evaluation and consider geometry-patterns for the worst offenders when unfreezing for release.)

Recap, Failure Modes & Self-Test

The whole module in one card — then the six ways part models die in the wild.

Eight ideas to keep
  • 1
    The model is a recipe, not a shape. Parametric history + B-rep result; the Parasolid kernel cooks it on every rebuild.
  • 2
    Sketch states are DOF arithmetic. Blue = freedoms remain, black = zero, red = contradiction. Ship black.
  • 3
    Relations before dimensions; dimension what you'd inspect. Geometry rules first, then the few numbers that matter (→ A21).
  • 4
    End conditions are intent. Through All ≠ Blind-that-happens-to-go-through.
  • 5
    Feature order is program flow. Fillet→Shell ≠ Shell→Fillet. The rollback bar is your debugger.
  • 6
    Reference stable parents — origin, planes, layout sketch — and the tree bends without breaking.
  • 7
    Equations + configurations turn one file into a product family. Rules once, variants forever.
  • 8
    Mass properties are engineering outputs. Density in, servo inertia and weight budgets out. STEP out = intelligence gone — export deliberately.
Six ways part models fail
⚠ The floating first sketch

Base sketch never anchored to the origin — every downstream feature inherits an arbitrary frame; drawings, mates and CNC datums all fight it forever.

⚠ Reference to a vanished face

A sketch or mate hung on a fillet face or pattern instance; one edit upstream deletes the face ID and the tree lights up yellow. Faces for convenience, planes for permanence.

⚠ The under-defined production sketch

Blue geometry drifts during an unrelated edit; the part changes silently; the error is found at goods-inward. Fully define, always.

⚠ Order-blind editing

Inserting a shell above existing fillets (or draft after shell) silently changes wall thickness and inner corners — the "same features, different part" trap.

⚠ Over-defined by decree

Conflicting dims accepted with Fix relations "to make the red go away" — the sketch is now a lie: numbers on screen that don't drive anything.

⚠ STEP round-tripping

Team edits a STEP re-import of their own native part (tree lost), then wonders why nothing is parametric. Neutral formats are for others; natives are the source of truth.

🧠 Final quiz — 5 questions

1 · A sketch shows black geometry but the status bar says "Under Defined". What's the likely culprit?

Tap to reveal ↓ Something invisible is still loose — usually a construction entity, a point, or the sketch's relationship to the origin (the whole black shape can still slide as a rigid body if no entity is anchored). Display/Delete Relations ▸ "Under defined" filters straight to the loose entities; anchoring one vertex to the origin typically finishes it.

2 · Fillet R8 then Shell 3 mm: what is the inner corner radius? Shell first instead: what changes?

Tap to reveal ↓ Fillet→Shell: inner radius = 8 − 3 = 5 mm — the shell offsets the rounded skin. Shell→Fillet: the shell hollows the sharp solid, then the fillet touches only outer edges — inner corners stay sharp (stress raisers, un-mouldable). Feature order is part of the design.

3 · Why is "Up To Surface" more intent-rich than a Blind depth that currently gives the same result?

Tap to reveal ↓ Blind stores a frozen number; Up To Surface stores a relationship. When the target face moves (part grows, boss moves), Up To Surface follows automatically — the Blind value silently becomes wrong. End conditions encode what should happen on change, which is the definition of design intent.

4 · Your design table drives 40 bolt sizes. A new engineer adds "M12-special" by editing geometry directly in that config. What breaks?

Tap to reveal ↓ The design table and the model now disagree: table cells no longer match the config's actual dimensions. Next time the table is edited and applied, it overwrites the manual change without warning — "M12-special" reverts. Rule: once a table drives a parameter, the table is the single source of truth; add a column/row, never side-edit.

5 · A supplier asks for "the CAD". What do you send for (a) a quote for machining, (b) co-development where they modify the design?

Tap to reveal ↓ (a) STEP AP214/AP242 (+ PDF drawing with tolerances → A21): exact geometry, no recipe to leak, universally readable. (b) Native files via Pack and Go (part + drawings + design tables + templates) so the tree, equations and configs survive — plus agreement on SolidWorks version, since natives don't open backwards.
Memory Hook

"Blue is a warning, black is a promise —
model the recipe, not the shape."

Sketch → relate → dimension → feature. The tree tells the design story; the solid is just today's output.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems, fully worked — DOF bookkeeping, parametric rules, feature-order geometry and pattern math.

WE-1 · Constraint counting a slot sketch
A straight slot: 2 parallel lines + 2 semicircular end arcs, centreline through origin. Entity DOF: 2 lines × 4 = 8; 2 arcs × 5 = 10; centreline (construction) = 4 → Σ = 22 Relations: 4 coincident endpoint joins (arc-end to line-end).......... 4 × 2 = 8 2 tangent (arc to line at each junction pair)*............. 2 × 1 = 2 2 equal-radius arcs....................................... 1 lines parallel............................................ 1 centreline horizontal + midpoint-at-origin................ 1 + 2 = 3 slot symmetric about centreline (symmetric relation)...... 2 Σ removed = 17 Remaining = 22 − 17 = 5 → the solver still reports what's loose: slot LENGTH, slot WIDTH (radius), and residual arc-angle freedoms the joins absorb in practice — the status bar typically shows 2 DOF: L and W. Two dimensions (centre-to-centre length, width) → fully defined.

Discussion: hand-counting reaches "about 2" rather than exactly 2 because coincident + tangent pairs at the same junction share a freedom the solver de-duplicates. The exam skill is the method: budget entity DOF, subtract relations at face value, and know that junction stacks can overlap. (SolidWorks' straight-slot tool applies this whole bundle in one click — now you know what it did.)

WE-2 · Equation-driven flange family
Rules: "FlangeD" = 2.2·"BoreD"; "PCD" = ("BoreD"+"FlangeD")/2; "HoleN" = int("PCD"/25)+4 For "BoreD" = 60 mm: FlangeD = 2.2 × 60 = 132 mm PCD = (60 + 132)/2 = 96 mm HoleN = int(96/25) + 4 = 3 + 4 = 7 holes Pattern angle = 360/7 = 51.43° Sanity: hole pitch = π·PCD/N = π·96/7 = 43.1 mm — comfortably > 2.5 × a Ø12 bolt hole. ✓

Discussion: the int() rule guarantees hole count grows stepwise with size — 7 holes is unusual (odd counts complicate on-site alignment), so a production rule would round to even: "HoleN" = 2*int("PCD"/50)+4 → 6 holes at 60° for this bore. Writing the better rule is the MSc-level answer: equations encode judgement, not just arithmetic.

WE-3 · Shell/fillet order on a die-cast housing
Housing outer corner R10, wall t = 2.5 mm, die-cast ADC12. Order A — Fillet(R10) → Shell(2.5): r_inner = 10 − 2.5 = 7.5 mm Order B — Shell(2.5) → Fillet(R10): r_inner = 0 (sharp) Stress check (pressure-tight housing, internal corner): K_t at sharp internal corner ≈ 3+; at r/t = 7.5/2.5 = 3 → K_t ≈ 1.2 Casting check: sharp internal corner = hot-spot + die-wear site → reject. Order A is the only manufacturable, fatigue-sane program.

Discussion: the same two features produce a 2.5× difference in stress-concentration factor and decide whether the die shop accepts the part. Feature order is not CAD pedantry — it is geometry, and geometry is load path. In review, read trees bottom-up asking "what would reordering change?"

WE-4 · Circular pattern coordinates for inspection
6 holes, PCD 96 mm (R = 48), first hole at 15° from +X, part origin at centre. Hole k (k = 0…5): θ_k = 15° + k·60° x_k = 48·cos θ_k, y_k = 48·sin θ_k k=0: (46.36, 12.42) k=1: (12.42, 46.36) k=2: (−33.94, 33.94) k=3: (−46.36, −12.42) k=4: (−12.42, −46.36) k=5: (33.94, −33.94) (mm)

Discussion: these are exactly the numbers a CMM programmer or the CNC setter (→ A40, G54 at the part origin) reads from your model. Because the sketch anchored the pattern axis at the origin and dimensioned the PCD (page 3 discipline), Mass Properties, the drawing table and the probe program all agree without translation. A floating first sketch would have made every one of these coordinates arbitrary.

Problem Set — 10 Questions

Work each one before revealing. They escalate from bookkeeping to design judgement.

P1 · How many DOF does a sketch circle have, and what is the minimum constraint set to fully define one concentric with the origin?

Tap to reveal ↓3 DOF (x, y, r). Concentric-with-origin (a coincident centre) removes 2; one diameter dimension removes the last. Two inputs total — relations did two-thirds of the work.

P2 · A line's endpoints are coincident with two fully-defined circles' centres. How many DOF does the line have left?

Tap to reveal ↓Zero. Each pt-pt coincident removes 2 of the line's 4 DOF; both ends tied to defined points leaves nothing free. The line is fully defined with no dimensions at all.

P3 · Give two reasons Through All beats Blind 20 mm for a clearance hole in an 18 mm plate.

Tap to reveal ↓(1) Intent: if the plate thickens to 25 mm, Through All still pierces; Blind 20 becomes a blind pocket silently. (2) Review/CAM clarity: Through All declares "clearance hole" to every reader and post-processor; Blind invites "why 20?" archaeology.

P4 · A revolve fails with "sketch crosses the centreline". What's wrong geometrically, and what's the legal edge case?

Tap to reveal ↓Profile area on both sides of the axis would sweep overlapping, self-intersecting material — Parasolid can't build a valid B-rep. Legal edge case: geometry touching the centreline (a point or coincident edge) is fine — that's how you revolve a solid shaft rather than a tube.

P5 · Sweep a Ø16 circular profile along a path with a 7 mm bend radius. What happens and why?

Tap to reveal ↓Failure (or a garbage self-intersected body): the inner fibre of the sweep needs the path bend radius > profile half-width (8 mm). At r = 7 < 8 the inner wall folds through itself. Same physics as minimum tube-bend radius: r ≥ ~2·D in real plumbing.

P6 · Why should the rib-thickness rule for a moulded part be an equation ("RibT" = 0.6·"WallT") rather than a number?

Tap to reveal ↓The 0.5–0.7 × wall rule exists to prevent sink marks; it must track the wall. As an equation, thickening the wall to 3 mm automatically moves ribs to 1.8 mm; as a number, the rule silently breaks and the sink mark appears on the A-surface at T1 samples — the most expensive possible discovery point.

P7 · Mirror-the-feature vs mirror-the-sketch-geometry: name the two maintenance differences.

Tap to reveal ↓(1) Feature mirror stays linked: edit the seed, the mirror follows; mirrored sketch entities are independent copies that drift apart. (2) Feature mirror is suppressible/reorderable as one tree item and carries the seed's intelligence (wizard-hole callouts etc.); sketch copies carry none.

P8 · A part has configs "as-cast" and "machined". Which features are suppressed in which, and which config does the casting supplier's STEP come from?

Tap to reveal ↓"as-cast": machining cuts (bores, faces, tapped holes) suppressed; draft + machining stock present. "machined": everything unsuppressed (stock consumed by cuts). The foundry gets STEP of as-cast; the machine shop gets machined (plus as-cast for fixture design). One file, two truths, zero duplicate models.

P9 · Your Izz from Mass Properties feeds a servo sizing (→ A09). List three model errors that corrupt it and their signature.

Tap to reveal ↓(1) Wrong/default density → mass and I scale together (check m first). (2) Mass Properties taken about the wrong axis/coordinate system → I wildly off while m is right; select the rotation-axis coordinate system explicitly. (3) Suppressed features or missing bought-out overrides in this config → both m and I low; verify config and overrides. Rule: validate m and COM before trusting any I.

P10 · Design review: a tree reads Sketch1(floating) → Boss → 14 features referencing each other in a chain → fillets → shell. Write the three-line verdict.

Tap to reveal ↓(1) Anchor Sketch1 to the origin and re-hang the frame on default planes — every datum downstream is currently arbitrary. (2) Break the vertical chain: introduce a layout sketch and re-reference the 14 features horizontally so failures stay local. (3) Move the shell above the cosmetic fillets (and check every inner corner) — shell-after-fillet vs fillet-after-shell is a different part; decide which one you actually mean.

Key Derivations, Glossary & Exam Traps

The three pieces of theory worth deriving once — and the seven traps that cost marks and rebuilds.

Derivation 1 · Sketch DOF and the constraint-counting rule
A rigid body in the plane has 3 DOF (x, y, θ). Sketch entities are NOT rigid bodies — they are defined by their defining points/parameters: line = 2 endpoints = 4; circle = centre + r = 3; arc = centre + r + 2 angles = 5. Each scalar equation of constraint removes exactly 1 DOF: pt-on-curve = 1 eqn; pt-pt coincident = 2 eqns (x and y); tangent = 1; symmetric = 2. Fully defined ⇔ number of independent constraint equations = Σ entity DOF Over-defined ⇔ equations exceed DOF or are dependent (consistent but redundant — SolidWorks flags both; "dependent" is why a correct-looking dim can still turn things yellow).

Insight: this is the same Gruebler/Kutzbach bookkeeping as mechanism mobility (→ A48 page 2) applied to sketch entities — CAD constraint solvers and linkage DOF counts are one theory wearing two hats.

Derivation 2 · Why shell-after-fillet gives rin = rout − t
Shell = inward offset surface at distance t. The offset of a cylinder of radius R (about the same axis) is a cylinder of radius R − t. A convex fillet face is a partial cylinder of radius R → its inward offset is radius R − t. Hence inner corner radius = R − t, valid while t < R. At t = R the offset degenerates to a sharp edge; t > R self-intersects → shell failure — which is why "shell fails on the filleted corner" always means t ≥ smallest fillet radius.

Insight: most mysterious shell failures are this inequality in disguise. Audit the smallest concave/convex radius the offset must pass through; either enlarge the fillet or thin the wall.

Derivation 3 · Rebuild as a topological sort
Features + references form a directed acyclic graph (DAG): edge parent → child. Rebuild = execute features in tree (temporal) order, which must be a valid topological order of the DAG — a child may never precede its parent. Reordering by drag is legal ⇔ the move preserves topological order (SolidWorks blocks moves that would put a child above its parent). Circular references are impossible within a part's DAG — but in-context assembly references can create rebuild loops ACROSS files: A drives B drives A. That is why external references get lock/break tools and a bad reputation.

Seven exam & workshop traps
  • 1
    "Defined because it looks right." Colour, not appearance, is the verdict — drag-test or read the status bar. Blue geometry in a submitted model loses marks and sleep.
  • 2
    Counting a symmetric relation as 1 DOF. It constrains both mirror partners — 2 equations. Coincident pt-pt is also 2, pt-on-line is 1. Know the per-relation count.
  • 3
    Shell thickness ≥ smallest fillet radius → offset self-intersection → failure (Derivation 2). Check radii before blaming the software.
  • 4
    Editing a design-table-driven dimension by hand. The table wins on next apply and reverts you silently (WE-Q4). Tables are single-source-of-truth.
  • 5
    Assuming STEP keeps parametrics. Every neutral format ships the B-rep only. The recipe never leaves the native file.
  • 6
    Mid-plane vs blind on symmetric parts. Exam favourite: a Blind boss "centred by dimension" loses symmetry the moment the depth changes; Mid-Plane keeps the default plane as the true symmetry plane and mirrors stay valid.
  • 7
    Units ambush. IPS templates in an MMGS world (or a design table in inches) yields 25.4× errors that survive until manufacture. Fix the template, print units on the drawing, and sanity-check mass against a hand estimate (ρ·V envelope).
