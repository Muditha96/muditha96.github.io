📡Industrial Communication from Zero

Every protocol in this library — Modbus, PROFINET, EtherCAT, IO-Link, CAN, OPC UA, TSN — is the same six ideas arranged differently: a voltage on a wire, a bit, a frame, a checksum, a rule about who may talk, and a promise about when. Learn those six once and the eight advanced modules stop being eight subjects and become eight variations. This primer starts at “what is a signal” and ends with you able to read any protocol datasheet in the field.

A Plant That Made a Nine-Million-Rupee Decision on Data That Was Twenty-Seven Seconds Old

COLD OPEN — KADUWELA, PLASTIC INJECTION MOULDING CORE

Eighteen injection moulding machines. Last year the plant added energy meters and a machine-data system: an RS-485 cable daisy-chained around the shop floor, Modbus RTU at 9600 baud, feeding a SCADA package that logs every five seconds and draws the utilisation dashboard the production manager looks at every morning.

The system “works”. There are no alarms. The dashboard is populated, the trends are smooth, and machine 12 has been showing 94 % utilisation for months — which is why the plant was three weeks from ordering a nineteenth machine for LKR 9.2 million to relieve it.

Machine 12's own cycle counter says its real utilisation is 63 %. Nothing is broken. Three ordinary communication faults — one in each of the three layers this primer teaches — are between the shop floor and that dashboard, and every one of them is invisible from the screen.

FAULT 1 — SOMEBODY TURNED A LINE INTO A STAR CORE

RS-485 is a transmission line. It must be one cable, run from one end of the plant to the other, with a matched resistor at each end — and nothing hanging off the middle. When machines 7, 11 and 15 were added, an electrician did the natural thing and ran a spur to each from the nearest point on the trunk: 12 m, 18 m and 9 m of cable, hanging off the middle of the line like branches.

Every unterminated branch reflects the signal back down the cable. The rule for how long a branch may safely be: signal travels in cable at roughly 0.2 m per nanosecond the transceiver's edge takes 100 ns to rise so the edge is physically 100 × 0.2 = 20 m long a stub must be under about 1/10 of that → 2 m maximum theirs are 12 m, 18 m and 9 m — six to nine times over. Result: the reflected edge arrives back while the real bit is still being read, and the receiver sometimes reads the sum of the two. Errors are random, worse at some addresses than others, and they look exactly like "a faulty meter".

Two termination resistors were also missing entirely — the installer fitted one, at the panel, because that was the end he could see.

FAULT 2 — THE POLLING BUDGET NOBODY ADDED UP CORE

Modbus RTU is strictly one question at a time. The SCADA asks a machine for some registers, waits for the answer, then asks the next machine. So the update rate of the whole system is the sum of every question and every answer — and, critically, of every timeout.

At 9600 baud, 8 data bits + even parity + 1 stop = 11 bits per character one bit = 1/9600 = 104.17 µs one character = 11 × 104.17 = 1.146 ms request (8 bytes) = 9.17 ms device turnaround (typical) = 10.00 ms response (10 registers, 25 bytes) = 28.65 ms silent interval t3.5 = 4.01 ms ------------------------------------------------ one poll = 51.82 ms The SCADA reads 40 registers per machine in 4 blocks of 10, because whoever configured it copied the vendor's example: 16 live machines × 4 polls × 51.82 ms = 3.32 s And then the part that matters. Two machines were scrapped last year and never removed from the poll list. Each dead address is asked 4 times, each attempt waits a 1000 ms timeout, and the driver retries 3 times before giving up: 2 machines × 4 polls × 3 tries × 1000 ms = 24.00 s ------------------------------------------------ TOTAL SCAN TIME = 27.32 s Two absent devices own 88 % of the entire network's time.

Now the fault that actually cost LKR 9.2 million. The SCADA logs every 5 seconds, but the underlying value only changes every 27 seconds. So five or six identical readings are logged for every one real measurement. The machine cycle is 42 s. Sampling a 42-second process every 27 seconds and then repeating each sample five times does not produce a smooth trend — it produces an aliased one, and aliased data looks more confident than the real thing, not less. Machine 12's idle periods fell into the gaps.

📈

Sampling faster than your data updates does not give you more information. It gives you the same number repeated, and it makes a noisy, sparse measurement look like a clean, dense one. Before you trust any industrial dashboard, ask one question: how often does the number underneath actually change? If nobody in the building knows, the dashboard is decoration.

FAULT 3 — THE TWO ENDS OF THE PLANT ARE NOT AT THE SAME ZERO STRETCH

RS-485 works by comparing two wires against each other rather than against ground, which is why it survives a factory at all (page 5). But the receiver still has to be able to see both wires: they must stay within a window of roughly −7 V to +12 V relative to the receiver's own ground. Outside that window the comparison is meaningless.

The trunk runs 240 m across a shop floor with a 55 kW extruder on it. measured ground-to-ground voltage, machines idle 0.4 V measured with the extruder running 9.4 V measured transient at extruder start 31 V peak −7 V to +12 V window → exceeded on every start Symptom: a burst of CRC errors every time the extruder starts, six or seven times a shift, which the SCADA logs as "communication fault" and then recovers from — so nobody investigates it.

PREDICT FIRST
You inherit an industrial network that drops messages intermittently. Where is the fault most likely to be?
B — and it is not close. The rule of thumb every experienced integrator will give you is roughly 60 % physical layer, 30 % configuration, 10 % everything else. It is a field heuristic rather than a measured statistic, but it will point you at the right half of the problem more often than any other single sentence in this primer. The reason is structural: the physical layer is the only part installed by hand, in a hurry, by someone who cannot see the consequence — a missing resistor, a stub, a shield earthed at both ends, a cable run beside a VFD. D is the most expensive wrong answer, because a device that reports errors is usually the victim of the fault rather than its cause, and replacing it moves the symptom to its neighbour. The discipline: never open a protocol manual until you have checked termination, stub length, shield earthing and cable routing.
CHECKPOINT

Three faults, three layers: a wire problem, a timing problem, and a grounding problem. Notice that not one of them was about Modbus. That is the thesis of this primer — the protocol is rarely the hard part, and you can diagnose most of an industrial network before you know which protocol it speaks.

What a Signal Actually Is — and Why Digital Survives a Factory

START FROM SOMETHING YOU ALREADY KNOW CORE

A communication cable carries a voltage that changes with time. That is the whole of the physical layer. If electricity is unfamiliar, borrow the plumbing you already understand — the analogy is not a teaching crutch, it is a genuine mathematical correspondence that engineers use in both directions.

Fluid / mechanicalElectricalWhat it means on a cable
Pressure (bar)Voltage (V)The “push”. A signal is a changing voltage.
Flow rate (l/min)Current (A)How much charge moves. Signal wires carry almost none.
Restriction (an orifice)Resistance (Ω)Termination resistors, cable resistance
Accumulator (stores pressure)Capacitance (F)Cable capacitance — it rounds off your edges and limits length

That last row is worth pausing on, because it is the single best gift your mechanical background gives you here. An unterminated cable is hydraulic water hammer. A pressure wave travels down a pipe, hits a closed valve, and comes back. An electrical edge travels down a cable, hits an unmatched end, and comes back. Engineers fix both the same way — make the far end look like more pipe, or more cable. A 120 Ω termination resistor is an orifice sized to swallow the wave.

ANALOGUE, DIGITAL, AND WHY THE FACTORY CHOSE DIGITAL CORE

An analogue signal carries its meaning in its exact value: 12.4 mA means 52.5 °C, so anything that changes the value changes the meaning. Noise added anywhere along the cable is permanent and undetectable — the receiver has no way to know the sender meant 12.4 and not 12.6.

A digital signal carries its meaning in which side of a threshold it is on. Add noise and the value moves, but as long as it stays on the correct side of the line, the meaning is untouched — and the receiver can rebuild a perfectly clean copy. That single property, called regeneration, is why a digital network can cross a factory through five switches and arrive bit-identical, and why an analogue signal cannot.

  • 1Digital does not degrade gracefully — it degrades suddenly. This is the trade. An analogue signal gets slowly worse and you can see it coming on a trend. A digital link is perfect, perfect, perfect, then fails. There is no warning on the screen, which is why error counters matter so much (page 7) — they are the only early warning that exists.
  • 2The noise in a factory is not random hiss. It is switching: VFD output edges (A08), contactor coils collapsing, welding, and the inrush of a big motor. It arrives as bursts, coupled in by capacitance from a power cable running alongside yours — which is why cable segregation is a rule, not a preference.
  • 3Faster edges are more fragile, not more robust. A 100 Mbit/s Ethernet edge is a hundred times shorter than a 9600-baud one, so a cable defect a hundred times smaller is enough to ruin it. Going faster is not automatically going better, and a great deal of industrial practice exists to manage exactly this.
CHECKPOINT

You should be able to say why a factory network is digital rather than analogue, in one sentence, using the word threshold. And you should have the water-hammer picture in your head for what an unterminated cable does — it comes back on page 5.

Hex exists for one reason: it lines up with binary. 0x1F4 = 0001 1111 0100 = 500. When a manual tells you “bit 11 of register 0x0040 is the fault flag”, hex is what lets you find bit 11 by eye instead of by long division. Learn to read it and half of industrial documentation stops being cryptic.

THE FOUR TRAPS THAT COST REAL MONEY CORE

A register arrives as sixteen ones and zeroes. Nothing in the message says what they mean. The meaning lives entirely in the device's manual, and every one of these four mistakes ships to production somewhere every day.

TRAP 1 — SCALING. A pressure transmitter sends 6382. Manual: "value × 0.01 bar". True reading 63.82 bar. Read as raw: 6382 bar (obvious, harmless) Read with the wrong factor ×0.1: 638.2 bar (obvious) Read with a plausible wrong factor: 23.08 bar The dangerous errors are the plausible ones. Always verify a live reading against a physical gauge before you trust a scale factor. TRAP 2 — SIGNED OR UNSIGNED. A temperature register reads 65526. As unsigned 16-bit: 65526 → "sensor fault!" As signed 16-bit: −10 → a cold morning. Same bits. The difference is one checkbox in the SCADA tag. TRAP 3 — WORD ORDER. A 32-bit energy total in registers 40001-2. Registers contain 0x0001 and 0x86A0. High word first: 0x000186A0 = 100,000 kWh Low word first: 0x86A00001 = 2,258,632,705 kWh Both are "correct" Modbus. The standard never defined it, so every vendor chose. This single ambiguity is the most common fault in industrial data collection anywhere in the world. TRAP 4 — BIT-PACKED STATUS. A drive's status word reads 0x0037. That is not "55 of something". It is eight separate flags: 0000 0000 0011 0111 |||| ||||-- bit 0 ready = 1 |||| |||--- bit 1 running = 1 |||| ||---- bit 2 fault = 1 ◀ the one that matters |||| |----- bit 3 at speed = 0 ||||------- bit 4 local/remote = 1 Logging 0x0037 as a number and trending it is meaningless.

The single most useful tool a beginner can have in front of a live Modbus device. Type the raw register values a device is giving you and try every interpretation until one of them looks like reality. That is not cheating — it is exactly how commissioning engineers work when a vendor's manual is thin.

How to use it on a real device. Pick a value you can verify physically — a temperature you can measure, a pressure with a gauge next to it, an energy total you can read off the meter's own display. Then write the answer in the plant's tag documentation, because the next person will otherwise repeat all of this.

PREDICT FIRST
An energy meter reports a total that jumps from 65,530 to 4 between two readings. What has happened?
C — it rolled over, and the true consumption is 10 units, not −65,526. A 16-bit unsigned counter's largest value is 65,535; the next increment wraps to 0. So 65,530 → 65,535 → 0 → 4 is a normal increase of ten. Any software that subtracts the previous reading from the current one will record −65,526, and a monthly total built by summing those differences will be catastrophically wrong once per rollover — usually as a huge negative spike that somebody then “fixes” by taking the absolute value, which quietly turns it into a huge positive one. The correct handling is to detect the wrap (new value less than old value, on a counter you know only increases) and add the modulus: difference = new − old + 65,536. Better still, ask the vendor for a 32-bit version of the same register, which most meters have and most integrators never look for — at 32 bits the same meter rolls over roughly every seventy years instead of every few days. A is the trap: it is the intuitive answer, and a checksum would have caught a genuine corruption (page 7), so a value that passes CRC and still looks wrong is almost never a transmission fault.
CHECKPOINT

Four traps to carry: scaling, signed-ness, word order, bit packing. Between them they cause more wrong industrial data than every wiring fault combined — and unlike wiring faults, they produce numbers that look completely reasonable.

The Serial Frame — How Eight Bits Cross One Wire

WHY SERIAL WON CORE

You could send eight bits on eight wires at once. Early computers did. Industry does not, and the reason is the same reason a factory does not run eight hydraulic hoses where one will do: every wire is a cost, a connector pin, a failure and a length limit. Worse, eight parallel wires must all arrive at the same instant, and over 200 m they will not — a problem called skew that gets harder the faster you go.

So industry sends the bits one after another down a single path and pays for it in time. Everything on this page is the bookkeeping of that decision.

THE UART FRAME — THE SHAPE OF ONE CHARACTER CORE

The receiver has no separate clock wire, so it must work out where each bit begins from the data itself. The trick is nearly a century old and still in use everywhere: hold the line idle at logic 1, and announce every character with a single start bit of logic 0. The receiver sees that falling edge, waits half a bit time to land in the middle of the start bit, then samples every bit time after that.

The notation you will see on every device: 9600 8E1 9600 = baud bits per second on the wire 8 = data bits E = parity None / Even / Odd 1 = stop bits frame = 1 start + 8 data + 1 parity + 1 stop = 11 bits to deliver 8 bits of payload → efficiency 8/11 = 72.7 % Over a quarter of a serial link is spent on framing, always. Modbus RTU's default is 8E1. If a device is set to 8N1 (10 bits) and the master to 8E1 (11 bits), you get parity and framing errors that look exactly like a cable fault — and the fix is a dropdown box.
INTERACTIVE — BIT TIME, FRAME TIME, AND WHAT BAUD REALLY BUYS CORE

Defaults are the Kaduwela plant on page 1: 9600 8E1, a request of 8 bytes and a response of 25. Now raise the baud rate and watch the total move far less than you expect — because the device's own thinking time does not care how fast your cable is.

This is why "just increase the baud rate" stops helping around 19200: past that point the silent interval stops shrinking.
CHECKPOINT

Be able to compute, without the tool: at 9600 8E1, one character takes 1.146 ms. Everything else on a serial network is that number multiplied by a byte count and added to a turnaround. Most “the network is too slow” arguments end the moment somebody does this arithmetic.

The Physical Layer — the 60 % of Faults That Live in the Cable

THE ONE IDEA THAT MAKES INDUSTRIAL NETWORKS POSSIBLE CORE

A factory is electrically hostile: motors switch, drives chop, contactors collapse, and every one of those events couples a spike into every cable nearby. The single invention that lets data survive it is differential signalling, and it is worth understanding properly because it explains RS-485, CAN, PROFIBUS, Ethernet and single-pair Ethernet all at once.

Send the signal on two wires, twisted together, one carrying the inverse of the other. Any interference that couples into the pair couples into both wires almost equally, because they occupy the same physical space. The receiver does not look at either wire — it looks at A minus B, and in that subtraction the shared noise cancels. That is why the pair must be twisted: twisting is what guarantees both wires see the same field.

RS-485 in numbers — memorise these four: driver output ≥ 1.5 V differential into a loaded bus receiver threshold ±200 mV — a 7.5× margin, which is why it works at 1200 m common-mode range −7 V to +12 V — the limit fault 3 broke termination 120 Ω at each end, matching the cable

The common-mode window is the one people forget. Cancellation only works while the receiver can still see both wires within its input range. Two ends of a 240 m plant sitting 31 V apart during a motor start is outside that window, and no amount of twisting helps — the fix is galvanic isolation, which breaks the ground path entirely.

SIX FIELD RULES THAT PREVENT MOST FAULTS CORE
  • 1Terminate both ends, and only both ends. 120 Ω at each physical extremity of an RS-485 trunk — not at the panel because that is convenient, and never in the middle. Three terminations overload the driver; one gives you reflections. Check with a meter: an unpowered, properly terminated bus reads about 60 Ω across A–B (two 120s in parallel). That measurement takes ten seconds and settles the argument.
  • 2No stubs longer than about 2 m. One cable in, one cable out, at every device. If a device is far from the trunk, move the trunk — do not spur to it. This is the single most-violated rule in industrial wiring.
  • 3Earth the shield at one end only. Earthed at both ends, the shield becomes a conductor between two different earth potentials and carries current — a ground loop, which injects exactly the noise the shield was fitted to stop. One end, at the panel, every time.
  • 4Keep data cable away from power cable. The practical rule is 200 mm from any cable feeding a VFD or a contactor, and cross at 90° where you must cross. A parallel run of 20 m beside a drive output is a capacitor, and it will couple the drive's switching edges straight into your data.
  • 5Use the cable the standard specifies. Characteristic impedance is a real physical property — 120 Ω for RS-485/PROFIBUS, 100 Ω for Ethernet. Ordinary screened instrument cable is typically 50–80 Ω, and every joint between mismatched cable is a small reflection. It works at 9600 baud over 50 m and it will not work when the plant expands.
  • 6Fit isolation on any run that crosses buildings, long distances, or a big load. An isolated repeater breaks the common-mode problem permanently and costs less than one day of chasing intermittent faults.
THE SENTENCE TO CARRY
“One line, two terminations, no stubs, one earth.”
PREDICT FIRST
A 400 m RS-485 network works perfectly with 12 devices. Adding a 13th, 3 m from the trunk, makes the whole bus unreliable — including devices far away from the new one. Why?
C — and the clue is in the words “including devices far away”. A reflection is not a local phenomenon. The edge travels down the spur, finds an unmatched end, and comes back onto the trunk as a second copy of itself, which then propagates in both directions to every device on the bus. So a fault created at one point degrades everything — and it degrades the most distant devices worst, because their own signal is weakest by the time the echo arrives. This is precisely why the symptom is so confusing in the field: the device you just added often works fine, and its neighbours three hundred metres away start failing. A is wrong on the numbers (the standard load limit is 32, and modern 1/8-load transceivers allow 256). D is a real failure mode but the wrong one here — excess load lowers the differential voltage gradually and symmetrically, which shows up as errors that get steadily worse with each device added, not as a step change on device thirteen. The fix is not a resistor on the spur, which would only add load; it is to re-route the trunk so the device sits on the line.
CHECKPOINT

You now hold the four RS-485 numbers (1.5 V, 200 mV, −7/+12 V, 120 Ω) and the 60 Ω meter check. That is enough to commission or condemn most serial networks in Sri Lankan industry, and it is the foundation A01 builds its RS-485 chapter on.

Topology — the Shape of the Network Decides How It Fails

FIVE SHAPES, FIVE FAILURE MODES CORE

Topology is not an aesthetic choice. The shape of the wiring determines what happens when one thing breaks, and that is usually the only question a plant actually cares about.

THINGS THAT ARE TRUE IN A FACTORY AND NOT IN AN OFFICE STRETCH
  • 1A ring is not automatically redundant. The cable makes a loop; a protocol makes it redundant. Without one, a loop is a broadcast storm that takes the network down in seconds. MRP recovers in about 200 ms, RSTP in a second or more, and PRP/HSR in zero because they send everything twice on both paths. Match the recovery time to what the machine can tolerate.
  • 2Unmanaged switches are cheap and they hide from you. They cannot tell you a port is erroring, cannot prioritise time-critical traffic, cannot be segmented into VLANs, and cannot participate in a ring. On any network you will have to diagnose later, buy managed.
  • 3Line topology is why machine builders love EtherCAT. One cable in, one cable out, down a row of drives — no switch cabinet, no star wiring, minimal installation. The price is that a broken cable stops everything after it, which a ring closes for the cost of one more cable.
  • 4Wireless is a topology decision, not a convenience. It removes the cable failure mode and adds interference, coexistence and battery failure modes. Use it where cables genuinely cannot go — rotating tables, AGVs, moving cranes — and not to save installation cost.
CHECKPOINT

For any network you meet, answer two questions before anything else: what shape is it, and what happens when one cable is cut? If nobody on site can answer the second one, you have found the plant's real risk.

Once you see this pattern, every protocol datasheet in the library becomes readable. The names change; the structure does not.

This is why essentially every industrial protocol uses a CRC — Modbus RTU a CRC-16, Ethernet a 32-bit FCS, CAN a 15-bit CRC. You do not need to compute one by hand; you need to know three things about it.

  • 1A CRC detects; it does not correct. A frame that fails its CRC is discarded silently — not repaired, and usually not reported to your application. What happens next is the protocol's business: Modbus does nothing and the master times out; TCP retransmits; EtherCAT increments a counter and carries on; CAN retransmits automatically and eventually shuts a persistently faulty node off the bus.
  • 2The error counters are the only early warning you get. Because digital fails suddenly (page 2), a network at 0.001 % errors and one at 0 % look identical from the SCADA screen — until humidity, a new drive or a corroding connector pushes it over. Every managed switch, every PROFINET device and every serial gateway keeps CRC, alignment and discard counters. Read them monthly and trend them. A rising counter is a cable telling you it is about to fail, months in advance.
  • 3A message that passes CRC and still looks wrong is not a network fault. It is a scaling, sign, word-order or address fault — go back to page 3. This single distinction will save you days: CRC failures are cable problems, plausible-but-wrong values are configuration problems, and they are diagnosed in completely different places.

Who Talks When — the Rule That Decides Everything Else

ONE WIRE, MANY DEVICES, ONE PROBLEM CORE

If two devices transmit at once on a shared medium, both messages are destroyed. Every protocol must therefore answer one question — who may talk, and when? There are only five families of answer in industrial use, and knowing which family a protocol belongs to tells you almost everything about its behaviour before you read a word of its manual.

📈

The modern correction almost nobody is told. People still explain industrial Ethernet's problems in terms of collisions — but a switched, full-duplex Ethernet network has no collisions at all. Every device has its own private wire to a switch port. CSMA/CD has been irrelevant to real plants for twenty years. The remaining non-determinism is queuing: two frames arriving at a switch destined for the same output port, where one must wait behind the other. That is a completely different problem, and it is the one TSN exists to solve — by scheduling gates on the switch's output queues rather than by managing collisions. If you understand that distinction you are ahead of most of the field.

CHECKPOINT

For any protocol, ask which of the five families it belongs to. That one fact predicts its worst-case latency, whether losing one device stops everything, and whether it can do coordinated motion — before you have read anything else about it.

Time — the Only Thing That Makes This “Industrial”

FOUR WORDS THAT ARE NOT SYNONYMS CORE

Latency

How long one message takes, end to end. Measured in ms or µs.

Cycle time

How often the whole exchange repeats. The number a machine designer cares about.

Jitter

How much the latency varies between one cycle and the next. The industrial number.

Throughput

How much data per second. The number vendors advertise, and the least relevant of the four.

Determinism is not speed. It is a guarantee: "this message will arrive within X, every time, worst case" A 100 Mbit/s link that is usually 1 ms but occasionally 40 ms is useless for motion control. A 1 Mbit/s link that is always 2 ms ±10 µs is perfect for it. Industry buys the guarantee, not the bandwidth. Everything strange about industrial networking - scheduling, time synchronisation, summation frames, priority queues - exists to buy that guarantee.

TWO CONSEQUENCES PEOPLE GET WRONG CORE
  • 1Never log faster than the data updates. If the scan takes 27 s, logging at 5 s does not give you five times the resolution — it gives you the same value five times, and it makes a sparse measurement look dense and trustworthy. Set the log interval from the measured scan time, and if you need faster data, fix the scan, not the logger. As a working rule, sample at least five times faster than the fastest change you actually care about; below twice as fast, the trend you draw can be pure fiction.
  • 2Every industrial link needs a watchdog and a defined failure action. Ask of every device: what does it do when the network goes quiet? Hold last value, go to zero, go to a configured safe state, or trip? All four are correct answers in different applications and the wrong one is dangerous — a valve holding its last value forever because a switch died is a real accident mechanism. Write the answer down for every actuator, exactly as you would for loss of air (A12, page 12).
PREDICT FIRST
A machine needs 20 ms cycle time. Your Modbus TCP link measures 4 ms average, 6 ms median, and 45 ms at the 99.9th percentile. Is it good enough?
B — and the average is the least useful number on that list. A machine cycle is not an average; it is a deadline, and a deadline is either met or missed. At the 99.9th percentile the link takes 45 ms, which means that roughly once in every thousand cycles the machine does not get its data in time. At a 20 ms cycle that is once every twenty seconds — about 4,300 missed deadlines per day. What happens then depends entirely on the watchdog design: a held value, a jump, a fault trip, or a quiet corruption of a position. Always ask for the tail, never the mean. If a vendor quotes an average latency and cannot give you a 99.9th percentile or a worst case, they have not measured determinism — they have measured speed, and page 9 exists to tell you those are different things. C is worse than useless: more retries lengthens the tail. The real fixes are to remove the queuing (a dedicated switch, VLAN separation, or taking non-critical traffic off the segment) or to move to a protocol that schedules rather than queues.
CHECKPOINT

The sentence that separates industrial from IT networking: “fast is an average, deterministic is a worst case, and only one of them keeps a machine running.”

THE TRADE-OFF, IN ONE LINE cut in high (on top of TCP/IP) → routable, firewalled, easy, works across the internet, latency of milliseconds and no guarantee cut in low (at layer 2) → microseconds, deterministic, cannot be routed, cannot cross a normal switch, stays in the cell Everything else is detail. A last observation that saves a lot of confusion: an old serial fieldbus like Modbus RTU implements layers 1, 2 and 7 only, and skips 3–6 entirely. It has no concept of routing, sessions or encryption, because on a single 1200 m cable with one master, none of them is needed. That is not primitiveness — it is a design that matched its problem, which is why forty-seven-year-old Modbus is still the most widely implemented industrial protocol on earth.

WHAT IS GENUINELY NEW — AND IT IS NOT WHAT THE MARKETING SAYS CORE

Three things changed between 2025 and 2026 that will still matter in five years. Everything else in the trade press this year is a variation on them.

  • 1TSN finally has a published industrial profile. IEC/IEEE 60802:2026 is now issued — the joint IEC/IEEE profile that selects which of the two dozen TSN sub-standards an industrial network must implement, with what defaults, plus YANG models so a device can describe its own capabilities as a machine-readable data sheet. This was the missing piece: TSN's individual mechanisms have existed for years, but without a profile every vendor implemented a different subset and nothing interoperated. Expect conformance-tested multi-vendor TSN to become real over the next two to three years, not immediately. See A37.
  • 2Single-Pair Ethernet grew out of the process industry. Ethernet-APL — 10BASE-T1L carrying data and power on one twisted pair, for up to 1,000 m, in intrinsically safe hazardous areas — is in production use, developed jointly by FieldComm Group, ODVA, the OPC Foundation and PI. In December 2025 those bodies published a unified SPE specification extending 10BASE-T1L and Power-over-Data-Line to higher-powered devices in ordinary factory environments, not just hazardous process ones, and 100BASE-T1L is in development. The significance for a plant engineer: the four-wire-plus-power sensor cable, and the analogue 4–20 mA loop it often replaces, finally have a two-wire Ethernet successor that reaches the field device.
  • 3OPC UA is pushing down to the field level. OPC UA FX (Field eXchange) extends OPC UA from its traditional home above the PLC down to controller-to-controller and controller-to-device communication, running over TSN so that time-critical and ordinary traffic share one secure network. A multi-vendor prototyping interoperability event ran in February 2026, and release candidates of the UAFX 1.00 series added new specification parts. It is real engineering with real momentum — and it is still early. Treat it as something to watch and evaluate, not something to specify on a plant that must run next year.
📈

The honest Sri Lankan read. In the plants you will actually walk into, Modbus RTU is still the most common thing on the wire, PROFINET and EtherNet/IP dominate machinery imported from Europe and America respectively, EtherCAT arrives inside machine builders' equipment, and IO-Link is spreading fast because it needs no new network — it sits under whatever you already have. TSN, APL and OPC UA FX are genuinely important and almost entirely greenfield. Learn them so you can evaluate a proposal and are not sold something a site cannot support; do not expect to commission one this year. The engineer who can make a twenty-year-old Modbus network reliable is worth more, today, in this country, than one who can recite the TSN sub-standards.

FIVE THINGS THAT ARE NOT NEW, AND KEEP BEING SOLD AS NEW STRETCH
“Industrial Ethernet is faster”

Its bandwidth is larger. Its determinism depends entirely on which layer it cuts into (page 10). Modbus TCP on office switches can be less predictable than Modbus RTU on a cable.

“Wireless removes cost”

It removes cable and adds interference management, coexistence surveys, battery replacement and a security surface. It is a tool for places cable cannot go.

“Cloud connectivity”

Almost always means an edge gateway polling the same registers you already have and posting them upward. The hard part is still page 3 — knowing what the numbers mean.

“Protocol X is open”

Check what is open: the specification, the conformance testing, or the implementation? Several “open” industrial protocols require membership to obtain a device ID.

“Gigabit will fix our latency”

Latency on a lightly loaded industrial network is dominated by device turnaround and switch queuing, not by transmission time. Ten times the bandwidth typically buys a few per cent (page 1).

“It is a plug-and-play standard”

Every industrial protocol has vendor-specific profiles, optional features and device description files. Interoperability is a conformance-testing achievement, not a promise on a datasheet.

Choosing — Seven Questions, in This Order

ASK THESE BEFORE YOU OPEN A CATALOGUE CORE
  1. 1What is already on this site? Almost always the deciding question, and almost always asked last. A second protocol means a second set of spares, a second engineering tool, a second body of knowledge and a gateway that will be blamed for everything. Matching the plant standard beats a technically better choice unless the requirement genuinely cannot be met.
  2. 2What cycle time does the process need? Not what the vendor offers — what the physics requires (page 9). A tank level does not need 1 ms. Paying for determinism you cannot use is the most common over-specification in this field.
  3. 3How far, and through what? 30 m in a panel, 400 m across a shed, 3 km to a pump station and a hazardous area are four different problems with four different answers.
  4. 4How many devices, and will that number grow? Design for the plant in five years. Retrofitting a bus that is full is far more expensive than over-sizing one now.
  5. 5Who supports it in this country? A protocol with no local spares, no local training and no local integrator is a risk that lands on you personally at 2 a.m. This weighs more heavily in Sri Lanka than in Germany, and it is a legitimate engineering criterion, not a compromise.
  6. 6What happens when it fails? Topology (page 6) plus watchdog behaviour. Answer this before selection, because it may force a ring, and rings must be designed in, not added.
  7. 7Does the data need to leave the cell? If it must reach SCADA, MES or a cloud, you need an information layer (OPC UA or MQTT) as well as a control layer. Plan the two together, not as an afterthought — the afterthought version is a PC in a corner running an unsupported script, and every plant has one.

Security in One Page — Why OT Is Not IT

THE PRIORITY INVERSION CORE

IT security ranks its goals Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability. Operational technology ranks them in exactly the opposite order — and almost every conflict between an IT department and a plant comes from this one inversion.

IT OT 1. Confidentiality 1. AVAILABILITY the plant must run 2. Integrity 2. Integrity the data must be true 3. Availability 3. Confidentiality nobody will steal a conveyor's speed setpoint Consequences the IT department will find strange, and which are correct: • you cannot patch during production • you cannot reboot for updates • you cannot run an aggressive vulnerability scan on a live PLC network - some PLCs fall over when scanned • an account lockout policy that locks out an operator at 3 a.m. is a safety problem, not an inconvenience
THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH ABOUT THE PROTOCOLS CORE

Read this once and it will change how you design networks for the rest of your career. Modbus, PROFINET RT, EtherCAT, CAN, PROFIBUS and most other control protocols have no authentication of any kind. There is no password, no signature, no identity check. Any device physically connected to the wire can send any command to any node, and every node will obey.

A laptop plugged into a Modbus network can write to any register on any device, at any time. This is not a vulnerability in Modbus. It is the design, from 1979, for a private cable inside a locked plant. Therefore: security comes from the network's shape, not from the protocol. The formal name for this is zones and conduits (IEC 62443, see A54): group assets by required protection into zones, and allow traffic between zones only through defined, controlled conduits.

This is also why “just put the PLCs on the office network so IT can reach them” fails in four separate ways at once: broadcast traffic disturbs cycle times; a DHCP server changes a device's address after a power cut; a routine vulnerability scan can crash older controllers; and ransomware that lands on a laptop in accounts now has a route to a machine that moves a two-tonne press.

THE REALISTIC MINIMUM FOR A PLANT LIKE YOURS CORE

Not a compliance programme. Eight things a single competent engineer can do, in order of value per hour spent.

  • 1Know what is on the network. An asset inventory — device, address, firmware, function, who supports it. Nothing else on this list is possible without it, and almost no plant has one.
  • 2Separate control from office. At minimum a managed switch with VLANs; properly, a firewall between the two with only the specific traffic you need crossing it. This one step removes most of the realistic risk.
  • 3No direct internet exposure. Ever. No PLC, HMI or gateway with a public IP, no port forwarded “temporarily” for a vendor. Internet-exposed industrial controllers are found by automated scanning within hours.
  • 4Back up every PLC and HMI program, off the machine, and test the restore. The most common serious OT incident in a plant this size is not an attack — it is a failed controller and a program nobody can find.
  • 5Remote access through a VPN with multi-factor authentication, enabled only when needed. Vendor remote support is the single most common way something unwanted arrives.
  • 6Change default passwords and stop sharing one account. Trivial, universally ignored, and the first thing any assessment will find.
  • 7Control USB and the engineering laptop. The laptop that touches every machine is the most dangerous object in the building.
  • 8Enable and read the logs you already have. Managed switches, firewalls and modern PLCs log more than anyone looks at (page 7 — the same discipline as error counters).
📈

Security is where a mechanical engineer who understands the plant beats a security specialist who does not. The specialist knows the attacks; you know what the machine does when it loses communication — and that second question is what turns a vulnerability into a consequence, and a consequence into a business case. Learn enough IEC 62443 vocabulary to hold the conversation (A54) and let your process knowledge do the rest.
