Page 1 · Foundations

What a Robot Really Is — Sense, Plan, Act

Precise Definition

A robot is a programmable machine that senses its environment, plans a course of action, and acts on the physical world through actuators — closing a loop between perception and motion.

Micro 15 described the body — links and joints. The full discipline is two halves that must meet: mechanics (the rigid-body maths of where the tool is) and software (the framework that moves data from camera to motor in milliseconds). An industrial arm is useless without both. ROS 2 is the dominant open framework for the software half — and increasingly the language the two halves speak.

Sense

Encoders, force/torque, cameras, LiDAR, IMUs feed the robot's model of itself and the world. Links to A11 Sensors.

Plan

Kinematics, motion planning & collision checking turn a goal pose into a safe joint trajectory.

Act

Servo drives execute the trajectory under closed-loop control — the A09 servo & A10 PID loops, one per joint.

The robotics stack, top to bottom: Task "pick that part" ← mission / behaviour Planning pose → collision-free path ← MoveIt 2 / Nav2 Kinematics path → joint angles θ(t) ← DH, IK, Jacobian Control θ(t) → motor torque ← per-joint servo loops Hardware drives, gearboxes, encoders ← the physical arm
Where this sits in the plant

Robots are the flexible apex of automation: where a PLC sequences fixed machinery, a robot re-programs its motion for new parts. They feed directly into machine vision and end-effector design — the eyes and hands that make an arm useful.

Page 2 · The Body

Anatomy, Joints & Degrees of Freedom

A robot arm is a serial chain of rigid links connected by joints. Each joint adds one degree of freedom (DOF). To place a tool at an arbitrary position and orientation in 3-D space you need six DOF — three to position (x,y,z) and three to orient (roll, pitch, yaw).

A classic 6R arm: three "regional" joints place the wrist, three "wrist" joints orient the tool. The frame at the gripper is the Tool Centre Point (TCP).

ConfigurationJointsStrength
Articulated (6R)6 revoluteMost versatile — full 6-DOF pose; welding, assembly, handling
SCARA2R + 1P + rotateFast, stiff in Z; high-speed pick-and-place & assembly
Delta / parallel3 parallel armsExtremely fast, light payload; packaging, sorting
Cartesian / gantry3P (XYZ)Simple kinematics, big workspace; 3D printing, CNC, palletising
Cobot6–7R, torque-sensedSafe beside humans; flexible low-volume cells
Why 7 DOF? Redundancy

A 7-DOF arm (many cobots) has one more joint than the 6 needed for a pose. That redundancy lets it reach the same TCP pose with many elbow positions — so it can dodge obstacles, avoid singularities, and keep a comfortable posture. The cost: infinitely many IK solutions to choose between.

Page 3 · The Maths Begins

Frames, Transforms & DH Parameters

To compute where the tool is, attach a coordinate frame to every link and describe how each frame relates to the next. A homogeneous transform — a 4×4 matrix — packs rotation and translation into one object you can chain by multiplication.

Homogeneous transform (frame i relative to i−1): ⎡ R₃ₓ₃ p₃ₓ₁ ⎤ R = 3×3 rotation, p = position T = ⎢ ⎥ ⎣ 0 0 0 1 ⎦ chain frames: T₀ⁿ = T₁·T₂·…·Tₙ

The Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) convention is the standard shorthand: it describes each joint with just four numbers instead of a full matrix, so an n-joint arm is a clean table of 4n values.

Four DH parameters per joint: θ (joint angle), d (link offset), a (link length), α (link twist).

θ — joint angle

Rotation about Zᵢ₋₁

The variable for a revolute joint — this is what the motor changes. (For a prismatic joint, d is the variable instead.)

d, a, α — geometry

Fixed by the build

d = offset along the previous Z; a = length of the common normal; α = twist between the two Z axes. These come from the robot's CAD and never change.

Why DH still matters in the ROS 2 era

Modern tools read geometry from a URDF file, not a hand-built DH table — but DH is why the maths is tractable and is how textbooks, controllers and your own sanity-checks express a chain. Understand DH and the 4×4 transform, and every kinematics library stops being a black box.

Page 4 · The Easy Direction

Forward Kinematics — Joints → Pose

Forward kinematics (FK) answers: "given the joint angles, where is the tool?" It is the easy, always-solvable direction — just multiply the per-joint transforms together. There is exactly one answer.

FK: given θ = (θ₁ … θₙ) find T₀ᵗᵒᵒˡ T₀ᵗᵒᵒˡ(θ) = A₁(θ₁) · A₂(θ₂) · … · Aₙ(θₙ) where each Aᵢ is built from that joint's 4 DH parameters. The result is one 4×4 pose: position + orientation of the TCP. Unique, closed-form, fast.

Worked 2-link planar example (the intuition behind every arm):

2-link planar arm, lengths L₁,L₂, angles θ₁,θ₂: x = L₁·cos θ₁ + L₂·cos(θ₁+θ₂) y = L₁·sin θ₁ + L₂·sin(θ₁+θ₂) Example: L₁=L₂=0.5 m, θ₁=30°, θ₂=45° x = 0.5·cos30 + 0.5·cos75 = 0.433 + 0.129 = 0.562 m y = 0.5·sin30 + 0.5·sin75 = 0.250 + 0.483 = 0.733 m
FK is your ground truth

Because FK is unique and exact, it's the check on everything else: solve inverse kinematics for joint angles, then run FK to confirm those angles really land on the target pose. Every robot controller does this round-trip continuously to verify its own commands.

Page 5 · The Hard Direction

Inverse Kinematics — Pose → Joints

Inverse kinematics (IK) answers the question you actually care about: "I want the tool here — what joint angles get it there?" This is the hard direction: it can have many solutions, one, or none, and is generally non-linear.

Multiple solutions

"Elbow-up" and "elbow-down" both reach the same point. A 6R arm can have up to 8 distinct solutions — you must pick one.

No solution

Target outside the workspace, or blocked by a joint limit → IK fails. The arm simply can't get there.

Singular solution

At a singularity the maths degenerates — infinite joint speeds for finite tool speed.

Two-link IK (the elbow-up / elbow-down pair): cos θ₂ = (x² + y² − L₁² − L₂²) / (2·L₁·L₂) θ₂ = ± acos(…) ← the ± is the two elbow solutions θ₁ = atan2(y, x) − atan2(L₂·sin θ₂, L₁ + L₂·cos θ₂) Reachability test: a solution exists only if |L₁−L₂| ≤ √(x²+y²) ≤ L₁+L₂
ANALYTIC IK

Closed-form, instant

For arms with a spherical wrist (J4-5-6 axes meet at a point), IK decouples into position + orientation and solves in closed form — microseconds, exact. This is why nearly all industrial 6R arms are built that way.

NUMERIC IK

Iterative, general

For odd geometries or redundant (7-DOF) arms, solve iteratively with the Jacobian (e.g. damped least-squares). Flexible, but slower and can get stuck in local minima.

The configuration trap

Switching IK solution branch mid-path — "elbow-up" flipping to "elbow-down" — makes a robot lurch violently through a wild reconfiguration. Good planners constrain the solution to stay in one branch, or plan in joint space so the choice is continuous. This is one of the most common causes of a "the robot moved somewhere crazy" incident.

Page 6 · Velocity & Limits

The Jacobian & Singularities

Positions are static; robots move. The Jacobian J is the matrix that links joint velocities to tool velocities — the derivative of forward kinematics. It governs speed, force and the dreaded singularities.

Velocity: ẋ = J(θ) · θ̇ (tool twist = Jacobian × joint rates) Inverse: θ̇ = J⁻¹ · ẋ ← needed to make the TCP follow a path Force (duality): τ = Jᵀ · F (joint torques from a tool wrench)

A singularity is a pose where the Jacobian loses rank — det(J) → 0. There, the arm can't move the tool in some direction, and the inverse J⁻¹ blows up: tiny tool motion demands near-infinite joint speed.

Boundary singularity: at full stretch, the arm cannot move the TCP further out — J⁻¹ diverges.

Boundary

Arm fully extended or fully folded — at the edge of the workspace. Avoid by staying clear of the reach limit.

Wrist (interior)

Two wrist axes line up (e.g. J4 & J6 collinear) → a wrist axis "disappears." Common and nasty mid-workspace.

Manipulability

√det(J·Jᵀ) measures how far you are from a singularity — planners maximise it to keep motion smooth.

Why your robot "alarms out" near a straight line

Drive a TCP in a straight Cartesian line through a wrist singularity and the controller commands an axis to spin at thousands of °/s for an instant — it can't, so it faults to protect itself. Fixes: plan around the singularity, slow down through it, use a redundant 7-DOF arm, or use damped least-squares IK that trades a little path error for bounded joint speed.

Page 7 · The Software Nervous System

ROS 2 — Nodes, Topics, Services, Actions

ROS 2 (Robot Operating System 2) is not an OS — it is a middleware: a set of libraries and conventions for building a robot out of many small programs that talk to each other. It is the de-facto standard for research and, increasingly, industry.

A ROS 2 graph: independent nodes, loosely coupled by named channels, all riding on DDS.

PrimitivePatternUse it for
TopicPublish / subscribe, many-to-many, streamingSensor data, state, commands — e.g. /joint_states, /cmd_vel
ServiceRequest / response, synchronous, one-shotQuick queries & commands — "get map", "reset odometry"
ActionGoal + feedback + result, long-running, cancellable"Navigate to X", "execute trajectory" — with progress & abort
ParameterNamed config value per node, runtime-settableGains, limits, frame names — tune without recompiling

DDS & QoS

ROS 2 rides on DDS (Data Distribution Service) — peer-to-peer, no central master (the big change from ROS 1). Per-topic QoS picks reliability vs best-effort, history depth and deadlines — vital for real-time and lossy links.

Why ROS 2 over ROS 1

Real-time support, multi-robot by default, security (SROS 2), Windows/embedded support, and lifecycle-managed nodes. ROS 1 reached end-of-life; Jazzy Jalisco (LTS to 2029) and Humble are the current stable lines.

The mental model that unlocks ROS 2

Think of it as a postal system for data, not a program. You write small single-purpose nodes; the framework handles discovery and delivery by topic name. Swap the camera node for a simulated one and nothing else changes — that decoupling is the whole point, and why the same code runs in simulation and on real hardware.

Page 8 · Describing & Driving the Robot

URDF, TF2 & ros2_control

Three pieces connect the kinematics maths of pages 3–6 to running ROS 2 code: a URDF model of the robot, the TF2 transform tree that tracks every frame in real time, and ros2_control, the hardware-abstraction layer that talks to the drives.

URDF

An XML description of links, joints, limits, inertias and meshes. It is the DH table in modern form — the single source of truth for the robot's geometry.

TF2

A live tree of every coordinate frame (base → wrist → tool → camera). Ask "where is the gripper in the world frame?" and TF2 chains the transforms for you, time-stamped.

ros2_control

Standard interface between controllers (position/velocity/effort) and hardware drivers — so the same controller runs on a real arm or in sim.

# URDF — one joint connecting two links (the DH row, in XML) <joint name="shoulder" type="revolute"> <parent link="base_link"/> <child link="upper_arm"/> <origin xyz="0 0 0.30" rpy="0 0 0"/> <!-- d, a, α --> <axis xyz="0 0 1"/> <!-- rotates about Z --> <limit lower="-2.96" upper="2.96" effort="150" velocity="3.14"/> </joint>
# A minimal ROS 2 publisher node (rclpy) — the "hello robot" import rclpy from rclpy.node import Node from geometry_msgs.msg import Twist class Driver(Node): def __init__(self): super().__init__('driver') self.pub = self.create_publisher(Twist, '/cmd_vel', 10) self.create_timer(0.1, self.tick) # 10 Hz def tick(self): msg = Twist(); msg.linear.x = 0.2 # drive forward self.pub.publish(msg) rclpy.init(); rclpy.spin(Driver())
Real-time is a property of the whole chain

A robot control loop must hit its deadline every cycle — a late command is a wrong command. ROS 2 enables this (DDS QoS, real-time executors, lifecycle nodes), but you still need an RT kernel, lock-free code on the hot path, and the per-joint PID/servo loop running at 1–10 kHz below ROS 2, often on the drive itself.

Page 9 · The Application Stacks

Nav2, MoveIt 2 & Simulation

You rarely write planning from scratch. Two mature ROS 2 stacks do the heavy lifting: Nav2 for mobile robots that drive, and MoveIt 2 for arms that manipulate. Both are built from the nodes, actions and TF2 of pages 7–8.

NAV2 — mobile

Drive there without hitting things

Localisation (AMCL / SLAM), global & local path planning, costmaps, recovery behaviours and a behaviour-tree brain. You send a "NavigateToPose" action; Nav2 handles the rest — AMRs, delivery, inspection.

MOVEIT 2 — arms

Plan a collision-free reach

Wraps IK, motion planning (OMPL/Pilz), collision checking against a live world model, and trajectory execution. You give it a goal pose; it returns a safe joint trajectory — pick-and-place, assembly, welding.

Gazebo / Isaac Sim

Physics simulators run the exact same ROS 2 nodes against a virtual robot — test planning & control before touching hardware.

RViz 2

The 3-D visualiser: see TF2 frames, sensor clouds, planned paths and the robot model live — the robotics debugger.

Digital twin link

The sim model + live telemetry is a digital twin — connecting robotics straight to Industry 4.0 (A31).

Sim-to-real — the modern workflow

Develop and test entirely in Gazebo/Isaac, then deploy the identical node graph to the real arm by swapping only the hardware-interface node. This "sim-first" loop is how teams ship reliable robots fast — and how reinforcement-learning policies are trained safely before ever touching a real, expensive, breakable machine.

What actually goes wrong on the floor
⚠️ Robot lurches mid-path

IK branch flip (elbow-up ↔ down) or a wrist singularity. Plan in joint space or constrain the configuration; slow through near-singular poses.

⚠️ "No IK solution" / unreachable

Target outside workspace or past a joint limit. Check reach (|L₁−L₂|…L₁+L₂) and the URDF joint limits before blaming the planner.

⚠️ Nodes can't see each other

ROS 2 DDS discovery: mismatched ROS_DOMAIN_ID, firewall blocking multicast, or QoS incompatibility (reliable vs best-effort). Check with ros2 topic list/info.

⚠️ Jerky or laggy motion

Control loop missing deadlines — too much on the RT path, GC pauses, or a slow transform lookup. Profile the executor; push the fast loop onto the drive.

Real Industrial Example — full circle to Micro 15

A 6-axis arm tending a CNC cell (the very setup behind Micro 15) runs entirely on ROS 2: a vision node publishes the part pose on a topic; MoveIt 2 solves IK (picking elbow-up to dodge the chuck), checks collisions against a live world model and returns a trajectory; a FollowJointTrajectory action streams it to ros2_control, which drives six servo joints each closing a 4 kHz PID loop; TF2 keeps the gripper-to-machine transform exact; and the whole cell is validated in Gazebo first. Every page on this deep dive, working at once.

Quick Check

A 2-link planar arm has L₁ = L₂ = 0.4 m. (a) Can it reach a point 0.95 m from its base? Show why. (b) For a point it can reach, why are there usually two joint solutions, and what is the danger of switching between them during a move?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
(a) Max reach = L₁ + L₂ = 0.8 m. Since 0.95 m > 0.8 m, the point is outside the workspace — no IK solution exists. (Reachable band: |L₁−L₂| = 0 up to 0.8 m.) (b) The IK term θ₂ = ±acos(…) has two signs → "elbow-up" and "elbow-down" both land the tool on the target. The danger: if a path forces the controller to switch branch mid-move, the elbow flips through a large, fast reconfiguration — the robot lurches unpredictably and may hit something or fault out. Planners must stay within one configuration branch.
