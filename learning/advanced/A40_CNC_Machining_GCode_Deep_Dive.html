A Machine Tool With a Position Loop

CNC is not "a robot that cuts metal". It is servo motion control (A09) wrapped around 200 years of cutting physics.

Definition

Computer Numerical Control (CNC) is the automatic control of machine-tool axes by a stored part program — coded positions, feeds and machine functions — executed by a controller that closes a position loop on every axis, typically to a resolution of 1 µm or better.

The idea is older than the computer: in 1949 John T. Parsons, making helicopter rotor blades, contracted the MIT Servomechanisms Laboratory to drive a milling machine from punched-card coordinate data. The first NC machine ran in 1952; the APT programming language followed in 1958, and when minicomputers replaced hard-wired logic in the 1970s, NC became CNC. Today a FANUC, Siemens SINUMERIK or Heidenhain control does the same job: read a block of text, plan a coordinated multi-axis move, and command the servo drives every millisecond or faster.

What makes a machine tool different from any other motion system is the cutting process in the loop. The servo sees not just inertia but tool forces, vibration and heat — several kW concentrated at a cutting edge. Everything in this module is about managing that interaction: the program (pages 2–4), the physics (pages 5–7), the software chain (page 8) and the verification (page 9).

Whatever the geometry, the control principle is identical: the interpolator plans a path, each axis closes a position → velocity → current cascade (exactly the structure of A09), and feedback comes from motor encoders or glass scales. Semi-closed loop (encoder on the motor) trusts the ballscrew; full-closed loop (linear scale on the axis) measures the table itself and removes screw pitch error and thermal growth from the equation.

Coordinates, Offsets & the Chain of Zeros

Every crash and every scrapped part traces back to one question: where does the control THINK the tool tip is?

Axis convention — ISO 841

The Z axis is always the spindle axis, positive direction away from the workpiece. X is the longest travel perpendicular to Z, and Y completes a right-handed set. Rotary axes A, B, C rotate about X, Y, Z respectively.

Program motion is described as tool motion relative to the part, even when physically the table moves — the control inverts signs internally. That convention is what lets the same G-code logic run a VMC, an HMC and a gantry mill. G90 makes coordinates absolute (positions in the active work system); G91 makes them incremental (distances from the current point). G21 selects millimetres, G20 inches — a wrong units line multiplies every move by 25.4, which is why the safety block on page 3 always states it explicitly.

From machine home to tool tip — the offset stack
tool-tip position = machine zero + work offset (G54) + programmed X·Y·Z + tool length H (G43) wrong sign on H → tool tries to reach Z0 through the table — the classic crash
Five-axis kinematics & TCP

Table–table

Trunnion A + rotary C under the part. Stiff, compact; part size limited. Most common on VMC-derived 5-axis.

Head–head

Both rotations on the spindle head. Huge parts stay still (gantries, aerospace skins); head is heavy and less stiff.

Head–table

B on the head, C in the table — the lathe-mill hybrid layout. Flexible for mill-turn work.

TCP — Tool Centre Point control (FANUC G43.4, Siemens TRAORI) is what makes simultaneous 5-axis usable: when a rotary axis moves, the control automatically translates the linear axes so the tool tip stays put on the programmed path. Without TCP the post-processor must bake the machine's exact pivot distances into every program — with it, the same program runs after a tool change of different length. The work datum itself is found by probing, and the logic of choosing datum features is exactly the 3-2-1 / datum-reference-frame discipline of A21.

The Grammar of G-Code

A 60-year-old language (ISO 6983) that still runs almost every machine tool on Earth — because it is small, modal and merciless.

Blocks, words & modality

A program is a list of blocks (lines). Each block holds words — a letter address plus a value: G01 X50. Y-12.5 F800. Most G-codes are modal: they stay active until another code from the same group replaces them.

Modality is the language's power and its danger. After one G01 F200, every following coordinate line cuts at 200 mm/min — no need to repeat it. But it also means a block inherits everything you didn't say: the active work offset, plane, units, feed mode. That is why professional programs begin with a safety block that forces every modal group to a known state, and why restarting a program mid-way requires re-establishing modality first.

Cutter compensation — why D offsets exist

You program the part contour, not the tool-centre path. G41/G42 shifts the path by the radius stored in the D register, so the same program cuts correctly with a nominally 10 mm end mill that actually measures 9.96 mm after regrind — the operator edits the offset, not the code. The pair geometry + wear offsets lets you dial a wall to tolerance in microns: measure the part, put the error in wear, re-run. On lathes the equivalent battle is tool-nose radius comp, without which every taper and radius is cut wrong by up to the nose radius.

Dialects: FANUC-style ISO code dominates; Siemens SINUMERIK adds high-level cycles (CYCLE81) and real variables; Heidenhain offers conversational programming. Macro B (FANUC) gives variables #100, arithmetic and logic — enough to write parametric families of parts and probing routines. ISO 14649 (STEP-NC) — feature-based, tolerance-aware programs — remains the perpetual "next step", tying into the digital-thread vision of A33.

Interpolation — Turning Text Into Coordinated Motion

The interpolator is the metronome: every millisecond it hands each servo axis its next micro-target.

Linear & circular interpolation

In G01, all axes start and finish together: each axis velocity is scaled so the vector feed along the path equals F. In G02/G03 the control solves the arc in the active plane from the centre offsets I, J (or radius R).

path length L = √(Δx² + Δy² + Δz²) axis feeds Vx = F·Δx/L Vy = F·Δy/L Vz = F·Δz/L arc centre (xc, yc) = (x₀+I, y₀+J) I, J measured FROM start point

CAM systems approximate free-form surfaces with thousands of tiny line segments. The geometric price is chord error — the sag between the true curve and its chord — and the computational price is block-processing load: every segment is a block the control must read, plan and execute.

chord error e ≈ l² / 8R (segment l on radius R) block rate = feed / (60 · l) blocks per second if block rate > control capacity → data starving: axes pause each block, feed collapses, finish shows facets

The eternal CAM trade-off: shorter segments give better geometry but can outrun the control. High-end controls fit smooth splines through the points (nano-smoothing) to escape the dilemma.

Look-ahead, jerk & following error

Look-ahead lets the control read tens to thousands of blocks ahead and plan velocity like a rally driver reading pace notes: brake before the corner. Without it, a sharp direction change at full feed would demand infinite acceleration — the control would either overshoot (gouging the corner) or halt at every vertex. Modern planners use jerk-limited S-curve profiles (the same profiles as A09, page 6) so acceleration itself ramps smoothly — that is what keeps a mould surface free of vibration marks.

Each servo axis still tracks with a small lag. For a classic proportional position loop the following error is e = V / Kv — at 10 m/min with Kv=16.7 s⁻¹ that is a full 10 µm. Matched Kv across axes keeps the path true even while each axis lags; feed-forward (A10's same trick) cancels most of the rest. Mismatched axis tuning shows up instantly as ovality on a ballbar plot (page 9).

Speeds & Feeds — The Actual Physics of Cutting

Four numbers rule every cut: surface speed, chip load, radial and axial engagement. Everything else is derived.

The core equations
cutting speed Vc = π·D·N / 1000 [m/min] → N = 1000·Vc / (π·D) table feed Vf = N · z · fz [mm/min] (z teeth, fz chip per tooth) removal rate MRR = ae · ap · Vf / 1000 [cm³/min] (ae radial, ap axial, mm) cutting power P ≈ MRR · kc / 60 000 [kW] (kc = specific cutting force, N/mm²)

Vc is a property of the tool–material pair (carbide on aluminium runs ~10× faster than on titanium); fz is a property of the edge — too low and the edge rubs instead of cutting, work-hardening the surface and burning the corner; too high and it chips. The classic beginner error is slowing everything down when chatter appears: halving fz below the edge radius makes rubbing worse. Diagnose first (page 7).

Chip thinning: when radial engagement ae < 50% of diameter, the average chip is thinner than fz. Compensate: fz,eff = fz · D / (2·√(ae(D−ae))) — the basis of high-efficiency milling on page 8, where tiny ae permits shockingly high feeds.

Climb vs conventional

Climb milling pulls the table INTO the cut — safe only because ballscrews are preloaded backlash-free. On a worn Bridgeport with 0.2 mm backlash, climb cutting snatches the table and breaks tools.

Tooling — Carbide, Coatings & the Taylor Curve

The cutting edge is a consumable running at 800 °C. Managing its death is a core production skill.

Materials & coatings

Cemented carbide — tungsten-carbide grains in a cobalt binder — owns modern machining: three times stiffer than steel, hot-hard to ~900 °C. Coatings add a low-friction, heat-blocking skin a few microns thick.

Insert geometry is a second dial: a large nose radius gives a stronger corner and better finish but more radial force (chatter risk on thin parts); positive rake cuts free but weakens the edge. The ISO insert code (e.g. CNMG 120408) encodes shape, clearance, tolerance and chipbreaker — reading it fluently is a shop superpower.

Tool life — Taylor's equation (1907)
Vc · Tn = C (T = life in min at speed Vc) n ≈ 0.10–0.15 HSS · 0.2–0.3 carbide · 0.3–0.6 ceramic carbide, n = 0.25: +20% speed → life × (1.2)−4 ≈ 0.48 — half the life +100% speed → life × 2⁻⁴ = 1/16

Speed is exponential, feed is roughly linear: if cycle time must drop, raise feed and depth before speed — the same MRR for far less edge temperature. Tool life economics then set the optimum: a $30 insert edge that saves 2 minutes per part on a $120/hr machine pays for itself after 8 parts.

The classic wear curve: fast break-in, long linear plateau, then runaway. Sister-tool counters in the control swap tools on schedule during lights-out runs.

How edges die & how they're held

Flank wear

Normal abrasion — the "good" death. Track VB; compensate with wear offsets.

Crater wear

Diffusion at high temperature hollows the rake face — reduce Vc or up the coating.

Built-up edge

Cold, sticky materials weld to the edge, then tear chunks away. Cure: MORE speed, sharper edge, better lube.

Chipping

Interrupted cuts, vibration, too-brittle grade. Use tougher grade, honed edge, stable setup.

Notch wear

At the depth-of-cut line, from work-hardened skin (stainless, Inconel). Vary ap between passes.

Thermal cracks

Comb cracks from temperature cycling — often caused by intermittent coolant. Go full flood or fully dry.

Toolholding sets the ceiling: total indicated runout at the flute directly steals chip load from one tooth and doubles it on another. Rule of thumb — every 10 µm of runout costs roughly half the tool life in small end mills. The ladder: ER collet (5–10 µm) → milling chuck (~4 µm) → hydraulic (~3 µm) → shrink-fit (≤3 µm, best rigidity). Balance grade matters above ~8000 rpm (G2.5 at speed), and BT/CAT taper vs HSK face-contact holders is the stiffness upgrade path for high-speed spindles.

Workholding, Forces & the Chatter Problem

The machine is a spring. The cut excites it. Whether the vibration grows or dies is pure — and beautiful — feedback theory.

Hold the part like a datum system

Workholding is applied 3-2-1 location (A21): three points define a plane, two a line, one kills the last freedom — clamps then apply force against the locators, never as locators themselves. A vise's solid jaw is a datum; the movable jaw is just force. Practical rules: cut toward the solid jaw, support thin floors (pocketing pressure bows them), use soft jaws bored in place for round or finished surfaces, and remember that a 2 kN cutting force at 150 mm stickout is a 300 N·m moment trying to peel your part out of the vise. Zero-point pallet systems repeat location to <5 µm and turn setup time into seconds — the fixture equivalent of a tool changer.

tangential force Fc ≈ kc · ap · h (h = instantaneous chip thickness) spindle torque T = Fc · D/2 · 10⁻³ [N·m] check vs motor curve at LOW rpm
Regenerative chatter — the machine remembers

Each tooth cuts a wavy surface; the next tooth re-cuts that wave. If the phase is wrong, chip thickness oscillates harder each pass — a self-excited vibration that grows until the tool screams, independent of how "smooth" the machine is.

tooth-passing frequency ft = N · z / 60 [Hz] best speeds: ft ≈ fn / k (k = 1, 2, 3…) → N = 60·fn / (z·k) stiffness of a cantilever k ∝ 3EI/L³ → shorten stickout 20% ⇒ ~2× stiffer

The stability lobe diagram converts this into shop moves. Find the dominant natural frequency (tap test with an instrumented hammer, or just listen to the chatter tone and use a phone FFT app), then speed up or down to a lobe peak — chatter often disappears by going faster, which is deeply counter-intuitive. The fix ladder, in order of leverage: 1) shorten stickout (L³ law — the biggest lever in the room), 2) move rpm to a stable lobe, 3) reduce radial engagement, 4) variable-helix / variable-pitch tools that break the regeneration phase, 5) tuned-mass damped holders for long reaches. Increasing feed slightly can also help — a thicker chip adds process damping.

CAM — From Solid Model to Spindle

Nobody types 200 000 blocks by hand. The CAM chain is where geometry, strategy and machine dialect meet.

The digital chain
Roughing has been reinvented

Conventional pocketing offsets the boundary inward at ~50% stepover: engagement angle swings wildly in corners, forcing feeds set for the worst case. Adaptive / trochoidal (HSM) roughing holds a constant engagement angle with light radial cuts (5–15% of D) at full flute depth and very high feed (chip-thinning compensated). Result: heat leaves in the chip, the whole flute length wears evenly instead of one corner, and MRR typically doubles to quadruples in tough alloys while tool life goes up. It is the single highest-ROI CAM feature of the last two decades.

Finishing is a geometry game: contour (Z-level) for steep walls, raster/parallel for shallow, scallop-constant paths for uniform texture, pencil and rest machining to chase corners a big tool couldn't reach. For ball-nose surfacing the scallop height ties stepover to finish:

scallop height h ≈ s² / (8·r) (s = stepover, r = ball radius) turned/milled theoretical peak Rt ≈ f² / (8·rε) → finish improves with the SQUARE of feed reduction

A machine that "reads microns" doesn't necessarily CUT microns. Error has a budget — audit it.

Accuracy vs repeatability vs resolution: resolution is what the control can command (0.1–1 µm); repeatability is how well the axis returns to the same place (the servo + mechanics); accuracy is how close that place is to the truth (geometry + thermal + calibration). A machine can repeat to 2 µm and still be 40 µm wrong — which is fine, because repeatable error can be compensated away. ISO 230 defines the test methods; pitch-error tables and volumetric compensation live in the control.

The diagnostic trio

Ballbar (QC20)

Telescoping sensor traces a programmed circle. The polar plot is a fingerprint: peanut at 45° = squareness; steps at axis reversals = backlash; oval = servo mismatch.

Laser interferometer

Measures true position along each axis to sub-µm → builds the pitch-error compensation table. Repeat after crashes and seasonally.

Touch probe

Spindle probe sets work offsets in seconds, measures features mid-cycle and closes the loop: measure → update wear offset → recut. Tool setter catches length error & breakage.

In-process gauging is a control loop (the same idea as A10, just slower): probe the finished bore, feed the deviation into the wear offset, and the next part lands centred in tolerance. Combined with SPC (A27) on a CMM, this is how a 24/7 cell holds ±10 µm without a human touching an offset. The datum logic for what to probe — and what counts as the part's reference frame — is A21's territory: probe the datums, not convenient surfaces.

thermal reality check: ballscrew ΔL = α·L·ΔT = 11.7 µm/m·°C 1 m screw warming 5 °C → 58 µm of position error — a whole tolerance band

Recap, Failure Modes & Self-Test

Eight ideas that make you dangerous around a machine tool — in the good way.

The whole module in 8 lines
  • 1CNC = servo position loops (A09) executing a text program — semi-closed trusts the screw, full-closed measures the table.
  • 2Tool tip position = machine zero + G54 work offset + programmed point + G43 length. One wrong term = crash or scrap.
  • 3G-code is modal: a block inherits everything you didn't say. Safety blocks force known state; comp needs a lead-in ≥ radius.
  • 4Interpolation trades chord error (l²/8R) against block rate — data starving turns fine feeds into faceted crawls.
  • 5Vc belongs to the material, fz to the edge. Never fix chatter by rubbing (fz below edge radius work-hardens).
  • 6Taylor: speed kills exponentially (V·Tⁿ=C) — for cycle time, raise feed & depth first. Replace edges in steady wear, on schedule.
  • 7Chatter is regenerative feedback — cure with stickout (1/L³), lobe-tuned rpm (often FASTER), engagement, variable helix.
  • 8Accuracy is a budget: geometry + thermal + servo + deflection. Probe datums, close the loop with wear offsets and SPC (A27).
Six classic failure modes
💥 The offset crash

Wrong H number, stale G54 from last week's setup, or G43 forgotten entirely — the tool drives through the part into the vise at rapid. Cure: safety blocks, distance-to-go check on first Z approach, single-block + rapid override 25% on every new program.

🌀 Chatter scrapping the finish

Long stickout + resonant rpm: walls show fish-scale marks, tool corners chip. Cure: shortest tool that reaches, tap-test or FFT the tone, move rpm to a stable lobe, drop radial engagement — slowing down blindly often makes it worse.

🔥 Recutting chips → dead tool

Chips packed in a pocket get re-cut, temperature spikes, carbide fails in seconds. Cure: air blast/through-tool coolant, trochoidal paths that fling chips out, peck retracts that actually clear, HSM strategies with room to evacuate.

🌡️ Thermal drift scraps the afternoon

Morning-warm machine cuts different from lunch-hot machine; a 5 °C screw drift is ~60 µm/m. Cure: warm-up program, thermal comp on, tolerance-critical ops after thermal soak, full-closed scales for the fussy axes.

👻 Backlash ghost steps

Quadrant marks on bores, position depends on approach direction. Cure: measure with ballbar, apply backlash comp, approach final position from one consistent direction in critical moves, preloaded/replaced ballscrew nut when worn.

📉 Data-starved "high speed" finishing

0.02 mm segments at 5000 mm/min = 4200 blocks/s into a 1 ms control: feed collapses to a stutter, finish shows facets. Cure: sane CAM tolerance (~1/4 of part tolerance), smoothing/spline modes on, shorter look-ahead-hungry moves avoided.

Quick check 1

A program runs G43 H7 but tool 7's length was measured with the OLD master gauge, 12 mm shorter. What happens at the first Z-2. cut, and which two habits catch this before contact?

Tap to revealThe control believes the tool is 12 mm shorter than reality, so the tip arrives 12 mm DEEPER than programmed — a crash into part or fixture. Catches: (1) first approach in single-block with rapid override low, watching DISTANCE-TO-GO equal the expected gap at the safe-Z stop; (2) a tool setter / probe re-measuring every tool in-machine so offsets can't inherit a stale reference.
Quick check 2

Carbide end mill, D = 10 mm, z = 3, in 6061 at Vc = 300 m/min and fz = 0.05 mm. Compute rpm and feed.

Tap to revealN = 1000·300/(π·10) ≈ 9 549 rpm. Vf = N·z·fz = 9549 × 3 × 0.05 ≈ 1 432 mm/min. (If the spindle tops out at 8 000 rpm, keep fz and accept Vc ≈ 251 m/min — feed drops to ~1 200 mm/min.)
Quick check 3

Why does climb milling need a backlash-free drive, and what happens on a worn-screw manual mill?

Tap to revealIn climb milling the cutting force acts in the SAME direction as table feed — the cut pulls the table. With backlash, the table gets snatched forward through the free play, chip load spikes, and the tool grabs or breaks. Preloaded ballscrews (CNC) have essentially zero backlash, so climb is standard; on a worn Acme-screw manual mill you cut conventional or fit a backlash eliminator.
Quick check 4

Chatter appears at 8 000 rpm with a 4-flute cutter, tone ≈ 1 070 Hz. Give the reasoning for a better spindle speed.

Tap to revealTreat 1 070 Hz as the dominant natural frequency. Stable pockets sit where tooth-passing frequency = fn/k: N = 60·fn/(z·k) = 60×1070/(4k) ≈ 16 050/k rpm → k=1: 16 050 (too fast), k=2: 8 025… already there and chattering means fn estimate is off or that lobe is shallow — try k=1.5-ish invalid, so test ~9 600 or ~6 900 (between lobes moves AWAY from resonance ratio). Practically: sweep rpm ±15% while listening, shorten stickout (1/L³), then drop ae. The point: chatter is fixed by CHANGING the phase relationship, not by universally slowing down.
Quick check 5

A ballbar plot shows a peanut shape tilted 45° and small steps where each axis reverses. Diagnose both.

Tap to revealPeanut at 45° = X–Y squareness error (the axes aren't perpendicular; the circle stretches along one diagonal, shrinks along the other). Steps at reversals = backlash / lost motion at quadrant changes. Fix order: mechanical squareness/comp first, then backlash compensation — and re-run the ballbar to confirm each fix separately.
Quick check 6

Carbide tooling with Taylor exponent n = 0.25: production wants 20% more spindle speed. What happens to tool life, and what would you propose instead?

Tap to revealLife ratio = (1.2)^(−1/0.25) = 1.2⁻⁴ ≈ 0.48 → tool life roughly halves for a 20% cycle-time gain on the cutting portion only. Counter-proposal: raise feed and axial depth (roughly linear in life), use adaptive roughing to raise MRR at constant edge temperature, or accept the speed increase only on the roughing tools where edges are cheap and cycle time dominates.
