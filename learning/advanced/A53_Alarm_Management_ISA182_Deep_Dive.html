The Alarm Problem

How "free" alarms broke the control room — and why a standard had to fix a human problem.

From Annunciator Panels to Alarm Floods

In the hard-wired era, every alarm cost money: a lamp box, a relay, an engineer's justification. A plant might have 50–200 alarms, each one debated. Then the DCS and SCADA arrived and an alarm became a checkbox — zero hardware cost, configured in seconds. Tag counts exploded into the tens of thousands, and every tag sprouted HI, HIHI, LO, LOLO, deviation and rate alarms "just in case".

The result: control rooms where operators face thousands of alarms a day, most of which require no action at all. The alarm system — supposed to be the operator's most reliable early-warning layer — became background noise. And when the plant actually gets into trouble, the noise becomes a flood that buries the one alarm that matters.

The Operator Bandwidth Wall — Load vs. Human Capacity

A human can meaningfully assess roughly one alarm per minute. During an upset, a badly managed system generates ten to a hundred times that. Above the green line, the alarm system has stopped doing its job — exactly when it is needed most.

⛽ Texaco Milford Haven, 1994

Refinery explosion during a storm-induced upset. In the last 11 minutes before the blast, the two operators received 275 alarms — one every 2–3 seconds. The critical failed-valve indication was buried. The official HSE report became a founding text of alarm management, and fed directly into EEMUA 191.

☢️ Three Mile Island, 1979

Over 100 alarms annunciated in the first minutes; the alarm printer ran hours behind events. Operators, misled by a stuck-open relief valve indication, cut emergency cooling. The lesson: an alarm system that shows everything communicates nothing.

Quick Check

Why did alarm counts explode when plants moved from hard-wired annunciators to DCS/SCADA?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The marginal cost of an alarm collapsed to zero. A hard-wired alarm needed a lamp, a relay and a justification; a DCS alarm is a checkbox. With no cost gate, every tag acquired multiple alarms "just in case" — and no one engineered the total operator load.

What Is (and Isn't) an Alarm

The single most important test in the whole discipline: does the operator have to do something?

The ISA-18.2 Definition
Alarm = an audible and/or visible means of indicating an equipment malfunction,
process deviation, or abnormal condition requiring a timely operator response.

Every word is load-bearing, but the last clause is the knife: if there is no defined operator action, it is not an alarm. It might be an event to log, a status to display, an alert for an engineer's dashboard — but it has no right to interrupt the operator. This one test, applied honestly during rationalization, typically deletes or demotes half of a legacy alarm database.

The Four Things Every Real Alarm Must Have
  • 1An abnormal condition — something is genuinely outside the normal operating envelope, not merely "different".
  • 2A consequence — if nobody acts, something concrete happens: trip, damage, off-spec product, emission, injury.
  • 3A defined operator response — a specific corrective action the operator can actually take from the control room (or dispatch).
  • 4Time to respond — enough time between annunciation and consequence for the response to work. No time → it should be an interlock/SIS, not an alarm.

🛡️ The alarm as a protection layer

In LOPA (→ A52), a well-engineered alarm + trained operator response earns a risk-reduction credit of 0.1 — one order of magnitude. That credit is only honest if the alarm is rationalized, prioritized, and the operator isn't drowning in noise. Alarm management is what makes the LOPA credit true.

⚖️ Classes vs. priorities

ISA-18.2 also defines alarm classes — groups with common administrative requirements (e.g. "safety-related", "environmental", "highly managed" alarms needing stricter MOC and testing). Class ≠ priority: class says how the alarm is managed; priority says how urgently the operator responds.

Quick Check

A "pump running" indication turns red when the pump stops on a normal, operator-commanded shutdown. Should it alarm?

Tap to reveal ↓A: No. The condition is not abnormal (it was commanded), there is no consequence and no corrective action. It is status information. Alarming expected state changes is one of the biggest sources of alarm-system noise — this is what state-based alarming (Page 8) exists to fix.

The Alarm Management Lifecycle

ISA-18.2's core contribution: alarms are not configured once — they are managed forever.

The Ten-Stage Lifecycle (ISA-18.2 Clause 6)

Philosophy governs everything; identification → rationalization → design → implementation put alarms in service; operation and maintenance run them; monitoring feeds bad actors back into rationalization; MOC guards every change; audit checks the whole machine. It is deliberately shaped like a quality loop, not a project.

Why does the lifecycle place Monitoring & Assessment in the loop rather than at the end?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Because alarm systems degrade continuously — instruments drift, processes change, operators shelve. Monitoring feeds bad actors back into rationalization every week, making the lifecycle a control loop around the alarm system itself. A one-time cleanup without monitoring returns to chaos within a year or two.

The Alarm Philosophy Document

One rulebook for the whole site — written before you touch a single setpoint.

What the Philosophy Fixes in Writing

The philosophy is the constitution of the alarm system: a site-level document that defines terms, sets the priority scheme, fixes KPI targets and assigns roles — so that a thousand later decisions (is this an alarm? what priority? who may shelve it?) are made consistently instead of per-engineer. ISA-18.2 makes it mandatory; audits check reality against it.

📋 Master Alarm Database

The philosophy's operational twin: one row per alarm holding its cause, consequence, operator action, time to respond, priority, class, setpoint basis, deadband and delay. It is the single source of truth — the DCS/SCADA configuration is audited against it, never the other way round.

🏭 Scaled to any plant

A 20-page philosophy and a spreadsheet master database work for a Sri Lankan factory with one SCADA server just as the full apparatus works for a refinery. The discipline — not the tooling — is the standard. Start with the priority matrix, the deadband table and the shelving rule.

Quick Check

Why must the philosophy be written before rationalization starts, not after?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Rationalization is thousands of micro-decisions (keep/kill, priority, setpoint). Without pre-agreed rules, each meeting invents its own — priorities become inconsistent, and the resulting database can't be audited because there's no reference to audit against. Philosophy first turns opinion into procedure.

Rationalization & Priority

Every alarm defends its existence — then gets a priority from consequence and time, not from volume.

The D&R Process (Documentation & Rationalization)

A small team — operator, process engineer, control engineer, facilitator — walks the alarm list tag by tag. For each candidate they record: cause (what went wrong), consequence (what happens if nobody acts), corrective action (what the operator does), time to respond (how long they have), and the basis for the setpoint. Fails the operator-action test → deleted or demoted to event. Survives → gets a priority from the matrix below. Typical throughput: 10–20 alarms/hour; typical outcome: 30–60% of the legacy database removed or demoted.

Priority = Severity of Consequence × Time to Respond

The matrix comes from the philosophy; the D&R meeting only supplies severity and time per alarm. Note what's absent: "how important the tag feels". Urgency is derived, never asserted.

🎯 Target distribution ≈ 80 / 15 / 5

A healthy rationalized system lands near 80% Low, 15% Medium, 5% High (EEMUA 191 guidance). If 40% of your alarms are High, priority no longer ranks anything — the operator is back to guessing. Priority inflation is the alarm system's grade inflation.

⏱️ No time to respond?

If the consequence arrives faster than any credible operator response (< ~1–3 min including diagnosis), an alarm is the wrong tool — the protection belongs in an interlock or SIS (→ A52). Alarms buy human judgment; when there's no time for judgment, automate.

Quick Check

During D&R, a "HIHI level" alarm on a tank turns out to have the same setpoint as the SIS trip. What's wrong?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Time to respond is zero — by the time it annunciates, the trip is already acting, so the "alarm" can't change the outcome. It should either be moved earlier (below the trip point with margin for the operator to act — Page 6) or reclassified as a trip notification event, not an alarm.

Setpoints, Deadbands & Delays

Detailed design: where the alarm sits in the envelope, and how it's stopped from stuttering.

The Operating Envelope — Room to Respond
SPalarm ≤ SPtrip − ṙmax · (Tdiagnose + Tact + Tprocess lag) − margin
…and SPalarm must still sit OUTSIDE the normal band, or it becomes a standing nuisance.

Two constraints squeeze every alarm setpoint: far enough below the trip to be actionable, far enough above normal to be quiet. If the two constraints can't both be met, the process — not the alarm — needs redesign.

Deadband & Delay Defaults (per ISA-18.2 guidance)
Signal typeDeadband (% of span)Typical on-delayWhy
Flow5%15 sNoisiest signal class — turbulence, pulsation
Level5%60 sSloshing, waves; large vessels move slowly anyway
Pressure2%15 sModerate noise; faster consequence
Temperature1%60 sQuiet, slow signal — thermal mass filters for you (→ A50)
Discrete (switch)n/a1–5 s de-bounceContact bounce & vibration (→ A11)
  • 1Deadband = hysteresis on the return journey: alarm sets at SP, clears only at SP−deadband (high alarm). Kills chattering from noise riding on the signal.
  • 2On-delay = condition must persist N seconds before annunciating — kills fleeting alarms. Off-delay = must stay clear N seconds before clearing — kills rapid re-annunciation.
  • 3Both consume response time: a 60 s on-delay on a fast excursion is 60 s the operator never gets back. Check delay ≪ time-to-respond (Page 15, Derivation 2).
Quick Check

A high-level alarm at 90% clears at 89.99% and re-alarms at 90.01% as waves slosh. Which tool fixes this, deadband or delay — and how?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Primarily deadband: set clearing at 85% (5% of span) so the wave amplitude can no longer straddle the set/clear pair. A 60 s off-delay helps too, but deadband attacks the root cause — the set and clear thresholds being closer together than the noise amplitude.

Quick Check

What's the difference between shelving and out-of-service, and why does the distinction matter?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Shelving is an operator's short-term, auto-expiring mute for a nuisance alarm during operation; out-of-service is a maintenance state under authorization for a broken/under-repair alarm, lasting until formal return-to-service. Merging them lets "temporarily annoying" quietly become "permanently disabled" — the shelved alarm auto-returns, the OOS alarm needs a signature.

State-Based & Advanced Alarming

The plant has modes — the alarm system must know which one it's in.

Why Static Alarms Fail on a Dynamic Plant

An alarm set for steady-state running is wrong in every other mode: during startup, "low flow" is normal for an hour; during shutdown, dozens of "equipment stopped" alarms announce exactly what the operator commanded; with a pump out for service, its alarms stand stale for a week. The cure is state-based alarming: alarm attributes (enabled/disabled, setpoint, priority) switch with the declared plant state — engineered, documented, and automatic.

Plant stateExample attribute change
StartupLow-flow & low-temp alarms disabled until "running" declared; tighter rate-of-change alarms enabled
Normal runFull rationalized alarm set active
Planned shutdown"Stopped/no-flow" family suppressed by design; safety alarms remain
Equipment out of serviceThat train's alarms suppressed; standby train's alarms promoted
Product / recipe changeQuality-related setpoints move with the recipe (batch: ISA TR18.2.6)

🥇 First-out & eclipsing

First-out: in a trip cascade, capture and highlight the alarm that initiated it; suppress or group the consequential followers. Eclipsing: when HI and HIHI both stand, annunciate only the more severe — the lesser is implied. Both cut flood volume without hiding information.

⚠️ Flood suppression, honestly

Designed suppression must be engineered per cause (documented condition → documented suppressed set), not a blanket "hide everything when trips are active". The operator must always be able to open the suppressed list — suppression hides annunciation, never data.

Quick Check

A compressor trip floods the board with 40 downstream alarms. Which advanced technique applies, and what must exist before it's allowed?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Designed (consequential/state-based) suppression: on "compressor tripped", suppress the documented set of downstream consequence alarms and present one first-out cause alarm. Prerequisite: the suppression logic and alarm set are engineered and recorded during rationalization, and the suppressed list stays viewable — it's a designed state, not an operator improvisation.

The Improvement Ladder (EEMUA benchmark levels)
  • 1Overloaded — >1000/day: alarms ignored wholesale; start with bad actors & deadbands, not philosophy debates.
  • 2Reactive — several hundred/day: the board is workable between upsets, useless during them; rationalization needed.

Quick Check

A plant averages a comfortable 140 alarms/day, but 6% of its hours are flood hours. Healthy or not?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Not healthy. Averages hide bursts: this system is quiet in steady state but collapses precisely during upsets — when it matters most. The flood metric (<1%) exists exactly to catch this; the fix is consequential/state-based suppression of upset cascades, not further trimming of the quiet-day load.

Alarm HMI & Operator Response

The last metre of the alarm system: how it looks, sounds, and gets acted on.

Presentation Rules (ISA-101 territory — → A14)
  • 1Colour is reserved for alarms. On a high-performance HMI the running plant is grayscale; red/amber/yellow appear only when something needs a human. A screen full of decorative red teaches the eye to ignore red.
  • 2Priority is triple-coded — colour + shape/symbol + sound, so it survives colour-blindness and noisy control rooms. Distinct tones per priority; the highest priority may repeat until acknowledged.
  • 3The alarm summary is a working list, not a log — sortable by priority then time, filterable by area, showing tag, description, priority, state, time-in-alarm. One line per alarm, plain language ("Reactor A jacket temp high — cooling valve TV-201"), not cryptic tag soup.
  • 4Silence ≠ acknowledge ≠ reset. Silence kills the horn (alarm still active & unacked); acknowledge records "a human saw it" (alarm may remain active); the alarm clears only when the process condition clears. Three separate acts, three separate meanings — collapsing them destroys the audit trail.
  • 5Shelved and suppressed lists are one click away. The invisible part of the alarm system must be permanently inspectable.

📖 Alarm response procedures

The rationalization record resurfaces at the console: for any alarm the operator can call up cause · consequence · corrective action · time available. This converts the D&R paperwork into real-time decision support — and is what makes the LOPA operator-response credit (→ A52) defensible.

🔔 Annunciation etiquette

New alarm: flashing + tone until acknowledged, then steady while active. Cleared-but-unacked: shown distinctly (the operator must learn it happened). Re-alarm after clear: full annunciation again. These small state rules are what make the summary trustworthy at 3 a.m.

Quick Check

Why must "silence" and "acknowledge" be separate actions?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Silence is ergonomic relief — the horn stops so the operator can think; it says nothing about the alarm being seen. Acknowledge is an accountable record that a human registered this specific alarm. Merge them and one reflexive horn-kill "acknowledges" a page of alarms nobody read — the audit trail and the operator's mental model both corrupt.

Running the Programme

Bad actors, weekly rhythm, MOC — the unglamorous loop that keeps the board quiet.

The Bad-Actor Pareto — Where the Noise Actually Lives
The Weekly Rhythm
  • 1Pull the numbers — historian or alarm-analytics report: rates, floods, top 10, stale list, shelved list. Any SCADA historian (→ A14) plus a spreadsheet is enough to start.
  • 2Treat the top bad actors — usually one of: deadband/delay too tight, dead or drifting instrument (→ A50), setpoint inside the normal band, missing state-based logic, or a genuine recurring process problem the alarm is correctly reporting.
  • 3Every fix goes through MOC — setpoint, deadband, priority and suppression changes are recorded against the master database; "temporary" console-side edits are the drift mechanism audits exist to catch.
  • 4Review the invisible lists — shelved > philosophy limit? out-of-service without return date? Chase them; these are the alarms incidents are made of.
  • 5Close the loop with operators — the people who shelved things know exactly which alarms are noise. Their irritation is your best rationalization input.

🧰 Tooling spectrum

Commercial alarm analytics (Honeywell DynAMo, PAS PlantState Integrity, ABB, AVEVA) automate the maths — but the entry level is a historian export and a pivot table counting annunciations per tag per week. The Pareto doesn't care who computes it.

🔁 Tie-ins across this library

Chattering usually means instrument or damping issues (A50); control-loop oscillation spraying deviation alarms means tuning (A10); alarm floods during trips mean missing suppression design; repeated genuine alarms mean a reliability problem (A25 RCA).

Quick Check

Your top bad actor is a low-flow alarm that fires 300×/week, always during the nightly CIP cycle. What's the right fix?

Tap to reveal ↓A: State-based suppression: low flow is normal in the CIP state, so the alarm should be disabled (by designed logic keyed to the CIP mode), not deadbanded, re-prioritized, or shelved nightly by hand. The alarm is correct in the run state — the system just didn't know what state it was in.

Failure Modes, Recap & Quiz

How alarm systems fail their operators — and the one-line truths to keep.

How Alarm Systems Fail
🌊 Flood during upset

The system works on quiet days and collapses in the event it exists for. Missing consequential/state-based suppression.

📢 Alarm as status

Commanded stops, running lights and FYI messages annunciated as alarms — noise by design. Failed the operator-action test.

🎖️ Priority inflation

Everything "High" because every engineer's tag feels important. Priority stops ranking; operators re-guess under stress.

🗄️ Shelved & forgotten

Suppression without timeout or review — the alarm that "didn't go off" in the incident report was muted years ago.

🎯 Setpoint = trip point

Alarm annunciates as the trip acts. Zero response time — a notification cosplaying as a protection layer.

📉 Nobody measures

No KPIs, no bad-actor list, no audit. Entropy wins in 12–24 months; the cleanup project becomes a cycle, not a system.

One-Page Recap
  • 1An alarm is an abnormal condition requiring timely operator action — no action, no alarm. This single test does half the work.
  • 2ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 impose a lifecycle: philosophy → identification → rationalization → design → implementation → operation → monitoring → MOC → audit — a control loop around the alarm system.
  • 3Priority = consequence severity × time to respond, from a matrix fixed in the philosophy. Healthy shape ≈ 80/15/5.
  • 4Setpoints must leave a response gap below the trip and clear air above normal; deadband + delays are the standard cures for chattering and fleeting alarms.
  • 5Only three sanctioned silences: shelve (operator, auto-expiring), designed suppression (engineered, state-based), out-of-service (authorized maintenance).
  • 6KPIs per operator: ~150/day target, ≤10 per 10 min, flood <1% of time, top-10 <5%, stale ≈ 0 — measured weekly, fed back via the bad-actor Pareto.
  • 7The HMI reserves colour for alarms, triple-codes priority, and keeps silence/acknowledge/clear as distinct acts with distinct meanings.
  • 8A rationalized alarm + trained response is worth ×0.1 in LOPA — alarm management is what keeps that credit honest (→ A52).
Final Quiz — Tap to Reveal

Q1: Rationalization demoted 40% of a plant's alarms to events. Production complains "we lost visibility". Answer them.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Nothing was deleted from the historian — events are still recorded and trendable. What changed is that conditions with no operator action no longer interrupt the operator. Visibility of data is preserved; visibility of the alarms that matter is restored, because they're no longer buried under 40% noise.

Q2: Why is "alarms configured" a meaningless KPI while "alarms annunciated per operator per day" is the core one?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Configured counts measure the database; annunciated-per-operator measures the human load the system actually imposes. The constraint being engineered is operator attention (~1 alarm/10 min sustained), so every KPI is normalized to what interrupts one human at one console.

Q3: A high-pressure alarm needs 4 min of operator response, the excursion can run at 5%/min, and the trip is at 92%. Highest workable alarm setpoint?

Tap to reveal ↓A: SP ≤ 92% − 5%/min × 4 min = 72% (before margin). If the normal band tops out near 70%, the design barely works and deserves margin review; if normal reaches 80%, alarm and normal overlap — the alarm can't do its job and the protection must move to an interlock or the process must change.

Q4: What makes designed suppression legitimate where a forced point or a 999% setpoint is not?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Designed suppression is engineered against a documented condition, reviewed in rationalization, automatically reversed when the condition ends, and permanently visible on the suppressed list. Forces and absurd setpoints share none of those properties — they're invisible, unbounded, and discovered during incident investigations.

Q5: Where does alarm management sit relative to the SIS in the protection-layer stack — and why must they stay independent?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The alarm + operator layer acts before the SIS: it's the layer that can correct the excursion so the trip never happens, worth one order of magnitude in LOPA. It must be independent of the SIS (separate sensors/logic where credited) so one failure can't erase both layers — and its credit is only valid if the alarm system is managed well enough that the operator actually notices (→ A52).

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems, fully worked — KPI audit, priority, setpoint placement, chattering cure.

WE1 — KPI Audit of an Overloaded Plant
A plant logs 8,640 annunciated alarms/day across 2 operator consoles.
Per operator: 8640 / 2 = 4,320 /day = 4320/24 = 180 /hr = 3 per minute, continuously.

Targets: ~150/day (6/hr). Overload factor = 4320 / 150 ≈ 29×.

Assessment: "Overloaded" band — the alarm system is functionally off. Plan (in order): (1) top-10 bad-actor treatment — at a typical 30–50% share this alone may halve the load; (2) blanket deadband/delay defaults per Page 6 (often another 30–50% off, since chattering dominates such systems); (3) operator-action-test triage of the top 200 tags; (4) full rationalization once load is low enough for D&R findings to be visible. Insight: at 29× overload you do not start with philosophy workshops — you stop the bleeding first, then institutionalize.

WE2 — Priority Assignment via the Matrix
Alarm: cooling-water low flow to a compressor bearing jacket.
Consequence if no action: bearing overheat → machine trip in ~20 min (major, not severe — trip protects).
Corrective action: start standby pump (2 min task). Time available ≈ 20 min.

Matrix row "Major", column "3–30 min" → Priority: MEDIUM.

Discussion: Two classic errors would inflate this: scoring the consequence as if the trip did not exist (that would be "severe — machine wreck", but the trip is a separate protection layer and rationalization scores the consequence given other layers), and letting the machine's cost argue for "High" (cost enters through severity, which the matrix already consumed). The matrix's job is to make exactly these debates terminate.

WE3 — Alarm Setpoint Placement
Tank high-level trip at 95%. Max fill rate = 2%/min. Operator diagnose+act = 6 min; process lag (valve travel + line pack) = 2 min. Margin = 3%.

SPalarm ≤ 95 − 2 × (6 + 2) − 3 = 95 − 16 − 3 = 76%
Normal band top = 70% → clearance = 76 − 70 = 6% of span.

Check the second constraint: with level noise of ±1% and a 5% deadband (clear at 71%), the alarm set at 76% stays outside normal operation — workable, but the 6% gap is worth protecting: any future "let's run the tank at 80%" debottleneck quietly deletes this alarm's usefulness, which is why setpoint bases are documented and MOC-controlled.

WE4 — Curing a Chattering Bad Actor
Flow alarm at 50 t/h on a signal with ±3% noise; deadband 0, delay 0 → 620 annunciations/week (top bad actor).

Apply Page-6 defaults: deadband 5% (clear at 47.5 t/h on a 0–100 span → 5 units), on-delay 15 s.
Deadband > peak-to-peak noise (6%) is not quite met → choose deadband 7% + 15 s delay.
Result (typical): 620 → <10 genuine annunciations/week — a ~60× reduction from two parameters.

Insight: the deadband must exceed the noise band measured at the alarm setpoint operating condition (turbulent flow noise grows with flow). Confirm with the historian, not the datasheet — and if 7% of span materially delays a real excursion's clearing indication, revisit whether the alarm's setpoint or the instrument's damping (→ A50) is the real problem.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Work each one before revealing — the reveal is the marking scheme.

Problems 1–10

P1: Convert the "150 alarms/day" target into alarms per hour and per 10 minutes. Why is the 10-minute number the more operationally meaningful?

Tap to reveal ↓A: 150/24 ≈ 6.25/hr ≈ 1 per 10 min. The 10-min bucket matches human working memory and task duration — an operator handles one thing at a time; burst structure (10 in one bucket, none for hours) is what averages hide and what the flood metric captures.

P2: A plant has 12,000 configured alarms and annunciates 90/day/operator. Another has 900 configured and annunciates 1,900/day. Which alarm system is healthier, and what does this say about "database size" KPIs?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The first, decisively — 90/day is inside target while 1,900 is deep overload. Configured count measures potential, annunciated-per-operator measures imposed load. Database size KPIs are only useful for rationalization progress tracking, never for operability.

P3: High alarm at 80%, deadband 3% of span, signal noise ±2%. Will it chatter? Show the arithmetic.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Set at 80, clear at 77. Noise peak-to-peak = 4% > deadband 3% → when the mean sits between ~78 and ~79, peaks cross 80 (set) and troughs cross 77 (clear) — it will chatter. Rule: deadband must exceed peak-to-peak noise (here ≥4–5%), or add on/off delay.

P4: An operator shelves "pump seal pot level low" every shift. What are the three legitimate long-term outcomes for this alarm?

Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Fix the underlying condition (seal pot genuinely low — a maintenance problem, → A25); (2) re-rationalize: change setpoint/deadband/state logic if the alarm design is wrong; (3) demote to event/alert if it fails the operator-action test. Perpetual shelving is a symptom report, never a solution.

P5: During a compressor trip, 55 alarms annunciate in 4 minutes; 51 are downstream consequences. Compute the flood status and name the two design tools that fix it.

Tap to reveal ↓A: 55 in 10 min ≫ 10 → flood. Fixes: designed consequential suppression keyed to the trip state (suppress the 51), and first-out capture presenting the initiating alarm prominently. Target: the operator sees ~4 actionable alarms plus one cause banner.

P6: Priority distribution comes out 55% High / 30% Med / 15% Low after rationalization. Diagnose the two most likely process causes.

Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Severity scored without crediting existing protection layers (everything looks catastrophic if you ignore the trips — see WE2); (2) time-to-respond systematically underestimated (defaulting to "<3 min" when real dynamics give 20). Both push cells up-left in the matrix. Re-calibrate the D&R team, don't bend the matrix.

P7: A level transmitter fails and reads 0% — the LO and LOLO alarms stand for 9 days. Which nuisance category is this, which lifecycle stage failed, and what's the correct state for those alarms during repair?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Stale alarms. Monitoring & assessment failed (a stale-list review would have caught it day one) plus maintenance discipline. Correct state: out-of-service under authorization with a return-to-service step that re-tests the alarms after the transmitter repair (→ A50 for the calibration).

P8: Design the alarm attributes for "cooling water supply temp high" on a chiller loop: normal 28–30°C, consequence matters above 38°C where the chiller trips, worst drift 0.5°C/min, operator response 5 min, noise ±0.3°C on a 0–50°C span.

Tap to reveal ↓A: SP ≤ 38 − 0.5×5 − margin(1°C) = 34.5 → set 34°C (8% above normal top, clear air ✓). Deadband: 1% of span = 0.5°C > p-p noise 0.6°C — marginal, use 1°C. On-delay 60 s (temp default). Priority: consequence = chiller trip (major), time ≈ 8 min at worst drift → Medium. Document the 38°C basis and the drift rate in the master database.

P9: Management wants a "target: zero alarms" campaign. Explain in three sentences why zero is the wrong target and what the right one is.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Alarms are the operator's engineered early-warning layer — zero annunciations means either a perfect plant (no), or a muted system that has abdicated its LOPA credit. The target is a right-sized load: ~150/day/operator of genuinely actionable alarms, floods <1%, stale ≈ 0. The goal is signal quality, not silence.

P10: Your SCADA supports only deadband, on-delay and a global "disable" bit per alarm. Sketch how you'd still implement the three sanctioned suppression states within ISA-18.2's intent.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Shelving: an HMI-scripted timer that sets the disable bit and clears it after the philosophy limit, writing both acts to the event log and a visible "shelved" screen. Designed suppression: PLC logic (→ A07) driving disable bits from state conditions, documented per alarm in the master database. Out-of-service: the same bit under a maintenance permit procedure with a tagged list and return-to-service test. The states live in procedure + logic + visibility, not in vendor features.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

The three pieces of mathematics under the discipline — and the seven ways exams and audits catch you.

Derivation 1 — Why Deadband Kills Chattering Exponentially (Rice's Formula)
For Gaussian noise of std-dev σ riding on a signal at distance a below a threshold, the mean
up-crossing rate is (Rice, 1945):   ν(a) = ν₀ · e−a²/2σ²

where ν₀ is the noise bandwidth-dependent crossing rate at zero margin.
With deadband D, re-annunciation requires first crossing D below the setpoint then returning:
effective rate ∝ e−D²/8σ² (margin a = D/2 either side of the mid-hysteresis point).

Consequence: annunciation rate falls with e−D² — doubling deadband from 1σ to 2σ
cuts chattering not by 2× but by ~e−(4−1)/8·4… orders of magnitude. Deadband is exponential medicine.

Exam form: you won't be asked to reproduce Rice rigorously — you'll be asked why a 5% deadband silences what a 2% deadband barely touched. Answer: crossing probability of Gaussian noise decays as exp(−a²/2σ²), so threshold margin buys exponential, not linear, quiet. The same mathematics runs comparator hysteresis in electronics (→ A29's gate-threshold cousin).

Derivation 2 — The Time Budget of an Alarm
Consequence at SPtrip; excursion rate ṙ; alarm at SPa; on-delay Td.

Time from annunciation to consequence:  Tavail = (SPtrip − SPa)/ṙ − Td
Required:  Tavail ≥ Tdiagnose + Tact + Tprocess lag + margin

Rearranged for design:  SPa ≤ SPtrip − ṙ·(Tresp + Td) − ṙ·margin
…and feasibility requires  SPa > top of normal band + deadband + noise.

The exam trap inside: the on-delay subtracts from the operator's budget — a 60 s delay on a 5%/min excursion costs 5% of span. Fast processes therefore get small delays and bigger deadbands; slow processes tolerate long delays. When the two feasibility inequalities can't both hold, the honest conclusions are only: slow the process, move the trip, automate the response, or accept there is no alarm — never "alarm anyway and hope".

Derivation 3 — Why Averages Hide Floods (Poisson Bursts)
If alarms arrived independently at the healthy average λ = 1 per 10 min,
P(N ≥ 10 in one 10-min bucket) = 1 − Σk=0..9 e−1/k! ≈ 1.1 × 10−7 — floods would be unheard of.

Real floods happen because arrivals are not independent: one root cause fires N consequences,
so the arrival process is clustered (a "cascade" process), and P(flood) is set by
P(upset) × (cascade size ≥ 10) — parameters you engineer with suppression design, not with averages.

Meaning: the average-rate KPI and the flood KPI measure two different physical mechanisms — background nuisance (independent arrivals) vs. cascade structure (dependent arrivals). That is why both appear in the standard, and why fixing one never fixes the other.

Seven Exam & Audit Traps
  • 1Alarm ≠ trip. An alarm demands a human response; anything acting automatically is an interlock/SIS. Exam questions love blurring this — always name who acts.
  • 2Shelving ≠ suppression ≠ out-of-service. Three states, three authorities, three durations. Using one word for all three loses marks and, in plants, alarms.
  • 3Deadband is % of span, not % of reading — same convention trap as instrument accuracy (→ A50). A 5% deadband on a 0–200 m³/h span is 10 m³/h everywhere.
  • 4Rates are per operator console, annunciated. Quoting plant-wide or configured numbers against the 150/day target is comparing apples to databases.
  • 5Priority comes from the matrix — severity given other protection layers × time to respond. Importing "asset value" or "who shouted loudest" invalidates the distribution.
  • 6On-delay spends response time. Any delay added to cure a fleeting alarm must be re-checked against Derivation 2's budget — the cure can starve the response.
  • 7The average rate can be perfect while the system is broken. Always quote average and flood and stale metrics; each detects a failure mode the others miss.

Hands-On Ladder (runs on any SCADA/PLC rig — or a spreadsheet)
  • 1Export one week of alarm/event history from any SCADA (or use a vendor demo project). Count annunciations per tag — build the Pareto. Find your top 10.
  • 2Compute the four core KPIs: avg/day, worst 10-min bucket, % time in flood, top-10 share. Grade the system against Page 9.
  • 3Take the worst chatterer; measure its noise band from the historian; size deadband + delay per Page 6; predict the reduction, apply, verify.
  • 4Run a mini-D&R on 20 alarms with a colleague: operator-action test, fill cause/consequence/action/time, assign priority from the matrix. Time yourselves — feel why real projects take months.
  • 5Write a 2-page alarm philosophy for your lab rig: 3 priorities, the matrix, deadband defaults, one shelving rule, two KPI targets.
  • 6Implement state-based suppression in a PLC (→ A07): a "CIP active" bit that suppresses two flow alarms, with the suppressed list shown on the HMI.
  • 7Build the setpoint-budget calculator (Derivation 2) in a spreadsheet; stress it with your rig's real rates.
  • 8Simulate an upset (or replay one from history) and count the cascade. Design first-out + consequential suppression for it on paper.

🎓 MSc & career context

Alarm management appears in process-safety and control-room-design courses, and in every serious plant's PSM system. For an automation engineer it is a high-leverage, low-capex specialism: a philosophy, a Pareto and three months of discipline visibly transform a control room.

🧠 Remember the load number

~1 alarm per 10 minutes sustained, ≤10 per 10 minutes burst, flood <1% of hours, 80/15/5 priorities. Four numbers — carry them into every control room you ever walk into.
