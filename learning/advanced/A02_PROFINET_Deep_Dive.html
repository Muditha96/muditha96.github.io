The Line That Only Failed When Somebody Watched Video

A RUBBER GLOVE DIPPING LINE, BIYAGAMA — 34 DEVICES ON ONE PIECE OF STRING

Thirty-four PROFINET devices — distributed I/O heads, eleven drives, two RFID readers, a leak-test station — daisy-chained through their own two-port switches into one line, 34 hops deep, running at a 1 ms update time. It passed its factory acceptance test. It ran for five months. Then the quality department mounted four inspection cameras and put them on the nearest free device port, because there was one.

From that week, the line dropped out roughly twice a shift with “station failure” on the devices at the far end — never the same device twice, always the far end, and always — though it took two months for anyone to notice the correlation — while somebody was reviewing footage from a laptop in the QA office.

Fault 1 — nobody had computed the latency, only the bandwidth. The integrator had checked the bandwidth, and the bandwidth was innocent. A PROFINET RT frame carrying up to 36 bytes of process data is a minimum-size Ethernet frame: 64 bytes on the MAC layer, 84 bytes on the wire once you add preamble and inter-frame gap.

RT frame on the wire = 7 preamble + 1 SFD + 6 DA + 6 SA + 4 VLAN + 2 EtherType + 2 FrameID + 36 data + 4 APDU status + 4 FCS + 12 IFG = 84 bytes = 672 bits t_frame = 672 bits / 100 Mbit/s = 6.72 µs cyclic load on the first link = 34 × 6.72 µs = 228.5 µs per 1 ms cycle = 22.8 % of the link, each direction, full duplex

Twenty-three per cent. Comfortable. But bandwidth is not what a line topology spends — it spends hops. A frame addressed to the last device is received in full and re-transmitted by every device switch in front of it, and each of those switches can be busy with something else when the frame arrives.

per hop, quiet network: store-and-forward of a 64-byte frame = 64 × 8 / 100 = 5.12 µs bridge processing (typical device) ≈ 3.0 µs ---------- 8.12 µs 33 hops × 8.12 µs = 268 µs ← this is the number the FAT measured per hop, with one full-size frame already in transmission: blocking = (1522 + 20) × 8 / 100 = 123.36 µs (15.2× the hop itself) 33 hops × (8.12 + 123.36) = 4,338.8 µs = 4.34 ms against an update time of 1 ms and a watchdog of 3 × 1 ms = 3 ms

A PROFINET RT frame is tagged with VLAN priority 6, so it jumps the queue — but it cannot interrupt a frame that has already started transmitting. Four cameras streaming through thirty-three device switches put a 1,522-byte frame in front of the cyclic data at hop after hop. The 268 µs design became a 4.34 ms worst case, the watchdog expired, and the devices at the end of the string — the ones with the most hops in front of them — reported station failure. The network never ran out of bandwidth. It ran out of hops.

Fault 2 — the spare part could not be fitted. During one of these stops a drive interface module was (wrongly) blamed and swapped for the identical spare from the store. It did not come up. A PROFINET device is not identified by its IP address or its MAC address but by a station name assigned over DCP, and the spare had spent a fortnight in a test rig where somebody had named it IO-Device-03 — a name that is not merely wrong, it is illegal, because PROFINET station names may not contain uppercase letters. The plant's automatic device-replacement feature could not act either, because that requires a spare with no name at all and a topology configured in the project; neither condition held.

consequence: wait for the integrator's laptop, the right TIA version and the licence 6 h 10 min of line downtime at 3,200 pairs/h = 19,700 pairs what it should have been, with topology configured and the spare factory-reset: plug in, controller reads LLDP neighbours, assigns the name < 2 min, no laptop, no engineer, no licence

Fault 3 — the “fix” was a ring, and the ring did not fix anything. After the second month the integrator closed the line into a ring with an MRP manager, and reported that the plant now had media redundancy. It did. What it did not have was a redundancy scheme fast enough to matter, because the ring's recovery time and the device's watchdog are two completely different clocks and nobody compared them.

PROFINET watchdog = update time × accepted cycles without IO data = 1 ms × 3 = 3 ms MRP reconfiguration (standard class) ≈ 200 ms 200 / 3 = 67× too slow

Every ring event still stopped the line — only now the diagnosis said “station failure” instead of “link down”, which made it harder to find, not easier. MRP protects you from a permanent loss of a cable. It does not protect a 1 ms cyclic connection from a momentary one, and no amount of ring topology will make it do so.

Three faults, three layers: a timing bound nobody computed, an identity model nobody understood, and a redundancy scheme whose recovery time nobody compared with the watchdog it was supposed to protect. All three are ordinary, documented and long-standing. They are diagnosed across the pages that follow and repaired, with numbers, in the table on page 14.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

The cyclic traffic used 23 % of the link. So why did adding camera traffic break a 1 ms cycle that had worked for five months?

C. This is the single most important idea in the module and it is the one that separates people who can design a PROFINET network from people who can only draw one. A store-and-forward switch that is halfway through transmitting a 1,522-byte frame will finish it, whatever arrives behind it. That is 123.36 µs at 100 Mbit/s, and on a line it can happen at every hop. Thirty-three hops turned a 268 µs design into a 4.34 ms worst case — and the worst case is the only case a watchdog cares about. Note what does not appear anywhere in that calculation: the total bandwidth.

A is the reflex answer and it is arithmetically impossible here. Four cameras at, say, 8 Mbit/s each is 32 Mbit/s; add 23 % cyclic and you are at roughly 55 % of one direction of a full-duplex 100 Mbit/s link. Nothing was congested. A link at 55 % average utilisation delivers worst-case latencies that violate a 1 ms cycle every day of the week — average utilisation tells you nothing about determinism.

B is wrong on the facts. RT frames carry a VLAN tag with priority 6 and every PROFINET-conformant switch has a strict-priority queue for them. Priority worked exactly as designed. Priority decides who goes next; it has nothing to say about who is already going. Fixing that requires either a schedule that keeps the wire clear (IRT, page 9) or frame preemption (802.3br / TSN, A37).

D is almost never true and is almost always blamed. An integrated two-port switch forwards a minimum frame in about 8 µs. Thirty-three of them cost 268 µs, which the FAT measured and accepted. The devices did their job; the topology did not.

Ordinary Ethernet, Extraordinary Rules

PROFINET does not replace Ethernet — it constrains it. Everything difficult about the protocol follows from that one decision.

DEFINITIONCORE

PROFINET is the industrial Ethernet standard of PROFIBUS & PROFINET International, specified in IEC 61158 (services and protocol) and IEC 61784 (profiles). It runs unmodified Ethernet at the physical and MAC layers, and adds four things on top: a device and engineering model, a name-based identity scheme, a set of traffic classes with a priority and (optionally) a schedule, and a diagnostic model.

Every one of those four is a constraint, not a feature. That is the sentence to carry out of this page. A PROFINET network is an Ethernet network that you are no longer allowed to treat as an Ethernet network — you may not plug in an arbitrary switch, you may not let arbitrary traffic share a segment with a 1 ms cycle, you may not assign identity by DHCP, and you may not build a topology without counting the hops. The pages that follow are, almost entirely, those constraints and the arithmetic behind them.

WHERE IT CAME FROM — AND WHY THAT STILL SHOWSSTRETCH

PROFIBUS DP, from 1993, was an RS-485 master–slave bus with a cyclic process image: the master read a fixed block of input bytes from every slave and wrote a fixed block of output bytes, in a fixed order, every cycle, whether anything had changed or not. Its slaves were described by a machine-readable text file, the GSD. Its slaves had slots into which modules were plugged. Its diagnostics were channel-oriented: this channel, on this module, in this slot, has this fault.

PROFINET, from 2003, kept every one of those ideas and changed only the wire. The GSD became the GSDML (XML instead of text). The slot model survived intact and gained subslots. The cyclic process image survived intact and became the IO CR. Channel diagnosis survived intact. This is why PROFINET feels natural to an engineer who grew up on PROFIBUS and feels strange to one who grew up on TCP/IP: it is a fieldbus that happens to be carried on Ethernet, not a network protocol that happens to be used in a factory.

It also explains the module's central tension. A fieldbus assumes the medium is dedicated, deterministic and yours. Ethernet assumes the medium is shared, best-effort and everybody's. PROFINET spends its entire specification reconciling those two assumptions — and every failure in the cold open was a place where somebody forgot which of the two they were standing in.

WHAT PROFINET IS NOT — FIVE HONEST LIMITSCORE
  • 1
    It is not deterministic by default. Plain RT gives you priority, which is a probabilistic improvement, not a bound. A hard bound requires either a counted, controlled hop budget (page 8) or IRT (page 9). Marketing material that says “PROFINET is deterministic” is describing conformance class C.
  • 2
    It is not secure. DCP has no authentication whatsoever. A laptop on any port can discover every device, read its name, and rename it — which stops the plant. RT frames are unauthenticated Layer 2 frames. The PROFINET Security Classes 1–3 published by PI are the answer and are only beginning to appear in products; until then, security is entirely a matter of segmentation and physical access (A54).
  • 3
    It is not a safety system. PROFINET carries PROFIsafe; it does not implement it. The network is a “black channel” that the safety layer assumes is broken (page 13).
  • 4
    It is not semantically rich. An IO CR is a block of bytes at a fixed offset. There is no type, no unit, no engineering range, no timestamp and no quality flag on a process value — those live in your PLC's symbol table, in a spreadsheet, or nowhere. If you want meaning on the wire you want OPC UA, and the modern answer is to run both.
  • 5
    It is not interoperable by accident. Two conformance-class-B devices from two vendors will exchange cyclic data. Whether their diagnostics, their I&M records, their parameter handling and their firmware-update procedures behave alike is a different question, and the answer is regularly “no”.

The Frame You Will Spend Your Career Looking At

The RT frame is the whole trick: strip the stack, keep the Ethernet, put the process image straight after the EtherType.

THE THREE CHANNELSCORE

Every PROFINET network carries three kinds of traffic on the same copper, and almost every design mistake is a failure to keep them apart.

NRT

ms–s

Ordinary TCP/IP and UDP. Web servers, engineering downloads, firmware updates, OPC UA, your laptop. Uses the full IP stack. Best effort — and it will happily block your cycle.

RT (RTC1)

1–4 ms

Layer 2 frames, EtherType 0x8892, VLAN priority 6. No IP, no stack, no acknowledgement. Sent by the provider on a timer, consumed by the consumer on arrival. Prioritised, not scheduled.

IRT (RTC3)

31.25 µs+

Frames placed in a hardware-computed time slot on every link in the path. Requires switches with PROFINET ASICs and a planned, fixed topology. Scheduled, therefore bounded.

The naming you will meet in tools and standards: RT Class 1 is unicast RT (the ordinary case); RT Class 2 was a multicast variant now largely absorbed; RT Class 3 is IRT. A device's GSDML declares which classes it supports, and a controller will refuse to establish a connection it cannot honour.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A device needs to send 4 bytes of process data per cycle. Its neighbour needs to send 36 bytes. How do their frame times on a 100 Mbit/s link compare?

C — and this has a real design consequence. The Ethernet minimum frame is 64 bytes. A VLAN-tagged RT frame spends 28 bytes on headers, status and FCS, so any IO data payload from 1 to 36 bytes produces exactly the same 64-byte frame and exactly the same 6.72 µs. Below 36 bytes, process data is free.

Two consequences follow, and both appear in real designs. First, splitting a device's data across two submodules to “keep frames small” is pointless — they travel in one frame anyway. Second, and much more important, adding a 37th byte is not a 3 % increase, it is a step: the frame becomes 65 bytes MAC, 85 on the wire, 6.80 µs. The steps are small after that, but the plateau below 36 is genuinely flat, so a device that reports 8 bytes and a device that reports 36 impose an identical load. When you are hunting for network load, count devices first and bytes second.

A and B both assume time scales with payload, which is true only above the plateau. At 128 bytes the frame is 14.08 µs, at 256 bytes 24.32 µs, and at the 1,440-byte maximum 119.04 µs — nearly eighteen times a minimum frame, which is why a single badly-configured device with a huge process image can cost more link time than twenty ordinary ones. D inverts the truth: short frames are inefficient in bytes carried per byte sent, but they are the cheapest thing you can put on the wire in time, and time is the currency here.

Relations, Slots and the Fixed Process Image

Everything above the wire in PROFINET is this picture: one relation, four conversations, and a device made of numbered positions.

THE THREE ROLESCORE

IO Controller

Usually the PLC. Holds the expected configuration, opens the AR, writes the parameters, owns the cycle. There is normally exactly one per device.

IO Device

The field device. Holds the actual configuration. Answers, provides inputs, consumes outputs, raises alarms. Knows nothing about the plant.

IO Supervisor

The engineering PC or an HMI. Opens a limited AR for commissioning and diagnosis without disturbing the controller's. This is the role your laptop plays — and the one an attacker plays.

A device can hold several ARs at once: one IOCARSingle from its controller, one or more Supervisor ARs, and a DeviceAccess AR for read-only access to records. In a redundant system it holds an IOCARSR to each of two controllers (system redundancy S2, page 11). Understanding that ARs are independent explains a common site puzzle: your laptop can read diagnostics from a device that the PLC says has failed, because your AR is fine and the controller's is not.

SLOTS, SUBSLOTS AND WHY THE NUMBERS MATTERCORE

A device is a rack of numbered positions, exactly as PROFIBUS DP left it. A slot is a physical module position. A subslot is a logical division within it — a channel group, a submodule, a port. The interface itself lives at slot 0, called the DAP (Device Access Point), and the physical ports live in its subslots.

slot 0 DAP — the head or interface module subslot 0x8000 the interface itself (IP, name, LLDP settings) subslot 0x8001 port 1 ← link state, port statistics, neighbour subslot 0x8002 port 2 ← cable diagnostics live here too slot 1..n the plugged modules subslot 1..m the submodules inside them each submodule reports ModuleIdentNumber + SubmoduleIdentNumber the controller compares them with the project at connect → match, or a module difference diagnosis and (usually) no cyclic data for that submodule

Those port subslots — the PDEV, the physical device object — are where every useful network diagnostic lives: link up/down, speed and duplex, error counters, and the LLDP neighbour. Knowing that port diagnostics are addressable submodules like any other is what lets you read a device's own view of its cabling from the PLC program, with no extra hardware, which is a genuinely valuable trick on a plant with no managed switches.

THE FIXED PROCESS IMAGE — AND ITS THREE CONSEQUENCESSTRETCH

An IO CR is a flat block of bytes whose layout is agreed once, at connect, and never changes while the AR lives. That single design decision produces most of PROFINET's character:

  • 1
    The load is constant and computable. Unlike a request/response protocol, nothing depends on how much data changed. A PROFINET network's traffic can be calculated exactly from the device list before a single cable is pulled — which is what page 7 does.
  • 2
    Nothing is ever “asked for”. There is no polling, no request, no round trip in the cyclic path. Latency is one-way, not two-way — the opposite of Modbus, where a transaction is a question and an answer and the turnaround dominates. This is the single biggest reason PROFINET scales to 1 ms where a serial poll cannot.
  • 3
    A configuration change is a disconnection. Adding a module, changing a submodule, or altering the IO length tears down and rebuilds the AR. On a running plant that is a station failure and, in most programs, a stop. This is why PROFINET has shared devices, configuration control (option handling) and plug/pull alarms — all mechanisms for making a change look less like a disconnection than it really is.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A device fails and is replaced with the same order number but a newer hardware revision, which reports a different SubmoduleIdentNumber for one of its analogue inputs. What happens at connect?

C, and the partial nature of it is exactly what makes it dangerous. The connect request carries the expected module and submodule ident numbers for every slot. The device answers per submodule: correct, substitute, wrong, or no module. A mismatch on one submodule does not usually prevent the AR — the rest of the device runs, the PLC's IO bytes for that submodule sit at their substitute value (often zero), and the plant appears to work. A tank level that reads a constant 0.0 % and a “module difference” entry buried in the diagnostic buffer is the classic signature, and it can survive for weeks.

The lesson is procedural rather than technical: after any device replacement, check the diagnostic buffer and the module state, not just whether the lamp is green. Green means the AR opened.

A is wrong and is the most common misconception — the order number is not what is checked on the wire; VendorID, DeviceID and the ident numbers are. B overstates it: a whole-device rejection happens for a DAP mismatch or a wrong DeviceID, not for one submodule. D describes what some tools offer as an explicit, operator-confirmed action; nothing in PROFINET silently rewrites your project, and a system that did would be uncertifiable.

GSDML — The Contract You Sign Before You Buy

WHAT IT ISCORE

A GSDML file — General Station Description Markup Language — is an XML document supplied by the device vendor that tells an engineering tool everything it needs to know to configure that device without the device being present. It is the direct descendant of the PROFIBUS GSD, and it is a legal-quality technical document: what the vendor writes in it is what the device must do.

GSDML-V2.43-Siemens-ET200SP-20230815.xml | | | | | | | [-- date of issue | | [-- device family | [-- vendor [-- GSDML schema version ← your tool must support at least this

The filename is not decoration. The schema version determines which features can be described at all — and a tool that only understands V2.35 cannot import a V2.43 file, which is the single most common cause of “this device will not import” on site. The date lets you tell two revisions apart when a vendor reissues a file to fix a defect, which they do more often than the industry admits.

A Device Is Its Name

Learn which step failed and you have already halved the diagnosis. “It will not connect” is four completely different faults.

IDENTITY IS A NAME, NOT AN ADDRESSCORE

A PROFINET IO device is addressed by its NameOfStation. The IP address is assigned to it by the controller after it has been found by name, and the MAC address is used only to deliver the frames. This is the opposite of every IT convention and it is the source of more site confusion than any other single feature.

The reason is maintenance, and it is a good reason. A device that fails is replaced by an identical unit with a different MAC address. If identity were the MAC, every replacement would be a configuration change. If identity were the IP, you would need DHCP and a server on a machine that must run without one. By making identity a name that the controller can write into a factory-new device over the wire, PROFINET makes a spare part genuinely a spare part — provided the rules below are followed.

DEVICE REPLACEMENT WITHOUT A PROGRAMMING DEVICECORE

This is the feature that makes PROFINET worth its complexity to a maintenance department, and it is the feature the cold-open plant paid 6 h 10 to discover it did not have. The mechanism is LLDP, the standard IEEE 802.1AB neighbour-discovery protocol: every PROFINET port continuously announces its own station name and port number to whatever is plugged into it, and listens for the same. The controller therefore knows the physical position of every device, not just its name.

When a nameless device appears where a named one used to be, the controller compares the LLDP neighbours reported by the devices on either side against the configured topology, concludes “this can only be glove-line.drive-07”, and assigns the name over DCP. The device is running in under two minutes with no laptop, no software licence and no engineer.

A contractor plugs a laptop into a spare port to check a device, using a tool that scans the network. The plant stops. What is the most likely mechanism?

Send Clock, Reduction Ratio, and the Budget

THE THREE NUMBERS THAT SET EVERY CYCLE TIMECORE

PROFINET does not give each device its own timer. The whole network runs on one heartbeat — the send clock — and a device's update time is that heartbeat multiplied by a power of two.

update time T_u = send clock T_sc × reduction ratio R T_sc the network heartbeat. RT: 250 µs, 500 µs, 1 ms, 2 ms, 4 ms. IRT adds 31.25 µs, 62.5 µs, 125 µs. All of them are multiples of 31.25 µs — the same unit the cycle counter in the frame uses (page 3). R 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 … 512. “Send my frame every Rth heartbeat.” phase which of the R heartbeats this device uses, 1 … R. This is the field that spreads the load, and the one nobody sets. example T_sc = 1 ms, R = 4, phase = 3 → this device exchanges data every 4 ms, in heartbeats 3, 7, 11, 15 …

The reduction ratio is the most under-used feature in PROFINET engineering. Almost every project leaves every device at R = 1 because that is the default, which means a temperature transmitter whose value changes over minutes is exchanged as often as a servo drive. Setting R = 32 on that transmitter costs nothing, changes nothing that anyone can observe, and removes 31 of every 32 of its frames from the wire. This is the same idea as scan classes on a serial bus (A01) and sampling intervals on a subscription (A06) — classify by how fast the physics actually moves, not by how fast the network can go.

IRT — Buying a Bound Instead of a Hope

123.36 µs at 100 Mbit/s — which is precisely the blocking term from page 8, paid once, deliberately, in advance instead of at random, at every hop. WHAT YOU GET Jitter under 1 µs. Cycles to 31.25 µs. A bound that does not depend on what anybody else plugs in. WHAT YOU PAY CC-C hardware everywhere in the domain, a topology that is now part of the program, and no casual changes.

IRT does not make frames faster. It makes the wire empty at the moment they need it.

HOW THE SCHEDULE IS BUILTCORE

In RT, a device decides when to transmit and the switches decide what order to serve. In IRT, the engineering tool decides both, for every frame, on every link, before the plant runs, and downloads the result into the switch silicon.

That requires three things that plain RT does not:

  • 1
    A common clock. One device is the sync master (usually the controller or a head switch); everything else is a sync slave. They synchronise with PTCP, PROFINET's own precision time protocol, measuring line delay in both directions so that a frame scheduled for microsecond 40 leaves at microsecond 40 everywhere. Loss of sync is a defined, alarmed state — a device that has lost sync leaves the red phase and its data stops.
  • 2
    A known topology. The planner cannot compute a schedule without knowing exactly which port connects to which port and how long every cable is. In an IRT domain the wiring is part of the configuration: swap two patch leads and the plant will not start, by design.
  • 3
    Hardware that can enforce it. A conformance-class-C switch has a scheduler in silicon that opens and closes transmission windows on a hardware timer. This is why an IRT domain cannot contain an ordinary office switch, and why an unmanaged five-port switch bought to “add a port” will silently destroy a working IRT network.

ISOCHRONOUS MODE — THE STEP BEYOND IRTSTRETCH

IRT makes the network deterministic. That is not the same as making the machine deterministic, because the application at each end still runs on its own timer. Isochronous mode (IsoM) closes the last gap by locking the device's input sampling, the controller's program execution and the device's output application to the same synchronised clock.

T_i input latch time — every device samples its inputs at the same instant, a fixed offset before the frame is sent T_o output apply time — every device applies its outputs at the same instant, a fixed offset after the frame arrives result: all inputs across the machine share one timestamp; all outputs across the machine act at one instant, whatever the wiring delays between them. this is what “print registration”, “flying shear” and “electronic gearing” actually require — not a short cycle, but a common instant.

The cost is that the PLC's cycle is now driven by the network rather than the other way round: the program runs in a synchronous task triggered by the send clock, and if it overruns, the machine faults. This is a real engineering discipline and it is where motion control genuinely lives — see A09 Servo & Motion Control for what happens on the other side of that frame.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

An IRT machine works perfectly. During a shutdown an electrician replaces a damaged patch lead, connecting the same two devices but using port 2 instead of port 1 on one of them. What happens on restart?

C — and this is a feature, not a defect, however it feels at 02:00. The IRT schedule is computed from the exact port-to-port topology and the exact line delays. Move a cable to a different port and the delay model, and therefore the schedule, is wrong. Rather than run with a silently invalid schedule — which would mean frames missing their windows and a machine losing synchronisation unpredictably — the domain refuses to enter the red phase and raises a topology error naming both ports. Everything upstream still runs; the isochronous machine does not.

The practical consequences are procedural and belong in your maintenance instructions: label every patch lead at both ends with device and port number; photograph the cabinet before you touch it; and record in the O&M manual, in plain language, that this machine's wiring is part of its software. Plants that treat an IRT domain like an office network lose hours to this.

D describes what happens if somebody opens the engineering tool, updates the topology and downloads again; nothing recalculates a safety-relevant motion schedule on its own, and a system that did would be uncertifiable.

PROFIsafe assumes every frame may be corrupted, lost, duplicated, delayed or forged — and detects all five from the endpoints alone.

WHY THE BLACK CHANNEL IS THE RIGHT IDEACORE

Certifying a network to SIL 3 would mean certifying every switch, every cable, every MAC chip and every firmware revision in it — and re-certifying on every change. PROFIsafe instead certifies only the two endpoints and gives them the means to detect every way the channel in between can lie. The network can then be ordinary, cheap, upgradeable and shared.

The consequences are worth stating explicitly because they are frequently misunderstood on site. A switch is not a safety component. Replacing one does not require a safety assessment. Adding non-safety traffic to a segment carrying PROFIsafe does not invalidate the safety function — it can only make it trip, never make it unsafe. And the same F-container runs unchanged over PROFIBUS, over PROFINET, and over a wireless link, which is exactly why PROFIsafe outlived the wire it was born on. For SIL determination, PFHD, architectures and the rest of IEC 61508/62061, go to A52; this page owns only the transmission part.

F-parameters — set once, checked forever F_Dest_Add the F-device's safety address. Unique in the whole plant. Set by DIP switch or by tool; folded into the CRC so a frame delivered to the wrong device is rejected, not obeyed. F_Source_Add the F-host's address. F_WD_Time how long the receiver waits before declaring the channel dead and going to the safe state. The number this page is about. F_SIL the claimed integrity level. F_CRC_Length 24-bit or 32-bit CRC, by payload size and PROFIsafe version. F_Par_CRC a checksum over all of the above, so a mis-set parameter cannot go unnoticed.
SAFETY FUNCTION RESPONSE TIME — AND THE SURPRISE IN ITCORE

The number a risk assessment actually needs is not the network's cycle time. It is the safety function response time — worst case from the hazard being detected to the actuator acting. PROFIsafe's own policy gives it as:

SFRT = TWCDT + ΔWD TWCDT total worst-case delay time — add up the worst-case delays of every element in the chain: sensor, bus, F-host, bus, actuator. ΔWD the largest single difference (watchdog − worst-case delay) over all the elements — not the sum. why only the largest: in the worst case exactly ONE element is the one that actually fails and runs out its watchdog. Every other element's contribution is already counted, at its worst-case delay, inside TWCDT. Adding all the margins would be counting the same failure several times over.

The consequence is counter-intuitive and genuinely useful: the element with the most generous watchdog sets your safety distance, however fast it normally is. An F-host running a 12 ms cycle with a 100 ms watchdog contributes 76 ms to SFRT that has nothing to do with its actual speed. Tightening a watchdog that was set generously “to avoid nuisance trips” can therefore shorten a guard fence — and that is a conversation worth having with real numbers, which is what the calculator below is for.

SECURITY — THE PROFINET-SPECIFIC PARTSTRETCH

Everything protocol-specific reduces to one sentence: DCP is unauthenticated, RT is unauthenticated, and both are Layer 2, so anything electrically on the segment has total control of it. A laptop can enumerate every device, read its type and firmware, rename it, change its IP, blink its LED and reset it to factory settings — with free software, in seconds, with no credentials.

PI's answer is the PROFINET Security Classes: Class 1 is hardening and network segmentation with no protocol change; Class 2 adds authentication and integrity protection of the communication; Class 3 adds confidentiality. Class 2 and 3 devices are beginning to appear and will take a decade to become the installed base. Until then, and largely afterwards, the controls that matter are architectural:

1 segment the PROFINET network is its own layer-2 domain. Nothing else on it. 2 route traffic in and out passes a firewall, not a switch. 3 ports disable unused switch ports; enable MAC-based port security. 4 physical a network port in a production cabinet is a key to that cabinet. 5 remote no direct remote access to the PROFINET segment, ever. 6 capture monitor for DCP Set frames in production — there is no legitimate reason for one while the plant is running. the whole programme — zones, conduits, IEC 62443, patching, monitoring — belongs to A54. EIGHT IDEAS TO CARRY OUTCORE
  • 1
    A device is its name. Not its IP, not its MAC. DCP assigns names, LLDP tells the controller where each device physically is, and those two facts together are what make a spare part a spare part.
  • 2
    Bandwidth is not determinism. Cyclic load is easy and almost always fine. Worst-case latency is hard, is what a watchdog measures, and is what fails.
  • 3
    A line topology spends hops, and a hop costs 131 µs once foreign traffic exists — not the 8 µs a factory acceptance test will measure on a quiet network.
  • 4
    1 to 36 bytes of process data all cost 6.72 µs. Count devices before you count bytes.
  • 5
    The reduction ratio is free and nobody uses it. Classify every device by how fast its physics actually moves, then distribute the phases.
  • 6
    Priority decides who goes next; a schedule decides when the wire is empty. That is the entire difference between RT and IRT, and the reason IRT costs a topology you may no longer change.
  • 7
    The watchdog and the recovery time are different clocks. Redundancy is bumpless only when trecovery < Tu × k, and MRP at 200 ms almost never satisfies it.
  • 8
    PROFIsafe trusts nothing between the endpoints, which is why the network can stay ordinary — and why the largest watchdog margin in the chain, not the average speed, sets your guarding distance.
SIX FAILURE MODES — AND THEIR SIGNATURES
⚠ Far-end station failures

Signature: devices at the end of a line drop, never the same one twice, correlated with some other activity. Cause: hop count × blocking exceeds the watchdog. Fix: separate the foreign traffic, then shorten the lines. Both.

⚠ Green lamp, wrong data

Signature: a value sits at a constant 0.0 while everything reports healthy. Cause: one submodule in module-difference state after a replacement, feeding substitute values. Fix: check module state per submodule after every swap, not the device LED.

⚠ The spare that would not start

Signature: an identical replacement does not come up; the controller cannot see it. Cause: the spare has a name (often illegal, often uppercase) and the topology was never configured. Fix: factory-reset spares in the store; configure port-to-port topology.

⚠ The ring that healed and stopped the plant anyway

Signature: a cable event, MRP reports a successful reconfiguration, and every device reports station failure. Cause: 200 ms recovery against a 3 ms watchdog. Fix: MRPD, a faster class, or a watchdog decision made per consequence and written down.

⚠ The laptop that renamed a device

Signature: instantaneous, total loss of one device that is present, healthy and answering. Cause: an unauthenticated DCP Set from a commissioning tool. Fix: architectural — segment, port security, physical access. There is no patch.

⚠ The unmanaged switch that cost a capability

Signature: device replacement stops working, or a whole branch loses topology detection, after somebody “added a port”. Cause: an unmanaged switch does not participate in LLDP and drops the segment to CC-A. Fix: the specification clause that forbids them.

QUICK CHECK — SIX QUESTIONS

1. A network has 60 devices at 1 ms with 20 bytes of IO each, all at R = 1. Is the cycle possible, and what is the load on the first link?

tap to revealTwenty bytes is below the 36-byte plateau, so each frame is the minimum 6.72 µs. 60 × 6.72 = 403.2 µs of 1000 µs = 40.3 %. It is arithmetically possible — but 40 % is high enough that alarms, record data and any engineering traffic are competing for what is left, and the answer a designer gives is “possible, but classify the slow devices and get it under 20 %”.

2. Why does adding a 37th byte of process data cost more, proportionally, than adding a 137th?

tap to revealBecause below 36 bytes the frame is padded to the 64-byte Ethernet minimum, so every payload from 1 to 36 bytes costs an identical 6.72 µs. The 37th byte is the first byte that is not free — it steps the frame off the plateau. After that, each byte adds a linear 0.08 µs, which as a proportion of a growing frame is a smaller and smaller increment.

3. A plant runs a 1 ms cycle over 20 hops and has never had a problem. Is it well designed?

tap to revealNo — it is lucky, and the distinction matters. Twenty hops on a quiet network is 162 µs and works perfectly. Twenty hops the moment any foreign traffic exists is 20 × 131.48 = 2.63 ms, comfortably past a 3 ms watchdog's margin and past it entirely if k is 3 and any second event coincides. The design has no margin against a future event that is certain to happen: somebody plugging something in. A design is judged on its worst case, not its history.

4. Your spare arrives, same order number, and will not adopt the failed device's name automatically. Give three possible reasons.

tap to revealAny three of: (1) the spare already has a name and is not factory-reset; (2) the port-to-port topology is not configured in the controller project, so there is nothing to match LLDP against; (3) a neighbour is not LLDP-capable — commonly an unmanaged switch in the path; (4) it is wired into the wrong port (port 2 instead of port 1); (5) the DeviceID differs despite the same marketing name; (6) the controller's “device replacement without exchangeable medium” option is not enabled.

5. Explain, to a plant manager, why a redundant ring did not prevent a stop.

tap to reveal“The ring did its job: it repaired itself and the plant came back without anyone visiting. But repairing takes about two-tenths of a second, and the drives are configured to raise an alarm if they miss three messages in a row — which is three-thousandths of a second. The ring is roughly seventy times slower than the alarm it was supposed to prevent. To make a ring genuinely invisible, the network has to send every message both ways round it at once, which needs a higher class of switch throughout. Otherwise we accept the stop and make sure it is fast and understood.” No jargon, one ratio, and an honest choice at the end.

6. An F-host has a 12 ms cycle, a 24 ms worst-case delay and a 100 ms watchdog. Sensor: 20/30 ms. Actuator: 15/50 ms. What is SFRT, and which number would you attack first?

tap to revealTWCDT = 20 + 24 + 15 = 59 ms. The watchdog margins are 10, 76 and 35 ms, so ΔWD = 76 ms and SFRT = 135 ms. Attack the F-host watchdog — it contributes 76 of the 135 ms and has nothing to do with how fast the F-host is. Reducing it to 50 ms gives ΔWD = 35 and SFRT = 94 ms, which at K = 1600 mm/s and a 250 ms stopping time shortens the safety distance from 744 to 678 mm. The caution: a watchdog set too tight causes nuisance trips, so reduce it deliberately and then prove it under worst-case network load.

WE5 — FROM MILLISECONDS TO MILLIMETRESCORE

Brief. A light curtain guards the infeed of a cartoner. Sensor: 20 ms worst-case delay, 30 ms watchdog. F-host: 12 ms cycle, 24 ms worst-case delay, 100 ms watchdog. Drive STO: 15 ms worst-case delay, 50 ms watchdog. The machine takes 250 ms to stop. The curtain has 30 mm detection resolution. How far back must it be mounted?

TWCDT = 20 + 24 + 15 = 59 ms ΔWD = max(30−20, 100−24, 50−15) = max(10, 76, 35) = 76 ms SFRT = 59 + 76 = 135 ms ISO 13855 approach speed: S = K(T₁ + T₂) + C K = 1600 mm/s T₁ = SFRT = 0.135 s T₂ = 0.250 s C = 8(d − 14) = 8(30 − 14) = 128 mm S = 1600 × (0.135 + 0.250) + 128 = 616 + 128 = 744 mm now reduce the F-host watchdog from 100 ms to 50 ms — nothing else changes ΔWD = max(10, 26, 35) = 35 ms SFRT = 59 + 35 = 94 ms S = 1600 × (0.094 + 0.250) + 128 = 550.4 + 128 = 678 mm 66 mm of factory floor recovered by changing one configured number

Three things to take from this. First, the F-host contributed 76 of the 135 ms and only 24 ms of that was real delay — the rest was an unexamined watchdog. Second, the sensor, which is the fastest element, contributed almost nothing to ΔWD because its watchdog was tight; tight watchdogs are worth money and generous ones are not free. Third, and this is the professional caution: reducing a watchdog increases the chance of a nuisance trip under worst-case network load, so the correct sequence is reduce it, then prove it — run the machine with the network deliberately loaded and confirm it does not trip. A safety distance justified by a watchdog that trips weekly will be widened again by the first operator who gets tired of it.

Four Derivations

D1 — THE FRAME TIME, THE PLATEAU, AND THE IMPOSSIBILITY CONDITIONCORE

Start from the Ethernet frame, because PROFINET adds nothing to it. A VLAN-tagged RT frame spends its bytes as follows:

MAC frame = DA 6 + SA 6 + VLAN 4 + EtherType 2 + FrameID 2 + IO data n + APDU status 4 + FCS 4 = 28 + n IEEE 802.3 requires a minimum MAC frame of 64 bytes, so MAC = 28 + max(n, 36) ← the plateau the wire also carries, per frame, 7 preamble + 1 SFD + 12 inter-frame gap: wire = MAC + 20 t_frame(n, B) = (48 + max(n,36)) × 8 / B check n = 36, B = 100 Mbit/s → 84 × 8 / 100 = 6.72 µs ✓ check n = 1440 → 1488 × 8 / 100 = 119.04 µs ✓

Now the load. Because the process image is unconditional, every device whose phase falls in a given send clock transmits exactly one frame in each direction. On a line, all of them cross the link nearest the controller, so with N devices spread over a reduction ratio R:

frames in the busiest send clock N_phase = ⌈ N / R ⌉ (evenly distributed) load = N_phase × t_frame / T_sc and since a send clock cannot contain more transmission than it has time: N_phase × t_frame < T_sc ← the impossibility condition rearranged, the ceiling on devices per segment: N_max = ⌊ α · T_sc / t_frame ⌋, α = the utilisation you will allow at T_sc = 250 µs, t = 6.72 µs, α = 0.8 → N_max = 29 devices at T_sc = 1 ms, t = 6.72 µs, α = 0.8 → N_max = 119 devices

Two things this derivation buys you. First, an argument you can win in a meeting: 46 devices at 250 µs is 124 % of the link, which is not a matter of tuning. Second, the plateau — and the plateau is why counting devices is a better first estimate of PROFINET load than counting bytes, which is the opposite of the intuition most engineers bring from IT networking.

D2 — THE LATENCY BOUND ON A LINESTRETCH

A store-and-forward bridge cannot begin transmitting a frame until it has received all of it, because the FCS is at the end and a bridge must not propagate a corrupt frame. So the frame is serialised twice per hop — once in, once out — and the second serialisation is what the next hop's delay is measured from. Per hop:

t_sf = MAC × 8 / B receive the frame completely t_bridge lookup, queue, transmit set-up — a device property, ≈ 3 µs at 100 Mbit/s, ≈ 1.2 µs at 1 Gbit/s t_block = 1542 × 8 / B the worst case that the outgoing port is already transmitting a maximum-length foreign frame L(H) = H × (t_sf + t_bridge + t_block) at 100 Mbit/s, minimum frame: t_sf 5.12 + t_bridge 3.00 = 8.12 µs t_block = 123.36 µs ratio = 15.2 : 1 at 1 Gbit/s: t_sf 0.51 + t_bridge 1.20 = 1.71 µs t_block = 12.34 µs ratio = 7.2 : 1 — better, because bridge delay does not scale with rate

Why priority does not appear in this expression. Strict priority determines which queued frame is selected next; it cannot revoke a transmission decision already taken. The instant the first byte of a foreign frame reaches the copper, the port is committed for the whole frame. Priority therefore removes queuing delay behind other frames but not blocking delay behind the one in progress — and on a line, the worst case assumes that happens at every hop independently.

three ways to attack L(H), in order of practical availability reduce H topology. Always available, costs cable and a switch. A star of branches b deep gives H = b + 1, not N − 1. remove t_block either isolate the RT path (administrative — free, fragile) or schedule it away (IRT — expensive, robust) or preempt the giant frame (802.3br / TSN → A37, which fragments it to ≈ 1 µs of residual blocking) increase B divides both terms by 10 — and is usually unavailable, because PROFINET field devices are 100 Mbit/s and the honest caveat: this is a bound, not a prediction. The typical latency is close to H × 8.12 µs. That is precisely why the fault appears months after the acceptance test and not during it.
D3 — TWO CLOCKS, AND WHY MRP CANNOT SATISFY THE INEQUALITYCORE

A consumer declares its provider dead when it has missed k consecutive expected frames. That is the entire watchdog mechanism, and it is measured in update times:

WD = T_u × k = T_sc × R × k, k ≥ 3 a redundancy protocol interrupts delivery for its recovery time t_rec, which is a property of the protocol and the ring and contains no term in T_u. bumpless ⇔ t_rec < T_sc × R × k MRP recovers by: detecting the break (test frames, or link-down), telling the manager, unblocking the redundant port, and flushing every switch's forwarding database so the new path is learned. Each stage is bounded by timers that were designed for plant restoration, not for a 1 ms control loop, and the standard class lands near 200 ms. T_u = 1 ms, k = 3 → WD = 3 ms 200 / 3 = 67× too slow T_u = 4 ms, k = 3 → WD = 12 ms 200 / 12 = 17× too slow T_u = 4 ms, k = 64 → WD = 256 ms satisfied — at 256 ms of blindness MRPD does not reconfigure at all. Every frame is sent both ways round the ring and the consumer accepts the first copy and discards the second, so a break removes a duplicate rather than a path: t_rec = 0, and the inequality holds for every T_u, unconditionally.

The general form of the lesson outlives PROFINET: any redundancy scheme that repairs has a recovery time you must compare with the detector above it; any scheme that duplicates has none. This is the same structural argument as FRER in TSN (A37) and PRP/HSR in substation networks, and it is why those schemes exist despite costing twice the bandwidth. Duplication is the only redundancy that is free of time.

D4 — WHY SFRT ADDS ONLY THE LARGEST WATCHDOG MARGINSTRETCH

This is the derivation candidates get wrong, and the error is always the same: summing every element's watchdog margin. Set up the timing properly and the reason becomes obvious.

a safety chain has elements i = 1..n (sensor, bus, F-host, bus, actuator), each with a worst-case delay WCDTₕ and a watchdog WDₕ ≥ WCDTₕ. Case A — nothing fails. Every element delivers within its WCDT and the signal arrives after Σ WCDTₕ = TWCDT. No watchdog runs out. Case B — one element fails, say element j. Its successor waits the full WD∐ before declaring the channel dead and commanding the safe state. Every OTHER element still contributes its worst-case delay: response = Σₕ≠∐ WCDTₕ + WD∐ = TWCDT − WCDT∐ + WD∐ = TWCDT + (WD∐ − WCDT∐) the worst such case is the largest margin: SFRT = TWCDT + max∐ (WD∐ − WCDT∐) = TWCDT + ΔWD Case C — two elements fail. Only one of them can be first; the second failure is downstream of a chain already commanding the safe state, so it cannot lengthen the response. Hence “max”, never “sum”.

Three consequences worth carrying. (1) A generous watchdog on a fast element is pure cost — it inflates ΔWD without ever reflecting real delay, and WE5 turned 50 ms of it into 66 mm of guarding. (2) Only one watchdog matters, so the design action is to find the largest margin and attack it, not to shave every element. (3) The floor is TWCDT: no watchdog tuning gets below the honest sum of worst-case delays, and beyond that point you must make an element genuinely faster.

The caveat, stated plainly: the exact bookkeeping — which delays belong to which element, whether the bus counts separately, how the F-host's own cycle enters — is defined by the PROFIsafe System Description and implemented slightly differently by each vendor's tool. Use the tool's number for the certificate; use this derivation to know whether the tool's number is plausible and which knob to turn. An engineer who can only read the tool's output cannot argue with it.

SEVEN TRAPS
TrapThe correction
“The network load is only 20 %, so we have plenty of margin.”Load and latency are different quantities. A 20 % link with 30 hops and any foreign traffic misses a 1 ms cycle. Average utilisation says nothing about a worst case.
“PROFINET is deterministic.”RT is prioritised. Only IRT (CC-C) or a counted hop budget on an isolated segment gives a bound. Priority cannot interrupt a frame in flight.

PTCPPROFINET's own precision time protocol, used to synchronise an IRT domain and measure line delays.
MRP / MRPDMedia Redundancy Protocol (repairs a ring, ≈200 ms) and Media Redundancy for Planned Duplication (sends both ways, 0 ms, needs CC-C).
Conformance class A/B/C/DA tested bundle of obligations: basic RT; + diagnosis, topology, device replacement; + IRT; + TSN.
Netload class I/II/IIIHow much foreign traffic a device survives without dropping its AR. Specify III wherever a laptop can reach.
PROFIsafe / F_WD_TimeThe safety profile carried inside ordinary IO data over a black channel, and its own watchdog — independent of the PROFINET one.
THE SHELF

Primary sources — free, and the ones that settle arguments. PI publishes far more openly than most people realise:

  • 1
    “PROFINET System Description — Technology and Application” (PI, free download). Around 40 pages, and the single best starting document in the entire fieldbus world. Read it in one evening before anything else.
  • 2
    The PI Guidelines, all free: PROFINET Design, PROFINET Installation, PROFINET Commissioning, and the Cabling and Interconnection Technology guideline. The Design guideline contains the topology and network-load rules this module derives; the Installation guideline is what you hand to a contractor.
  • 3
    IEC 61158-5-10 and 61158-6-10 for the services and the protocol, and IEC 61784-2 for the communication profiles. Buy these only when you need to settle a dispute at the byte level — the System Description covers everything else.
  • 4
    PROFIsafe System Description and PROFIsafe Profile v2.6 for the black channel, the F-parameters and the authoritative SFRT bookkeeping.
  • 5
    IEEE 802.1AB (LLDP) and 802.1Q for the Ethernet mechanisms PROFINET leans on, and 802.3 for the frame budget in derivation D1.

In your material folder (confirmed present): the PLC Handbook and Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) Book both place PROFINET in the context of a control system and are the right background for pages 4 and 12; Industrial Electricity and Motor Controls (Miller & Miller) for the panel and motor-starter context around the devices; Practical Electrical Engineering (Springer, 2016) for the transmission-line and signalling background under the physical layer; Embedded System Design (Marwedel) for what real-time actually means on the device side of the frame; and A Practical Introduction to Python Programming (Heinold) plus Practical Raspberry Pi (Horan) for the week-4 exercise below.

Tools worth owning: Wireshark with its native PROFINET dissectors — free, and the single most valuable item on this list; a managed switch with port mirroring, which is what makes Wireshark usable on a real plant; a free PROFINET scanning tool (several vendors publish one) for DCP discovery and station-name work; a cable tester that reports fault distance, not just continuity; and any PROFINET starter kit with one controller and two devices, which is worth more than any book for the first fortnight.

HONEST GAPS — WHAT THIS MODULE DOES NOT TEACH

Nineteen pages is a lot and it is still a selection. Stated plainly, so you know what you do not know:

  • 1
    Writing a PROFINET stack. The device side — ERTEC, TPS-1, netX and the open-source stacks — is a substantial embedded discipline of its own, and nothing here prepares you for it. A36 gives the embedded groundwork.
  • 2
    Ethernet-APL and process-industry PROFINET. Two-wire, intrinsically safe, long-distance field-level Ethernet is a genuinely different engineering problem — power budgets, Ex certification, trunk-and-spur design — and gets only a mention on page 10.
  • 3
    The full IRT planning algorithm. The phase planner on page 9 is a screening estimate. Real planners pipeline frames across non-contending links and produce shorter red phases; the algorithms are vendor-specific and not published in useful detail.
  • 4
    Certification and conformance testing. What a PI test laboratory actually does, and how to prepare a device for it, is a specialism worth knowing exists.
  • 5
    Deep TSN. Conformance class D rests on 802.1AS, Qbv, Qbu and CB, and those belong to A37, which owns them properly.
  • 6
    The security programme. Zones and conduits, IEC 62443, patching, monitoring and incident response are A54. Page 14 gives only what is peculiar to this protocol.
