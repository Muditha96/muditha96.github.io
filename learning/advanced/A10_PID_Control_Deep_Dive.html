Page 1 · Foundations

The Closed Loop — Where PID Lives

Precise Definition

A PID controller is a feedback algorithm that continuously computes an error — the difference between a desired setpoint and the measured process value — and drives an output that pushes that error toward zero, using three weighted responses to the present, past and predicted error.

Every closed-loop control problem has the same five blocks. The PID is the controller block; everything else is the machine it commands. Master this diagram and 90% of control engineering is naming which block is which.

The summing junction makes the error e = SP − PV. The PID turns that error into the actuator command u(t). The loop never stops correcting.

Two ideas decide everything that follows. Error e(t) = SP − PV is the only thing the controller sees. And feedback means the output you command changes the very measurement you read next — a self-correcting circle that can be made fast and stable, or made to oscillate, depending entirely on how you tune the three terms.

Page 2 · The Algorithm

The Three Terms in One Equation

The whole controller is one line of mathematics. Each term answers a different question about the error.

u(t) = Kp·e(t) + Ki·∫e(t)dt + Kd·de(t)/dt └─ present ──┘ └── past ──┘ └─ future ─┘ proportional integral derivative
P

Proportional · NOW

Reacts to the error happening right now. Bigger error → bigger push. The muscle of the loop.

I

Integral · PAST

Accumulates error over time. Keeps pushing until the leftover error is gone. The memory.

D

Derivative · FUTURE

Reacts to how fast error is changing. Anticipates and brakes early. The crystal ball.

This is the deep version of the micro-card hook “P reacts now, I remembers, D predicts.” The art is the three gains Kp, Ki, Kd. Get them right and the process is fast, accurate and stable. Get them wrong and you get sluggish response, permanent offset, or violent oscillation. Pages 3–5 take each term apart; Pages 6–7 set the gains.

Why PID dominates industry

It needs no mathematical model of the process, works on almost any single-input/single-output loop, runs in a few lines of code, and is intuitive to tune by hand. An estimated over 95% of all industrial control loops — temperature, pressure, flow, level, speed, position — are PID or a simplified PI.

Page 3 · The Muscle

The Proportional Term — and Why It Leaves Offset

The P term outputs a correction proportional to the present error: u_P = Kp·e. Double the error, double the push. Kp is the loop's aggressiveness.

Low Kp

Gentle, stable, but slow — and a large permanent gap to setpoint.

High Kp

Fast and tight, but overshoots and eventually oscillates as gain rises.

The fatal flaw: steady-state offset (droop)

A pure-P controller almost always settles below setpoint. The reason is simple and worth internalising: the actuator needs some output just to hold position against the load — but the P term can only produce output if an error still exists. So the loop must keep a small permanent error to generate the holding output. That residual gap is called offset or droop.

To hold the load, actuator needs output u_hold. P-only output is u = Kp·e → steady state needs e = u_hold / Kp ≠ 0 Bigger Kp → smaller offset, but never zero, and ringing arrives first.

Process-control language: instead of Kp, the field often uses Proportional Band (PB) = 100 / Kp (%). A 20% PB means the output swings full-scale over 20% of the measurement range — a high-gain, aggressive setting. The cure for offset is the Integral term, next.

Kp ≡ gain

Controls / instrumentation use Kp directly. High Kp = aggressive.

PB ≡ 100/Kp

Process industries use Proportional Band %. Low PB = aggressive.

Page 4 · The Memory

The Integral Term & the Windup Trap

The I term sums error over time: u_I = Ki·∫e dt. As long as any error remains, the integral keeps growing and keeps adding output — so it drives steady-state offset to exactly zero. This is why almost every real loop is at least PI.

Often written with integral time Ti (seconds/repeat): Ki = Kp / Ti Small Ti → strong, fast integral action (and more overshoot) Large Ti → gentle, slow integral action
Integral windup — the classic field failure

Every real actuator saturates: a valve can only open 100%, a drive can only deliver peak torque. Suppose the setpoint jumps far away. The output pins at maximum, yet the error stays large, so the integral keeps accumulating a huge value it can never use. When the process finally reaches setpoint, the integral is so over-charged that the output stays slammed on — producing a big, slow overshoot while it “unwinds.”

Same gains, same setpoint step. The only difference is whether the integrator is protected during saturation.

  • 1
    Clamping — stop integrating whenever the output is saturated. Simple, robust, the most common method in PLCs.
  • 2
    Back-calculation — feed the difference between commanded and actual (clamped) output back into the integrator to “bleed” it down at a tunable rate.
  • 3
    Conditional integration — only integrate when the error is within a sensible band near setpoint.
Rule for the floor

If a loop overshoots badly only after a big setpoint change or a long startup, suspect windup before you re-tune. A well-implemented anti-windup turns an ugly loop into a clean one without touching Kp or Ki.

Page 5 · The Crystal Ball

The Derivative Term, Noise & Filtering

The D term responds to the rate of change of error: u_D = Kd·de/dt. If error is shrinking fast, D brakes early — it adds damping, reduces overshoot, and lets you run higher Kp. It predicts where the error is heading and acts before it gets there.

Often written with derivative time Td (seconds): Kd = Kp · Td D contributes only during change — it is zero at steady state.
Why D is feared: it amplifies noise

Differentiation multiplies high-frequency content. A noisy sensor signal has small but very fast wiggles; de/dt turns those into large spikes that hammer the actuator. Three standard defences make D usable:

Derivative filter

Low-pass the D term (filter coefficient N ≈ 8–20). The “real” PID is always PI-D-with-filter.

Derivative on PV

Differentiate the measurement, not the error — so a setpoint step doesn't cause a violent “derivative kick.”

Just use PI

Many loops (flow, level, pressure) are noisy or fast enough that D hurts. PI is the workhorse; PID is for slow, clean loops.

Loop typeTypical formWhy
FlowPI (P small, I fast)Very noisy & fast — D would amplify turbulence noise
Pressure / LevelPIFast and often noisy; integral removes offset
TemperaturePIDSlow, clean, large lag — D shines here, predicting overshoot

Page 6 · Setting the Gains

Tuning Methods — From Ziegler–Nichols to Lambda

Tuning means choosing Kp, Ki, Kd so the loop is fast and stable. There is no single right answer — it's a trade-off between speed, overshoot and robustness. Four practical approaches dominate.

1 · Ziegler–Nichols (Ultimate Gain)

The classic 1942 recipe. Set I and D to zero, raise Kp until the loop oscillates with constant amplitude — that's the ultimate gain Ku at period Tu. Then read the gains from a table:

ControllerKpTiTd
P0.5·Ku——
PI0.45·KuTu/1.2—
PID0.6·KuTu/2Tu/8

Caveat: Z–N is aggressive (≈25% overshoot, quarter-amplitude decay) and pushing a real plant into oscillation can be unsafe. It's a starting point, not a final answer.

2 · Lambda / IMC tuning (model-based)

Modern, gentle, robust. Bump-test the process to find three numbers — process gain K, time constant τ, dead time θ (the FOPDT model). Then pick a desired closed-loop speed λ and compute the gains. Larger λ = slower but more robust. Preferred where overshoot is unacceptable (most process plants).

FOPDT model: G(s) = K·e^(−θs) / (τs + 1) Lambda PI: Kp = τ / (K·(λ+θ)) Ti = τ

3 · Auto-tune

The controller injects a relay or step test and computes gains itself. Built into virtually every modern PLC, drive and smart loop controller.

4 · Manual / heuristic

Raise Kp for speed → add I to kill offset → add a little D to tame overshoot. Slow loops: be patient, dead time bites.

The enemy is dead time (θ)

Dead time — pure transport delay before the process even starts to respond — is what makes loops hard. A high θ/τ ratio forces low gains and slow tuning, because the controller is always acting on old information. When dead time dominates, a Smith Predictor or model-predictive control beats plain PID.

Page 8 · The Code

Controller Forms & the Discrete Implementation

Two algebraically-different layouts of the same controller exist, and confusing them is a real tuning hazard.

IDEAL / PARALLEL

Kp, Ki, Kd independent

u = Kp·e + Ki∫e + Kd·de/dt. Each gain acts alone. Common in textbooks & many PLC function blocks.

STANDARD / SERIES

Kp scales all three

u = Kp·(e + (1/Ti)∫e + Td·de/dt). Kp multiplies I and D too — change Kp and the others move with it. Common in process controllers.

From calculus to code — the velocity (incremental) form

A PLC or drive runs the loop at a fixed sample time Δt (the scan — recall A07 PLC). The integral becomes a running sum and the derivative a finite difference. The velocity form below computes the change in output each scan — it gives bumpless transfer and handles anti-windup naturally:

// runs every scan, period dt e = SP - PV P = Kp * (e - e_prev) I = Ki * e * dt D = Kd * (PV_prev - PV) / dt // derivative on PV → no kick du = P + I + D out = out_prev + du out = clamp(out, OUT_MIN, OUT_MAX) // saturate → anti-windup e_prev = e ; PV_prev = PV ; out_prev = out
  • 1
    Sample fast enough. Rule of thumb: 10× faster than the loop's response. Too slow a scan = phantom lag and instability.
  • 2
    Derivative on PV, not error. Stops the setpoint-step “derivative kick” — standard in industrial blocks.
  • 3
    Bumpless & mode-aware. On Auto→Manual→Auto switches, pre-load the integrator so the output doesn't jump.
Page 9 · Beyond a Single Loop

Cascade, Feed-Forward & Where PID Connects

Real machines chain and augment PID loops. Two structures appear everywhere and you already met them in the servo deep dive.

Cascade control — a loop inside a loop

An outer (master) loop controls the slow variable you care about; its output is the setpoint of an inner (slave) loop that controls a fast variable. The inner loop crushes disturbances before they reach the outer one. This is exactly the position→velocity→current nest from A09 Servo.

A pressure spike in the steam header is caught by the fast inner flow loop long before it can move the temperature — the outer loop barely notices.

Feed-forward — don't wait for the error

PID is reactive — it only acts after an error appears. Feed-forward measures a known disturbance (or the command itself) and adds a calculated correction before the error happens. PID then only mops up the small leftover. In motion it's torque feed-forward; in a heat exchanger it's measuring inlet flow and pre-positioning the valve. Feed-forward + PID is the high-performance combination.

Drives / VFD (A08)

PI speed & current loops regulate motor speed and torque.

Servo (A09)

Cascaded P-PI-PI position/velocity/current — pure PID inside.

Process (A14)

SCADA/DCS run thousands of temperature, flow & level PIDs.

Page 10 · Engineering Practice

Failures, Real Example & Quick Check

What actually goes wrong on the floor
⚠️ Permanent offset

Pure-P or integral disabled/too slow. Add or speed up integral action (raise Ki / lower Ti).

⚠️ Big overshoot after a step

Integral windup during actuator saturation. Enable clamping or back-calculation anti-windup.

⚠️ Buzzy, jittery output

Derivative amplifying sensor noise. Filter D, take derivative on PV, or drop to PI.

⚠️ Slow rolling oscillation

Gains too high for the dead time, or scan too slow. Lower Kp/Ki; check sample time vs lag.

Real Industrial Example — extrusion temperature control

A plastic extruder barrel must hold each heating zone to ±1 °C. Temperature is slow, clean and full of lag — the textbook home for full PID. A band heater is the actuator (it saturates at 0–100% duty, so anti-windup is mandatory during warm-up), an RTD is the sensor (see A11 Sensors), and the loop runs in a PLC at a fixed scan (A07). The PLC's auto-tune does a relay test on startup, derivative-on-PV stops a kick when the recipe setpoint changes, and feed-forward from screw speed pre-heats the zone before the cold pellets arrive. Every term on this page is doing exactly one job.

Same process, same setpoint step. P (blue) leaves permanent offset; adding I (amber) erases offset but overshoots; adding filtered D (purple) damps it for a fast, clean settle.

Quick Check

A temperature loop holds setpoint perfectly during normal running, but every cold startup it overshoots by 15 °C and takes minutes to settle. The gains are otherwise fine. Name the cause and the fix — and say which term is responsible.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Cause: integral windup. During the long cold start the heater is saturated at 100% while a large error persists, so the integral term accumulates a huge value it can't use. When the barrel finally reaches setpoint the over-charged integrator keeps the heater slammed on, causing the overshoot while it unwinds. Fix: enable anti-windup — clamp (stop integrating) or back-calculate the integrator while the output is saturated. No re-tuning of Kp/Ki needed.
Memory Hook
“P pushes, I erases offset, D damps — clamp the I, filter the D.”
Page 11 · Worked Examples

Worked Examples — Full Solutions

Four exam-grade tuning and analysis problems, solved line by line. Attempt each before revealing your eyes down the page.

WE-1 · Ziegler-Nichols closed-loop (ultimate) tuning

Problem. With I and D off, a temperature loop first sustains constant-amplitude oscillation at Kp = Ku = 4.8, period Pu = 110 s. Compute classic Z-N PID settings, and a conservative alternative.

Classic Z-N (PID): Kp = 0.6·Ku = 2.88 Ti = Pu/2 = 55 s (Ki = Kp/Ti = 0.052 s⁻¹) Td = Pu/8 = 13.8 s (Kd = Kp·Td = 39.7) Some-overshoot variant: Kp = 0.33·Ku = 1.58, Ti = Pu/2, Td = Pu/3 PI only (noisy PV): Kp = 0.45·Ku = 2.16, Ti = Pu/1.2 = 91.7 s

Discussion. Z-N targets quarter-amplitude damping — aggressive, ~25 % overshoot. On processes where overshoot hurts (temperature with overshoot-sensitive product, level near trip points) start from the conservative row, or use IMC (WE-2). Always sanity-check: does Pu ≈ 110 s square with the physics? A loop that oscillates at ~2 min has dead time + lag of tens of seconds — believable for temperature.

WE-2 · Step-test → FOPDT fit → IMC (lambda) tuning

Problem. Open-loop step: output moved 10 %, PV (flow-jacketed reactor temp) eventually rose 8 °C on a 0–100 °C span. PV started moving after 20 s and reached 63.2 % of its final change 150 s after it started moving. Tune a PI controller for a smooth, robust response (λ = 3θ).

Step 1 — FOPDT model: K = Δ%PV/Δ%OUT = (8/100×100%)/10% = 0.8 (dimensionless) θ (dead time) = 20 s , τ (time constant) = 150 s Step 2 — IMC/lambda PI rules: choose λ = 3θ = 60 s Kc = τ / (K·(λ+θ)) = 150/(0.8×80) = 2.34 Ti = min(τ, 4(λ+θ)) → Ti = τ = 150 s

Discussion. λ is your one honest knob: λ = θ is fast/fragile, λ = 3θ is the robust default, λ = 5θ+ for critical loops. Note the Skogestad SIMC alternative gives Kc = τ/(K·2θ)·½ → same order. If the model is poor (τ, θ within ±30 %), IMC with λ ≥ 3θ still behaves — that robustness is why the method owns process industry tuning.

WE-3 · One scan of the discrete velocity-form PID

Problem. Velocity form, Ts = 0.1 s, Kp = 2.0, Ti = 5 s, Td = 0.5 s (derivative on PV, no filter for simplicity). Setpoint 50.0. Samples: PV[k−2] = 47.0, PV[k−1] = 47.6, PV[k] = 48.4. Previous output u[k−1] = 62.0 %. Compute u[k].

e[k] = 50−48.4 = 1.6 , e[k−1] = 50−47.6 = 2.4 Δu_P = Kp·(e[k]−e[k−1]) = 2.0·(1.6−2.4) = −1.60 Δu_I = Kp·(Ts/Ti)·e[k] = 2.0·(0.1/5)·1.6 = +0.064 Δu_D = −Kp·(Td/Ts)·(PV[k]−2PV[k−1]+PV[k−2]) = −2.0·(0.5/0.1)·(48.4−95.2+47.0) = −10·0.2 = −2.00 u[k] = 62.0 −1.60 +0.064 −2.00 = 58.46 %

Discussion. The PV is rising fast toward setpoint, so P and D reduce drive while I still nudges up — exactly the damping behaviour you want approaching target. Velocity form's gift: no integrator register to wind up, and bumpless transfer is free because u[k] builds on the actual previous output.

WE-4 · Proportional-only offset — predict it before you see it

Problem. P-only level loop: process gain K = 1.5 (fraction PV per fraction OUT), Kp = 2.0. After a setpoint step ΔSP = 10 %, what steady-state offset remains? What Kp halves it, and what is the catch?

Closed loop steady state: e_ss = ΔSP/(1+Kp·K) e_ss = 10 %/(1+2.0×1.5) = 10/4 = 2.5 % Halve it → need 1+KpK = 8 → Kp = 7/1.5 = 4.67 Catch: ×2.3 the gain slashes phase/gain margins — oscillation risk. The honest fix for offset is integral action, not gain escalation. (Surge-tank levels often WANT offset: P-only "averaging level control" absorbs flow swings deliberately.)
Page 12 · Problem Set

Problem Set — Test Yourself

Ten problems from definition to design. Paper first, then tap. Target 8/10.

P1 · Units

A vendor manual says "Proportional Band 25 %". What Kp is that, and why do vendors still use PB?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Kp = 100/PB = 4.0. PB is the % of span the error must traverse to swing the output full-scale — intuitive for operators ("narrow band = twitchy"). Also check Ti units: minutes/repeat vs seconds vs repeats/min differ across vendors and have caused real oscillation incidents on "identical" settings.
P2 · Term identification

A loop responds to a setpoint step with a sharp output spike that instantly decays, then a ramp. Which terms produced which feature, and what tells you D is on error rather than PV?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Spike = derivative kick (D differentiating the setpoint step), decay then ramp = P step + I ramp. A kick on setpoint change (not disturbance) is the signature of D-on-error; derivative-on-PV removes it because SP steps don't pass through the PV path.
P3 · Relay autotune

A relay (on-off) test with output amplitude d = 10 % produces PV oscillation amplitude a = 2.5 % at period 80 s. Find Ku and Pu, and Z-N PI settings.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Ku = 4d/(πa) = 40/(π·2.5) = 5.09; Pu = 80 s (read directly). PI: Kp = 0.45·Ku = 2.29, Ti = Pu/1.2 = 66.7 s. This is exactly what a PLC "autotune" button does — a controlled relay experiment plus these formulas.
P4 · Sample time

Process time constant τ = 2 s, dead time 0.3 s. Your PLC task runs PID every 500 ms. Acceptable? State the rule and the failure mode.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Rule of thumb Ts ≤ τ/10 (and ≤ θ/2): need ≤ 0.2 s (and ≤ 0.15 s). 500 ms is too slow — added effective dead time ≈ Ts/2 erodes phase margin; the loop hunts and tuning "won't hold". Move the loop to a 100 ms task or a faster controller.
P5 · Windup arithmetic

Heater saturated at 100 % for 120 s with a constant error of 8 % (Kp = 2, Ti = 30 s, no anti-windup). How much surplus output has the integrator stored, and what does it mean physically?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Integral contribution grows by Kp·(1/Ti)·e·t = 2×(1/30)×8×120 = 64 % of output span beyond what saturation could deliver. After PV reaches setpoint, the loop must "burn off" that 64 % by integrating negative error — i.e. a guaranteed, prolonged overshoot. Clamping the integrator during saturation stores ~0.
P6 · Filtered derivative

Why is pure derivative unusable on a real 4–20 mA signal, and what does the filter constant N ≈ 8–20 in Td/N actually set?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Differentiation multiplies each noise frequency ω by ω — high-frequency sensor noise dominates and slams the actuator. The practical D term is Td·s/(1+Td·s/N): a differentiator that rolls off above N/Td rad/s. N sets the max derivative gain (= Kp·N). Small N = smooth but weak D; large N = crisp but noisy. 10 is the classic default.
P7 · Cascade design

Furnace outlet temperature (slow) cascades to fuel flow (fast). State the two conditions that make cascade worth it, and which loop you tune first.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
(1) Inner variable responds ≥ 3–5× faster than the outer; (2) the major disturbances (fuel pressure swings) hit the inner variable, so the fast loop kills them before the temperature ever sees them. Tune inner first (outer in manual), then the outer treating the closed inner loop as its actuator.
P8 · Feed-forward sizing

Cold feed to a heated tank doubles its flow. Feedback alone reacts only after temperature sags. What signal and what static gain make an ideal feed-forward, and why keep the PID at all?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Measure feed flow (the disturbance); FF gain = (disturbance path gain)/(actuator path gain) — the heater move that exactly cancels the load change, applied instantly. PID stays to trim what FF gets wrong (model error, unmeasured disturbances). FF does the predictable 90 %; feedback guarantees the last 10 %.
P9 · Diagnosis from a trend

A flow loop's PV oscillates continuously at fixed amplitude and period even with the controller in MANUAL. Retune, yes or no — and what is it?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
No retune. Oscillation surviving manual mode is not the controller — it's the process/actuator: classically valve stiction (stick-slip limit cycle) or an upstream interacting loop. Stiction signature: square-ish PV, sawtooth OUT in auto. Fix the valve (positioner/repack); no gain setting cures mechanics.
P10 · Design problem

Specify the full controller for an extruder zone: PV = RTD via 0.5 s-lag transmitter, τ ≈ 180 s, θ ≈ 15 s, heater 0–100 % (saturates on warm-up), setpoint changed per recipe several times a day. Choose: P/PI/PID, D on what, anti-windup, sample time, tuning method, λ.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
Full PID (slow clean thermal PV earns D). D on PV with N≈10 (recipe steps must not kick). Anti-windup mandatory (clamping or back-calculation) for saturated warm-ups. Ts ≈ 1 s (≪ τ/10 = 18 s, and ≤ θ/2). Step-test → FOPDT → IMC: λ = 3θ = 45 s → Kc = 180/(K·60), Ti = 180 s, Td ≈ θ/2 ≈ 7 s. Add setpoint ramping between recipes and you've reproduced page 10's real example from specifications alone.

Key Derivations, Forms & Exam Traps

What an MSc examiner asks you to produce, and the conversions that break real plants.

Derivation 1 · Steady-state offset of a P-only loop
Loop: u = Kp·e , PV = K·u (statics), e = SP − PV e = SP − K·Kp·e ⇒ e(1 + KpK) = SP e_ss = SP/(1 + Kp·K) — never zero for finite Kp ∎ With integral action, u keeps changing while e≠0, so the only equilibrium is e = 0: that one sentence IS the derivation of why I erases offset.
Derivation 2 · Relay test → Ku (describing function sketch)
Relay of amplitude d switching on error sign ≈ square wave into the process. Fundamental of a square wave: (4d/π)·sin(ωt). At sustained oscillation the process attenuates this to PV amplitude a, phase −180°. Equivalent gain of the relay = output fundamental / input amplitude: N(a) = 4d/(π·a) = Ku , oscillation period = Pu ∎ One safe, bounded experiment replaces gain-cranking to instability — why every autotuner uses it (Åström–Hägglund, 1984).
Derivation 3 · Velocity form from the position form
Position (backward Euler): u[k] = Kp·e[k] + Kp(Ts/Ti)·Σe[i] + Kp(Td/Ts)·(e[k]−e[k−1]) Write u[k−1], subtract: Δu[k] = Kp(e[k]−e[k−1]) + Kp(Ts/Ti)e[k] + Kp(Td/Ts)(e[k]−2e[k−1]+e[k−2]) u[k] = u[k−1] + Δu[k] ∎ The Σ register vanishes → windup immunity + bumpless auto/manual transfer, at the cost of needing e[k−2]. Swap e→−PV in the P and D differences for kick-free setpoint handling.
Forms & conversions — the compatibility table

Typing series-form numbers into a parallel-form controller detunes or destabilizes the loop with "the same settings". Identify the vendor's form first — it is on one line of the manual and it is the most skipped line in industry.

The seven exam & plant traps
  • 1Ti units chaos: s vs min vs repeats/min. A Ti of "5" can differ by ×60 between controllers.
  • 2PB vs gain: PB % = 100/Kp — "increase the band" means lower gain.
  • 3D on error vs PV: setpoint kicks come from D (and P) acting on SP steps. Production loops: D always on PV, often P too (2-DOF).
  • 4Direct vs reverse acting: get it backwards and feedback becomes feed-forward into the rail. Check: PV above SP — should OUT rise or fall?
  • 5Z-N ≠ universal: it assumes oscillation is acceptable and targets QAD. Never relay-test a reactor near safety limits without approval.
  • 6Retuning a mechanical problem: stiction, hysteresis, oversized valves and noisy transmitters mimic bad tuning. Manual-mode test first (P9).
  • 7Scan-time creep: moving a tuned loop to a slower task silently adds Ts/2 dead time — the "nothing changed but it oscillates" classic.

  • 3Step-test discipline. Fit K, τ, θ from a step response by hand (63.2 % method), then with the half-rule. Tune via IMC λ = θ, 3θ, 5θ and compare responses.
  • 4Relay experiment. Implement the relay test in the sim; verify Ku = 4d/(πa) against the gain that actually sustains oscillation.
  • 5TCLab or kettle rig. Run a real thermal loop end-to-end: step test → model → IMC tune → disturbance test (blow on the sensor). Log and report like a commissioning engineer.
  • 6PLC autotune audit. On a test rig or simulator PLC, run autotune, then extract the gains and reverse-engineer which rule family it used (compare to Z-N/IMC predictions).
  • 7Cascade build. Add an inner flow loop to your sim (fast lag) with pressure disturbances; demonstrate ≥ 5× disturbance rejection vs single loop.
  • 8Plant loop autopsy. Pick one real oscillating loop at work: manual-mode test, stiction check, form/units check, then (and only then) retune. Write the one-page before/after report.
Self-assessment — you have mastered this module when you can…

Theory

Derive P-only offset, the velocity form, and relay Ku = 4d/(πa); explain each term's job in one sentence each.

Tuning

Take a step test, fit FOPDT, and produce defensible Z-N, IMC(λ) and SIMC settings — and say when each is appropriate.

Implementation

Write discrete PID (both forms) with anti-windup, D-on-PV and a filter; choose Ts from τ and θ.

Plant craft

Diagnose windup, kick, stiction and scan-creep from trends alone; design cascade + feed-forward and tune them in the right order.
