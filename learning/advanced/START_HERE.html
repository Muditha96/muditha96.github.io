READ THIS FIRST — WHY THE MATERIAL FEELS HARD

It is not you, and it is not a reading-speed problem. Three things are happening at once.

  1. The library has no order. A57 Control Systems Theory sits next to A19 Bearings in the index, and nothing warns you that one assumes four other modules and a page of Laplace transforms while the other assumes a mechanical degree you already have. That is what this page fixes.
  2. Three of the tracks are genuinely foreign to a mechanical background — electrical, PLC/instrumentation, and networks. They are not harder than mechanics; they are unfamiliar, which feels identical from the inside. Eight bridge primers are available specifically to close that gap, using analogies you already own: voltage is pressure, current is flow, capacitance is an accumulator, inductance is inertia.
  3. Every module is written at MSc depth on purpose. They are reference documents you will return to for years, not articles you read once. You are not supposed to absorb one in a single sitting, and treating them that way is the fastest route to feeling stupid about material you actually understand.
THE RESCUE PROTOCOL — WHEN A MODULE LOSES YOU

Do not push harder. Run this instead; it takes ten minutes and it works on any module in the library.

  1. Name the gap out loud. Is it a word you do not know, a symbol you cannot read, or a step you cannot follow? Three different problems, three different fixes. Most of the time it is a word, and one search ends it.
  2. Check the prerequisite chips on this page. If the module says “after A30, A55” and you have not read those, the module is not too hard — you are reading it out of order. Go back one link and return.
  3. First pass: read only the CORE blocks. Skip every STRETCH and RESEARCH block entirely. They are marked precisely so you can skip them. Roughly two-thirds of every module is CORE and it stands on its own.
  4. Read page 1 and the recap page, then the middle. The cold open tells you what the whole module is for; the recap tells you what you were supposed to keep. Reading those two first turns the middle from a wall of new material into a set of answers to questions you now have.
  5. Play with the interactive demo before you read the derivation. Move the sliders until the behaviour is obvious — then read why it does that. Intuition first, algebra second. This one habit is worth more than any other on this list.
  6. Always attempt the predict-first card, even when guessing. Being wrong on purpose is the single most effective study technique there is; the explanation lands about three times harder after a wrong guess than after a right one.
  7. If a derivation blocks you, skip it and go to the worked examples. Derivations are the last thing to master, not the first. Come back to page 18 after you have seen the result used twice; it will read completely differently.

The two-pass rule. Every module deserves two passes separated by at least a week: pass one is CORE only, no notes, no stopping — just get to the end. Pass two is everything, slowly, with a pen. Almost nobody does pass one properly, and it is the pass that makes pass two possible.

Your First Twelve Weeks

One focused block per week, roughly two to four hours. It starts on mechanical ground on purpose — weeks 1 and 2 exist to teach you the format while the content is still easy, so that in week 5 the difficulty is the subject rather than the shape of the page.

HOW TO USE THIS PAGE FROM NOW ON
  1. Bookmark this page, not the index. The index is a filing cabinet; this is the syllabus.
  2. Never start a module whose prerequisite chips are unticked. If you want it anyway, read the prerequisite's page 2 first — ten minutes of definitions will save you an hour of confusion.
  3. One module at a time, two passes, a week apart. Finishing six modules properly beats starting twenty.
  4. When a new module appears in the library, it will appear here too, already placed in a stage with its prerequisites filled in.
