One PLC Concept, Many Dialects

Every brand runs the same scan cycle you learned in A07 — what differs is the language around it.

The core truth

A PLC engineer's skill is 90% transferable: scan cycle, I/O, timers, state machines and interlocks are identical everywhere. The remaining 10% — addressing notation, software workflow, and download quirks — is what a "brand" really is.

The bridge between brands is IEC 61131-3: the international standard defining five programming languages (LD, FBD, ST, SFC, IL) and a common software model (programs, function blocks, data types). Every modern vendor claims compliance; every vendor also adds proprietary flavour — which is why this lecture teaches the standard first and the dialects against it.

Why "which brand?" matters anyway
  • 1
    Machines arrive with their PLC chosen. A Japanese knitting machine brings Mitsubishi; a German packing line brings Siemens; a Chinese bag-making machine brings Delta or Xinje. Maintenance engineers don't choose — they meet.
  • 2
    Software is the real lock-in. The CPU is a grey box; the IDE, cables, licences and the program archive are where brands capture you. Losing the only laptop with the licensed software (and the project file) is a plant-stopping event.
  • 3
    Job ads are written in brand names. "GX Works2 essential", "TIA Portal experience", "Studio 5000 preferred" — your CV is scanned for these exact strings, in Colombo and in Dubai alike.

The Siemens mental model: everything is a block — OBs (entry points: OB1 cyclic, OB35 timed, OB82 diagnostics), FCs/FBs (logic, FBs with instance DBs holding their memory), DBs (data). Addressing is %I0.0 / %Q0.0 / %M0.0 but modern practice is fully symbolic with optimized blocks — you name tags and forget addresses. TIA enforces structure that feels bureaucratic on day one and priceless on a 2000-I/O project.

Quirks that cost beginners days: hardware configuration must match reality and be downloaded separately from code; optimized vs standard block access breaks raw addressing assumptions; and project files are version-locked (a V17 project won't open in V16 — archive the installer with the project).

The Mitsubishi mental model: a flat device map with letter prefixes — X inputs, Y outputs (octal-numbered on FX: X0–X7, X10…), M internal relays, D data registers, T/C timers/counters. No mandatory structure — which makes small programs fast and large undisciplined programs unreadable. Ladder is the culture; ST exists but the installed base is 95% rungs.

Quirks: octal I/O numbering (there is no X8!); comments live in the project file, not the CPU — losing the laptop loses the documentation; FX3U programs don't upload into GX Works3 without conversion; and the classic RS-422 round-DIN programming cable (SC-09) is its own little economy in Panchikawatte.

The Omron mental model: memory areas — CIO (I/O + work bits, e.g. 0.00–0.15 per word), W work, H holding (retentive), D data memory, A auxiliary (system flags). Address = word.bit: input 0.05, output 100.03. The word/bit structure makes channel-wise data handling natural, and the differentiate-up/down instructions (DIFU/DIFD) are the local dialect for edges.

Quirks: the CIO word split between real I/O and work bits invites accidental overlap on small CPUs; CX-Programmer's function-key-driven editing (F-keys for contacts/coils) is beloved by veterans and baffling for a day; and the CX-One licence covers a whole suite (HMI, networks, servo) — the SL-relevant point being that one legal copy equips a workshop.

Delta & the Budget Tier — Fatek, Xinje, Wecon

Where most Sri Lankan SME automation actually happens: capable hardware, free software, thin manuals.

Delta — the SME default

Delta's strategy was to be the affordable Mitsubishi: same device letters, similar instructions, half the price, free software. It worked — Delta is everywhere in Sri Lankan SME machinery, retrofits and local machine building. Engineering quality is real (Taiwanese, ISO-certified, decent documentation); the gap versus the Japanese tier is in long-term firmware support, advanced diagnostics and application-engineering depth.

Allen-Bradley, Schneider & the CODESYS World

The corporate spec, the tender spec, and the universal runtime hiding inside a dozen logos.

Allen-Bradley / Rockwell — the corporate standard
hardware: Micro800 (CCW, free) · CompactLogix · ControlLogix software: Studio 5000 (Logix) · Connected Components Workbench (Micro800) model: TAG-based — no addresses at all: Motor.Start, Conveyor[3].Speed network: EtherNet/IP native (CIP) — the US counterpart to PROFINET (→ A02)

AB's Logix platform abandoned memory addresses entirely: everything is a named tag with a data type, programs are organised in tasks/programs/routines, and UDTs (user-defined types) let you build a "Motor" structure once and reuse it plant-wide. In Sri Lanka you meet AB inside multinationals (FMCG, tobacco, beverages) and on imported US-spec lines; in the Gulf job market it is a first-class citizen — worth a simulator-level acquaintance even if no local plant runs it.

Schneider Electric & the CODESYS universe
Schneider: Zelio (smart relay) · M221/M241/M262 (Machine Expert / -Basic) legacy Modicon Quantum/Premium (Unity Pro → EcoStruxure Control Expert) heritage: Modicon INVENTED the PLC (1968) and Modbus (→ A01) CODESYS: one IEC 61131-3 IDE licensed by 400+ brands — ABB AC500 · WAGO · Festo · Eaton · Bosch Rexroth · Delta AS (flavour) free runtime for Raspberry Pi (→ A36) = the cheapest real-PLC lab

Why CODESYS matters strategically: learn it once and you've pre-learned the IDE of dozens of brands — same editor, same ST, same online-change workflow under different paint. Its free soft-PLC on a Raspberry Pi is the single cheapest way in Sri Lanka to practise professional IEC 61131-3 structured programming on real hardware, and OpenPLC (fully open-source) covers the same ground for zero cost. Schneider itself appears in SL through Zelio relays in small panels and Modicon in water/power infrastructure.

Pin this table above your desk: it converts any brand's program you can read into every brand's program you can write. Note the two octal families (Mitsubishi, Delta), the word.bit family (Omron), the byte.bit family (Siemens), and the no-address family (AB).

Start/stop seal-in — Structured Text, three accents
// Siemens SCL "Motor" := ("Start" OR "Motor") AND NOT "Stop" AND NOT "OverloadTrip"; // Mitsubishi ST (GX Works3) Y0 := (X0 OR Y0) AND NOT X1 AND NOT M10; // CODESYS / Omron Sysmac / AB ST — pure IEC Motor := (Start OR Motor) AND NOT Stop AND NOT Overload;

Identical logic, cosmetic differences: Siemens quotes symbolic names, Mitsubishi happily mixes raw devices into ST, the IEC-native platforms look like clean Pascal. In ladder the same rung is: --[Start]--+--[/Stop]--[/OL]--(Motor)-- with a [Motor] branch in parallel with Start — in every brand, forever. The seal-in rung is your passport photo: same face, different passports.

Career Path, Pitfalls & Quiz

Which brand first, which second, and the tool-side mistakes that stop real production lines.

The Sri Lanka learning order — a defensible sequence
  • 1
    Concepts first (A07): scan, I/O, timers, state machines — brand-free. Everything after is dialect.
  • 2
    Mitsubishi GX Works2/3 second: the largest installed base on SL factory floors; a maintenance job magnet. Delta comes almost free with it (same device model).
  • 3
    Siemens TIA third: the plant/process tier at home AND the strongest single keyword for Middle East / EU / Australia job markets. Learn SCL properly here.
  • 4
    Omron fourth: rounds out the Japanese machine world; CX-Programmer fluency reads as "can service anything from Japan".
  • 5
    CODESYS/ST always: in parallel from day one — it future-proofs you across 400 brands and pulls your style toward structured, testable code. AB via CCW/simulator if the Gulf or FMCG multinationals are the target.
Six tool-side failures that stop plants
⚠️ The only project file

Program lives on one retired engineer's laptop; CPU upload has no comments/symbols. Fix: archive project + IDE installer + licence per machine, versioned, off-site copy.

⚠️ Version mismatch

TIA V17 file vs V16 office; GX Works3 vs an FX3U. Downgrade is rarely possible. Record IDE version ON the panel door.

⚠️ Wrong cable/driver day

SC-09 vs USB clones, RS-232 vs USB-serial latency, wrong COM port — hours lost before the first rung. Keep a tested cable kit per brand.

⚠️ Hardware config not downloaded

Siemens/Omron: code downloaded but I/O config stale → modules dark, no error you expect. Download BOTH, verify online-vs-offline equal.

⚠️ Forces left active

Same sin, every brand, different menu. Check the force table first on any "impossible" behaviour (→ A07 traps).

⚠️ Online edit without discipline

Live changes never written back to the master file → the archive lies. One rule: every online change is exported same day.

🧠 Quick Check 1

A Sri Lankan apparel factory's new bag-closing machine arrives from Taiwan. Statistically, which PLC and software will the panel most likely contain?

Tap to reveal Delta DVP/AS programmed in ISPSoft/WPLSoft (or another Taiwanese/Chinese budget brand like Fatek/Xinje). Taiwanese machine builders default to Delta; the free software means the factory can legally open the program — IF the builder shipped the project file. Ask for it at handover, always.
🧠 Quick Check 2

In a Mitsubishi FX program you see X7 followed by X10. Is X8 missing hardware?

Tap to reveal No — FX I/O numbering is octal: X0–X7, then X10–X17. X8 and X9 do not exist by definition. Delta copies this. Miscounting octal I/O during commissioning is the classic first-week Mitsubishi error.
🧠 Quick Check 3

Translate: Omron output 100.03 — what kind of address is this, and what's the Siemens byte.bit equivalent notation style?

Tap to reveal Omron CIO word 100, bit 03 (word.bit in 16-bit channels). Siemens would express an output as %Q12.3 — byte 12, bit 3 (byte.bit in 8-bit groups). Same physical idea, different word sizes in the notation — the page-8 table converts.
🧠 Quick Check 4

Why does Allen-Bradley's Logix platform have no equivalent of %I0.0 at all?

Tap to reveal Logix is fully tag-based: memory is allocated by named, typed tags (BOOL, INT, UDTs), and physical I/O maps to structured tags like Local:1:I.Data.0 — usually aliased to a name. Benefits: self-documenting programs, reusable UDT/AOI structures, no address bookkeeping. Cost: you must design a naming convention — the discipline IS the address map.
🧠 Quick Check 5

You must service: a cement plant DCS-adjacent PLC, a Japanese loom, and a local SME's conveyor retrofit. Name the three software packages you'll most likely open.

Tap to reveal TIA Portal (or STEP 7 classic) for the cement plant's S7; GX Works2/3 for the loom's Mitsubishi FX/Q; ISPSoft/WPLSoft for the SME's Delta. That trio covers the majority of Sri Lankan service calls — which is exactly the page-10 learning order.
🧠 Quick Check 6

A factory asks you to standardise future purchases on one budget brand to save money. What three questions decide if that's wise?

Tap to reveal (1) What does one hour of downtime cost vs the PLC price difference? (2) Is there a competent local agent with stock and firmware support for the next 10 years? (3) Are safety functions involved (budget brands lack SIL/PL-rated options — → A35)? Cheap iron is rational for low-consequence machines with local support; elsewhere the "saving" is borrowed downtime.

Worked Examples

Four field-grade exercises: translation, selection, migration and archive rescue.

Worked Example 1 — Same machine, four dialects

Task: a conveyor with Start (NO), Stop (NC wired, read as true-when-healthy), overload relay, and a 3 s start-delay siren. Write the core logic per brand.

// Siemens SCL (siren TON as IEC block) #SirenTmr(IN := ("Start" OR "Run") AND "StopHealthy" AND "OLHealthy", PT := T#3s); "Siren" := #SirenTmr.IN AND NOT #SirenTmr.Q; // sounds during delay "Run" := #SirenTmr.Q; // Mitsubishi ladder (GX Works2, devices) LD X0 (Start) OR Y1 (Run seal) AND X1 (Stop healthy) AND X2 (OL healthy) → OUT M0 LD M0 → OUT T0 K30 (3.0 s, 100 ms base) LD M0 ANI T0 → OUT Y0 (Siren) LD T0 → OUT Y1 (Run) // Omron (CX-Programmer) LD 0.00 OR 100.01 AND 0.01 AND 0.02 → OUT W0.00 W0.00 → TIM 000 #30 · LD W0.00 ANDNOT T000 → OUT 100.00 (siren) LD T000 → OUT 100.01 (run) // Delta: byte-for-byte the Mitsubishi solution with DVP devices

Discussion: notice what stayed identical — the seal-in shape, the NC-stop philosophy (healthy = TRUE, so a broken wire stops the machine, → A44's fail-safe logic), and the timer gating. What changed: timer syntax (IEC block with PT vs K-constant in 100 ms units vs #BCD), and address spelling. Do this exercise once per new brand and the dialect tax is paid in an afternoon.

Worked Example 2 — Selecting a PLC for a 20-I/O SL machine

Given: a local tea-packing machine: 12 DI, 8 DO, one 4-20 mA load cell input, Modbus link to one VFD, budget-sensitive, one-shift operation. Score the candidates.

criteria (weight): price(3) · local agent/stock(3) · analog+comms fit(2) · free software(2) · downtime cost tolerance(2) Delta DVP-SA2/AS : 3+3+2+2+2 = 12/12 — selected Mitsubishi FX5U : 1+3+2+0(licence)+2 = 8 — better iron, over budget Siemens S7-1200 : 1+2+2+0+2 = 7 — overkill tier for one-shift packing Xinje XD : 3+2+1(verify analog)+2+1 = 9 — viable, thinner support

Discussion: the decision is dominated by agent + price, not CPU features — every candidate executes 20 I/O of ladder identically. The load-cell channel and Modbus port are the only technical differentiators worth checking datasheet-deep (resolution, isolated RS-485). Had this been a three-shift export line, the downtime-cost row would flip the answer to FX5U or S7-1200: same matrix, different weights — that is the method to show an interviewer.

Worked Example 3 — Migration: FX2N fleet to FX5U

Given: a factory with nine FX2N machines, spares drying up. Plan the migration.

1 · UPLOAD & ARCHIVE every FX2N program (GX Works2) + verify vs running CPU 2 · CONVERT: GX Works2 project → GX Works3 conversion path (FX3U step if needed) 3 · REMAP: octal I/O carries over; check SPECIALS — D8000-series system regs, high-speed counter instructions, comms (RS instruction → predefined protocol) 4 · REWIRE: FX5U terminal layout differs; 24V sink/source check per card 5 · PILOT one machine · run a scan-time + function test script · then fleet

Discussion: the dangerous 20% is never the ladder — it is system devices, comms instructions and analog scaling that changed generation-to-generation. The professional habit: build a migration test checklist from the OLD machine's behaviour (what does each mode do, what are the alarm paths), not from the code — the code may contain dead logic nobody remembers. Same skeleton applies to S7-300→S7-1500 (rewrite in TIA, absolute→symbolic) or CP1→NX (CX→Sysmac).

Worked Example 4 — The archive-rescue audit

Given: you inherit maintenance of 30 machines, mixed brands, no documentation culture. Design the software-asset rescue.

per machine, one row: brand/CPU · IDE + VERSION · cable type · licence status · project file? · comments in file or CPU? · upload possible? · backup date triage: RED no file + upload loses comments (Mitsubishi/Delta/Omron typical) AMBER file exists, wrong IDE version, licence unclear GREEN archived project + installer + cable, verified this year

Discussion: reds get scheduled downtime for a verified upload NOW, while the machine still runs and the old technician still answers calls — comments are re-created by interview, which is expensive but cheapest today. The deliverable is a fireproof (and cloud) archive: project + IDE installer + licence record + cable photo per machine. This audit, done unasked in your first month, is the highest-visibility move a young SL maintenance engineer can make.

Problem Set — 10 Questions

Translation drills, selection calls, and rescue scenarios — the daily diet of a multi-brand engineer.

Problem 1

Translate to Siemens notation: Mitsubishi X12 (input) and Y7 (output).

Tap to reveal X12 octal = decimal input #10 → %I1.2 (byte 1, bit 2). Y7 = output #7 → %Q0.7. The octal→byte.bit conversion (8 per group in both, conveniently) is: octal digit pair maps to byte.bit directly for the first two bytes.
Problem 2

A Mitsubishi T0 K50 with default 100 ms base equals what IEC timer preset?

Tap to reveal 50 × 100 ms = T#5s. The K-constant-times-base convention (with 10 ms and 1 ms timer ranges too) vs IEC's explicit PT := T#5s is a classic translation slip — a K50 copied as "50 s" makes a machine six times slower to react.
Problem 3

Why do Delta skills transfer to Mitsubishi almost line-for-line, but not to Omron?

Tap to reveal Delta deliberately cloned the Mitsubishi device model — X/Y octal, M/D/T/C, similar instruction mnemonics — so programs map almost symbol-for-symbol. Omron uses a different architecture: CIO word.bit areas, TIM/CNT syntax, DIFU edges — same concepts, different grammar family.
Problem 4

A plant's S7-300 program exists only inside the CPU. What can you recover by upload, and what is lost?

Tap to reveal Upload recovers the executable logic (blocks, instructions, addresses). Lost forever unless archived: symbolic names, comments, and network titles — on classic S7 they live in the offline project. A 5000-rung program without symbols is technically present and practically unreadable; budget weeks of re-documentation.
Problem 5

Which two ecosystems give a Sri Lankan student a complete legal toolchain for LKR 0, and what does each teach best?

Tap to reveal Delta (ISPSoft/WPLSoft + simulator) — teaches the Mitsubishi-family device world that dominates SL machines. CODESYS (free IDE + Pi runtime) — teaches professional IEC 61131-3 structure and ST. (OpenPLC and AB's CCW are honourable free mentions.)
Problem 6

An Omron CP1E program uses W0.00–W0.15 and also CIO 0.00 inputs. A colleague adds an output card mapped to CIO 0. What happens?

Tap to reveal Address collision risk: on small CPUs CIO words are shared between physical I/O mapping and work bits; remapping hardware onto a word the program already uses as work bits corrupts logic silently. Fix: keep work bits in the W area (that's what it's for), audit the I/O table after any hardware change.
Problem 7

Same seal-in rung; write it in AB Structured Text with tags, including an alarm-horn ONS (one-shot).

Tap to reveal Motor := (Start OR Motor) AND NOT Stop AND NOT Overload; plus one-shot: HornPulse := Motor AND NOT MotorPrev; MotorPrev := Motor; — the manual edge pattern (or use OSR/ONS in ladder). Note: zero addresses anywhere; tag names carry the meaning.
Problem 8

Your factory's German line PLC shows TIA V16; your laptop has V18. Options?

Tap to reveal TIA opens older projects by upgrading them — but then the file no longer matches the plant's V16 baseline, and downgrading is not supported. Correct practice: install V16 alongside V18 (TIA versions coexist), work in the machine's native version, and only upgrade as a planned, tested, documented event for the whole machine — never casually on a service call.
Problem 9

Rank for a Gulf-region automation job application: GX Works, TIA Portal, Studio 5000 — and justify in one line each.

Tap to reveal TIA Portal first — Siemens dominates Middle East infrastructure/process tenders. Studio 5000 second — US-spec oil & gas and FMCG sites. GX Works third — valued in packaging/machinery but a narrower keyword there. (For a SL-domestic career the order roughly reverses — market decides, not preference.)
Problem 10

A budget-brand PLC controls a machine with a light curtain. The vendor proposes wiring the curtain into a normal input "handled in software". Verdict?

Tap to reveal Reject. Safety functions require rated hardware: a safety relay or safety PLC/PLr-assessed circuit (→ A35), not standard I/O on any brand — and budget brands offer no safety-rated options at all. Standard PLC scan + non-redundant input ≠ PL c/d/e. The curtain wires through a safety relay that removes power; the PLC merely gets told about it.

Deep Structure, Glossary & Traps

The three structural ideas under every IDE — and the traps that cross brands with you.

Structure 1 — three memory philosophies
FLAT DEVICE MAP (Mitsubishi, Delta, Omron-ish) fixed letter areas: X/Y/M/D — simple, fast, discipline is on YOU BLOCK-STRUCTURED (Siemens) OB/FB/FC/DB — data belongs to blocks; instance DBs give FBs private memory TAG-BASED (Allen-Bradley, CODESYS/Sysmac direction) named typed tags + UDTs/structs — the compiler owns the map

Why it matters: the philosophy predicts how programs rot. Flat maps rot by address collisions and mystery M-bits; block systems rot by copy-pasted FCs with absolute addressing; tag systems rot by naming anarchy. Reading a brand's memory philosophy tells you what discipline to import on day one.

Structure 2 — where the truth lives (project vs CPU)
CPU always holds: compiled logic (uploadable) project file may hold ALONE: symbols · comments · UDT definitions · HMI tags · network config documentation brand reality: Siemens classic & Mitsubishi & Delta → comments in FILE TIA (optimized) & AB Logix → much more stored IN the CPU

Why it matters: this single fact drives the archive discipline of pages 10–11. Knowing per-brand what an upload can and cannot recover is the difference between a 2-hour and a 2-month recovery — ask it as your first question at any new plant.

Structure 3 — the online/offline contract
every IDE maintains TWO programs: offline (file) and online (CPU) operations: download (file→CPU) · upload (CPU→file) · compare · online edit the invariant to enforce: compare = EQUAL at the end of every visit

Why it matters: nearly every "the machine doesn't match the drawing" disaster is a violated online/offline contract. Brands differ in edit granularity (AB online edits per rung; Siemens per block; Mitsubishi write-during-RUN with caveats) but the professional invariant is brand-free: leave with a verified-equal archive or you left a trap for the next engineer.

Seven cross-brand traps
  • 1
    Octal I/O (Mitsubishi/Delta): X8/Y8 don't exist; wiring lists written in decimal shift every terminal after the eighth.
  • 2
    Timer bases: K50 means 5 s or 0.5 s depending on the timer's base — check the range, not the constant. IEC's T#5s exists precisely to kill this trap.
  • 3
    Retentivity defaults differ: AB tags retain by default; Siemens M-bits don't unless configured; Mitsubishi has latched ranges. After a power cycle, "same program" behaves differently per brand.
  • 4
    NC stop philosophy survives translation — verify it did. Copying logic between brands while flipping input polarity (healthy=1 vs pressed=1) silently deletes fail-safety (→ A44).
  • 5
    Uploads lie by omission: logic yes, meaning maybe. Know per brand whether comments/symbols live in the CPU before promising a recovery.
  • 6
    Trial-licence cliffs: TIA/GX trials expire mid-commissioning if you let them. Track licence dates like calibration dates.
  • 7
    USB-serial adapter roulette: cloned cables and wrong drivers cause "cannot connect" that looks like a dead CPU. Keep one KNOWN-GOOD cable kit per brand, labelled, hoarded.
