What a Control System Actually Is

Two ideas separate an amateur from an engineer: the loop is a dynamic system, and feedback buys accuracy at the price of potential instability.

A control system makes a physical quantity — temperature, position, flow, speed, pressure — follow a desired value despite disturbances and despite imperfect knowledge of the plant. Everything in this module exists to answer two questions quantitatively: how fast and how accurately will it respond? and will it stay stable while doing so?

Open Loop vs Closed Loop

Open loop — no measurement

The controller commands the actuator and trusts the result. A toaster, a stepper motor without an encoder, a fixed-speed pump. Cheap, always stable, but accuracy depends entirely on the model and it cannot reject a disturbance it never sees.

Closed loop — measure and correct

The output is measured, compared with the setpoint, and the error drives the controller. Accuracy no longer depends on knowing the plant precisely, and disturbances are automatically opposed — but the loop can now oscillate or go unstable.

The bargain, stated exactly:
Feedback trades a known model for a stability problem.
Open loop cannot become unstable and cannot correct anything. Closed loop corrects everything and can destroy the machine. The entire discipline is about buying the first benefit without paying the second cost.

Two Different Jobs: Servo vs Regulator

Servo (tracking) problem

The setpoint moves and the output must follow it: robot axis, CNC contour, batch temperature ramp, motion profile. Performance is judged by rise time, overshoot, following error.

Regulator (disturbance-rejection) problem

The setpoint is essentially constant and the job is to reject load changes: level in a surge tank, furnace temperature, header pressure. Performance is judged by peak deviation and recovery time after a load step.

  • !
    A tuning that is excellent for one can be poor for the other. The two use different transfer functions (y/r versus y/d), so they have different optimal controller settings. This is exactly why modern PID implementations offer setpoint weighting (β on the proportional term, two-degree-of-freedom control): it lets you tune for disturbance rejection and then soften the setpoint response separately. Always state which problem you are solving before you tune.
The Vocabulary of Performance

Speed

Rise time tr, peak time tp, settling time ts (usually to ±2%), bandwidth ωB. Faster always costs stability margin or actuator effort.

Accuracy

Steady-state error ess — offset that remains forever. Set by system type and loop gain (Page 8). Integral action drives it to zero for steps.

Robustness

Gain margin, phase margin, sensitivity peak. How much can the plant change before the loop becomes unstable? Real plants always change.

Integral performance indices used to compare tunings objectively:
IAE = ∫|e(t)|dt  ·  ISE = ∫e²(t)dt  ·  ITAE = ∫t·|e(t)|dt
ISE punishes large early errors; ITAE punishes long tails and gives well-damped, professional-looking responses; IAE is the usual practical compromise. Quoting one of these turns "it looks better" into an engineering claim.
Quick check: A colleague says "we don't need feedback — the VFD holds speed accurately from its frequency reference." Under what conditions is he right, and what breaks his claim?
Tap to reveal ↓A: He is right in the sense that a VFD in open-loop V/f mode sets synchronous speed accurately, and for a fan or centrifugal pump that is usually good enough. What breaks the claim is slip: an induction motor's shaft speed falls below synchronous by the slip, which grows with load — typically 1–3% at full load, more when hot. So the shaft speed varies with torque, which is a disturbance the open loop cannot see. Practical consequences: (1) for speed accuracy better than a few percent, you need either sensorless vector control (which estimates the slip — a model-based correction, still open loop on position) or a real encoder closing the loop; (2) for position or synchronisation between axes, open loop is hopeless because speed error integrates into unbounded position error; (3) for a conveyor that must hold a rate regardless of load, the disturbance-rejection argument alone justifies closing the loop. That is the servo-versus-regulator distinction in one practical example — and note the cost of closing it: now the loop can hunt and oscillate, so it must be tuned (A08, A09).

Modelling & the Laplace Transform

Why every control engineer works in the s-domain: it converts calculus into algebra, and differential equations into ratios of polynomials.

Physical systems obey differential equations. Solving them repeatedly for every input is miserable. The Laplace transform maps a function of time into a function of a complex variable s, and in doing so turns differentiation into multiplication by s. Convolution becomes multiplication; a differential equation becomes a polynomial ratio called the transfer function, and the whole of classical control follows from manipulating it.

Definition and the Properties That Matter
F(s) = ℒ{f(t)} = ∫0∞ f(t)·e−st dt   where s = σ + jω (complex frequency)

The three properties that do all the work:

ℒ{f(t−θ)} = e−θs·F(s)  — pure time delay → multiply by e−θs (the one transcendental term in the subject)

  • !
    Read the table backwards. That is the real skill. A pole at s = −a means a decaying exponential e−at; a complex pair at −σ ± jω means a decaying sinusoid; a pole at s = 0 means an integrator (a ramp response); a pole with positive real part means a growing exponential — instability. Pole locations are response shapes.
From Physics to Transfer Function — Three Worked Models
① Stirred tank (thermal, first order) Energy balance: m·cp·dT/dt = Q_in − U·A·(T − T_amb) Rearrange: τ·dT/dt + T = K·Q_in + T_amb with τ = m·cp/(UA), K = 1/(UA) Transform (deviation variables, zero initial condition): G(s) = T(s)/Q(s) = K / (τs + 1) ② Mass–spring–damper (mechanical, second order) Newton: m·ẍ + c·ẋ + k·x = F(t) Transform: (m s² + c s + k)·X(s) = F(s) G(s) = X(s)/F(s) = 1 / (m s² + c s + k) Standard form: G(s) = (1/k) · ωn² / (s² + 2ζωns + ωn²) with ωn = √(k/m) and ζ = c / (2√(km)) ③ DC motor (electromechanical, coupled) Electrical: L·di/dt + R·i = V − Ke·ω Mechanical: J·dω/dt + b·ω = Kt·i Transform & eliminate i: ω(s)/V(s) = Kt / [(Ls+R)(Js+b) + KtKe] Since electrical τe = L/R ≪ mechanical τm = J/b, set L ≈ 0: ω(s)/V(s) ≈ Km / (τm' s + 1) — the motor looks FIRST order with Km = Kt/(Rb + KtKe) and τm' = RJ/(Rb + KtKe)

Notice the pattern. The order of the transfer function equals the number of independent energy-storage elements (thermal mass, spring + mass, inductance + inertia). This is the single most useful modelling heuristic in engineering: count the places energy can be stored, and you know the order of the dynamics before you write a single equation. Note too the back-EMF term KtKe in ③ — it appears in the denominator as extra damping, which is why a DC motor is naturally self-stabilising in speed (A55).

Anatomy of a Transfer Function
G(s) = Y(s)/U(s) = N(s)/D(s) = K·(s − z1)(s − z2)… / (s − p1)(s − p2)…

Poles pi: roots of the denominator → the natural modes, i.e. how the system moves when left alone. They set stability and response shape.
Zeros zi: roots of the numerator → frequencies the system blocks. They shape the response but not the stability.
Order n = degree of D(s)  ·  Relative degree = n − m (poles minus zeros): a physical system always has n ≥ m (it cannot respond instantaneously).

DC (steady-state) gain = G(0) = K·(−z1)(−z2)… / (−p1)(−p2)… — what the output settles to per unit step input.
Final Value Theorem:   y(∞) = lims→0 s·Y(s)   (valid only if the system is stable)
Initial Value Theorem:   y(0+) = lims→∞ s·Y(s)
The final value theorem is how every steady-state-error result on Page 8 is obtained. The caveat in italics is the one examiners test: applying it to an unstable system produces a finite, confident, and completely wrong answer.
Linearisation — the Assumption Behind Everything

Laplace transforms and transfer functions require linear, time-invariant (LTI) systems. Real plants are neither. We rescue the theory by working in deviation variables about an operating point:

For a nonlinear system ẏ = f(y, u), expand as a Taylor series about (ȳ, ū) and keep first-order terms:
ẏ' ≈ (∂f/∂y)|op·y' + (∂f/∂u)|op·u'  where y' = y − ȳ, u' = u − ū

Classic example — flow through a valve or orifice: F = Cv·√(ΔP)
Linearised gain: dF/dΔP = Cv/(2√ΔP) → the gain halves as ΔP quadruples
  • 1
    Process gain is not a constant. The square-root valve above means a loop tuned at low flow is twice as aggressive at four times the flow — a classic cause of loops that are stable at one production rate and oscillate at another. Cures: square-root extraction in the transmitter, equal-percentage valve trim to linearise the installed characteristic (see the Control Valves module), or gain scheduling.
  • 2
    Always state the operating point with any model. "K = 2.4 °C per % valve, τ = 90 s, θ = 20 s at 60% load" is a model; without the last clause it is a rumour.
Quick check: A system has transfer function G(s) = 10(s+2)/((s+1)(s+20)). Without solving anything, state its DC gain, its order, which pole dominates, and roughly how long a step response takes to settle.
Tap to reveal ↓A: DC gain = G(0) = 10 × 2/(1 × 20) = 1.0 — the output settles at the same value as the input step. Order = 2 (two poles), with one zero, so relative degree 1. Poles at s = −1 and s = −20, corresponding to time constants 1 s and 0.05 s: the pole at −1 is 20× slower and therefore dominant, so the response looks essentially first order with τ ≈ 1 s. Settling: a first-order system reaches 98% in about 4τ, so ≈ 4 seconds. The fast pole only affects the first fraction of a second. The zero at s = −2 sits between the poles and lifts the initial slope — it makes the response a little faster than a pure τ = 1 s exponential, but does not change stability. The professional habit shown here: reducing a high-order model to its dominant pole (plus dead time for the rest) is exactly how a plant with a dozen poles becomes the FOPDT model that PID tuning rules need — Page 4.

Block-Diagram Algebra

Four reduction rules collapse any loop to a single transfer function — and the result explains why feedback makes a system faster, more accurate, and less sensitive.

The Four Rules
① Series (cascade): Gtotal = G1·G2 ② Parallel: Gtotal = G1 ± G2 ③ Negative feedback: Gtotal = G / (1 + G·H) ← the master formula ④ Positive feedback: Gtotal = G / (1 − G·H) ← denominator can hit zero: instability Unity feedback (H = 1) special case: T(s) = G / (1 + G)
Deriving the Master Formula (do this once, from memory, forever)
Let the error be e = r − H·y and the output y = G·e. Substitute: y = G·(r − H·y) y = G·r − G·H·y y + G·H·y = G·r y·(1 + G·H) = G·r T(s) = y/r = G / (1 + G·H) ∎ The quantity L(s) = G(s)·H(s) is the LOOP GAIN (open-loop transfer function). Everything in classical control is expressed in terms of L(s): T(s) = L/(1+L)/H closed-loop (complementary sensitivity) S(s) = 1/(1+L) SENSITIVITY function — error and disturbance path and identically S + T = 1 (with H = 1)

That last identity, S + T = 1, is one of the deepest statements in the subject. It says you cannot make both small: wherever you achieve good tracking (T ≈ 1) you have poor sensitivity suppression, and vice versa. You can only choose the frequency ranges in which each dominates — good tracking at low frequency, good noise rejection at high frequency, and a crossover in between where the compromise lives. That crossover region is precisely where gain and phase margin are measured (Page 11).

What Feedback Buys You — Three Quantitative Results
① Accuracy: gain error is divided by (1 + L) For unity feedback with proportional gain K and plant gain Kp: T(0) = K·Kp / (1 + K·Kp) If K·Kp = 99 → T(0) = 0.99 (1% offset). If K·Kp = 999 → 0.1% offset. Steady-state error ≈ 1/(1 + loop gain) ② Sensitivity: plant variation is divided by (1 + L) dT/T = [1/(1 + L)] · (dG/G) A 50% change in plant gain, with L = 20, becomes a 50/21 ≈ 2.4% change in closed-loop gain. THIS is why feedback control works on plants we barely understand. ③ Speed: closed-loop bandwidth is multiplied For a first-order plant G = Kp/(τs+1) under proportional control K: T(s) = K·Kp / (τs + 1 + K·Kp) = [K·Kp/(1+K·Kp)] / [ (τ/(1+K·Kp))·s + 1 ] τ_closed = τ / (1 + K·Kp) → a gain of 9 makes the loop TEN TIMES faster And the DC gain approaches 1 as K rises — accuracy and speed improve together.
  • !
    Then why not use infinite gain? Because result ③ is only true for a pure first-order plant with no dead time. Add a second pole, or any dead time, and high gain produces phase lag beyond −180° at the crossover frequency, which turns negative feedback into positive feedback and the loop oscillates. Every limit in this module is ultimately a limit on how much phase lag the loop accumulates before its gain falls below unity. Hold that sentence; it is the thesis of the whole subject.
Disturbance Rejection — a Different Transfer Function
With the disturbance entering at the plant input (see the Page 1 diagram): y = G·(u + d), u = C·(r − y) y/r = C·G / (1 + C·G) ← setpoint tracking (servo problem) y/d = G / (1 + C·G) ← disturbance rejection (regulator problem) Note the numerators DIFFER. Both denominators are 1 + C·G, so both share the same poles and the same stability — but their zeros and gains differ, so the BEST TUNING FOR ONE IS NOT THE BEST FOR THE OTHER.

This is why the same PID that gives a crisp, non-overshooting setpoint response may recover sluggishly from a load change. Practical resolutions, in order of preference: setpoint weighting / two-degree-of-freedom PID (tune the feedback for disturbances, then filter or weight the setpoint path for tracking); setpoint ramping instead of steps; and feedforward for measurable disturbances, which attacks d before the error even appears — the subject of the Advanced Process Control module.

Quick check: A plant has gain that drifts between 1.5 and 4.5 as the catalyst ages (a 3:1 variation). Under proportional control with K = 8, how much does the closed-loop DC gain change? What does the answer tell you about why feedback is worth its trouble?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Closed-loop DC gain T(0) = K·Kp/(1 + K·Kp). At Kp = 1.5: L = 12, T = 12/13 = 0.923. At Kp = 4.5: L = 36, T = 36/37 = 0.973. So a 3:1 (200%) change in the plant produces a 5.4% change in closed-loop gain — the sensitivity function 1/(1+L) has attenuated the variation by a factor of roughly 13 to 37. That is result ② in action, and it is the single most important practical argument for feedback: you do not need an accurate model to get accurate control. Two professional caveats, though. (1) The stability margins are not equally forgiving — the same 3:1 gain swing eats directly into gain margin, so a loop tuned comfortably at Kp = 1.5 may be marginal at 4.5. Tune at the highest expected process gain and you will be safe (if sluggish) at the lowest. (2) The steady-state offset (7.7% falling to 2.7%) never vanishes under pure proportional control — that is what integral action is for (Page 8).

First-Order Systems & FOPDT

Three numbers — gain, time constant, dead time — describe most industrial processes well enough to tune them. Learning to extract them from a chart recording is the most valuable practical skill in this module.

The Pure First-Order System
G(s) = K / (τs + 1)   one pole at s = −1/τ, DC gain K Unit step response: y(t) = K·(1 − e−t/τ) t = τ → 63.2% of final value ← the DEFINITION of τ t = 2τ → 86.5% t = 3τ → 95.0% t = 4τ → 98.2% ← practical "settled" t = 5τ → 99.3% Initial slope: dy/dt|0 = K/τ → if it kept that slope it would arrive at exactly t = τ

Gain K

Δy/Δu

Steady-state change in PV per unit change in MV. Units matter: °C per % valve. This is the number that decides controller gain.

Time constant τ

63.2%

How much energy storage divided by how fast it drains. Sets the speed of response — and is tunable-around.

Dead time θ

e−θs

Pure transport delay before anything happens. The enemy — it cannot be tuned around, only compensated or designed out.

FOPDT — the Workhorse Industrial Model
G(s) = K·e−θs / (τs + 1)   — First Order Plus Dead Time Why it dominates process control: · A real plant has many poles; the slowest dominates and the rest LOOK like dead time when lumped together. · Genuine transport delay (pipe runs, conveyors, thick walls, analysers) adds directly to θ. · Every classical tuning rule (Ziegler–Nichols, Cohen–Coon, AMIGO, SIMC/lambda) takes exactly K, τ, θ as its input. The single most important ratio in process control: θ/τ — the controllability ratio θ/τ < 0.1 very easy loop; aggressive tuning fine; almost any PI works θ/τ ≈ 0.2–0.5 normal; classic PID territory θ/τ ≈ 1 difficult; expect slow, conservative tuning θ/τ > 2 very hard; consider feedforward, a Smith predictor, model-predictive control, or RE-ENGINEER the measurement location
  • 1
    Dead time is the fundamental limit on loop speed. No controller can react to something it has not yet measured. A loop's achievable closed-loop time constant can never be much faster than θ, no matter how clever the algorithm. Halving θ is worth more than any amount of retuning.
  • 2
    So attack θ physically first. Move the sensor closer, use a thinner thermowell (or one with heat-transfer paste), shorten sample lines to analysers, increase circulation, reduce transmitter damping, and raise the scan/sample rate. These are usually cheap and always permanent.
Extracting K, τ and θ from a Real Step Test
PROCEDURE (open-loop step / "bump" test) 1. Put the controller in MANUAL and let the process settle. 2. Step the MV by a known amount Δu — big enough to see clearly above noise (typically 5–10%), small enough to stay in the linear region and not upset production. 3. Record the PV until it fully settles. Note the final change Δy. 4. K = Δy / Δu (state the units!) 5. θ = time from the step until the PV first genuinely moves. 6. τ: two standard methods — (a) TANGENT method: draw the tangent at the point of steepest slope; θ is where it crosses the initial value, τ is from there to where it crosses the final value. (Classic, sensitive to noise.) (b) TWO-POINT (Smith) method — preferred: t₂₈ = time to reach 28.3% of Δy, t₆₃ = time to reach 63.2% τ = 1.5·(t₆₃ − t₂₈) θ = t₆₃ − τ 7. Repeat in the opposite direction and at another load — the gains should be similar. If they are not, the process is NONLINEAR and you must decide which operating point to tune for.

Integrating (Non-Self-Regulating) Processes
G(s) = Ki·e−θs / s  — level in a tank with a fixed-speed outlet pump, position from a velocity command, pressure in a closed vessel, batch accumulation. Step response is a RAMP, not a curve: the PV never settles by itself. · There is no τ and no steady-state gain — only an INTEGRATING RATE Ki = (rate of change of PV) / (change in MV), e.g. %/min per % valve. · Test by stepping the MV and measuring the SLOPE, not the final value. · These loops need little or no integral action — the process already integrates. Too much reset on a level loop is the classic cause of slow, large-amplitude cycling. · Level loops are often deliberately tuned LOOSE ("averaging level control") so the tank absorbs flow surges instead of passing them downstream.
  • !
    Diagnostic: put the controller in manual, make a small MV step, and watch. If the PV settles at a new value → self-regulating, use FOPDT. If it ramps away indefinitely → integrating, use the rate model and re-think the integral term. Getting this classification wrong is the most common reason a "correctly tuned" level loop cycles forever.
Quick check: A step test gives Δu = 10%, Δy = 24 °C, t₂₈ = 70 s and t₆₃ = 130 s. Find K, τ, θ and the controllability ratio. Is this a comfortable loop?
Tap to reveal ↓A: K = Δy/Δu = 24/10 = 2.4 °C per % valve. τ = 1.5(t₆₃ − t₂₈) = 1.5(130 − 70) = 1.5 × 60 = 90 s. θ = t₆₃ − τ = 130 − 90 = 40 s. Controllability ratio θ/τ = 40/90 = 0.44 — squarely in normal PID territory, so a conventional PI/PID will work, but it is not a fast loop: with λ = θ tuning, the achievable closed-loop time constant is around 40 s and full settling takes a couple of minutes. Interpretation and next steps: (1) the loop is tunable but dead-time-limited, so before spending time on clever tuning, ask why there are 40 s of dead time — a heavy thermowell, a long transport line, or excessive transmitter damping are all fixable and would improve the loop more than any retune; (2) verify linearity by repeating the test downward and at another load, since a temperature loop with a valve often shows gain changing with flow; (3) with K = 2.4, controller gain will be modest — remember Kc is roughly inversely proportional to process gain, so if K later doubles at high load, the loop becomes twice as aggressive and may oscillate. Tune at the highest-gain condition.

Second-Order Systems

Two numbers — ζ and ωn — predict overshoot, ringing and settling time exactly. Every closed loop you tune ends up here.

A first-order system cannot overshoot. Add a second energy store and it can: the system now has momentum, so it can pass its target and come back. Because a well-tuned closed loop is normally dominated by a complex pole pair, the second-order model is the standard language for specifying performance — "10% overshoot, settle in 2 seconds" is a statement about ζ and ωn.

Poles, Zeros & Dead Time

Poles decide stability. Zeros decide surprises — including the response that goes the wrong way first.

Dominant Poles & Model Reduction
A pole is DOMINANT if its real part is much closer to the origin than the others. Rule of thumb: if |Re(p_fast)| > 5·|Re(p_slow)|, the fast pole contributes little and can be neglected (or lumped into dead time). G(s) = 100 / ((s+1)(s+20)) ≈ 5 / (s+1) (matched DC gain) poles at −1 and −20 → τ = 1 s and 0.05 s → keep the slow one WHY THIS IS LEGITIMATE: the fast mode decays in 0.25 s while the slow mode is still barely moving. It affects only the very start of the response — which is precisely the part that LOOKS like dead time on a trend.

This is the theoretical justification for the FOPDT model of Page 4, and for a practical habit: a plant with eight poles behaves like one pole plus dead time, and that is why three numbers are enough to tune most loops. The corollary is a warning — model reduction is valid for the low-frequency behaviour that dominates the step response, but the neglected fast poles still contribute phase lag near the crossover frequency, which is where stability is decided. That is why a loop tuned from an FOPDT fit can still be less robust than the fit suggests, and why margins (Page 11) must be checked against the full model when one is available.

Dead Time: the Transcendental Nuisance
Pure delay: G(s) = e−θs Magnitude: |e−jωθ| = 1 at every frequency (it removes no amplitude) Phase: ∠e−jωθ = −ωθ radians = −57.3·ωθ degrees (unbounded lag!) THIS is why dead time is lethal: it contributes phase lag that grows WITHOUT LIMIT as frequency rises, while doing nothing to reduce gain. Stability needs the loop gain below 1 by the time the phase reaches −180°. Dead time races the phase to −180° for free. Phase lag from dead time alone reaches −180° at ω = π/θ. RATIONAL APPROXIMATIONS (needed for root locus / pole-based methods): First-order Padé: e−θs ≈ (1 − θs/2) / (1 + θs/2) → note the RHP ZERO at s = +2/θ: dead time BEHAVES like a non-minimum-phase element. Same speed limit, same cause. Taylor (crude): e−θs ≈ 1/(1 + θs) — understates the damage Higher-order Padé for accuracy near crossover.

Dead time (θ)

Phase lag −ωθ, no gain reduction. Sources: transport in pipes/conveyors, analyser cycle time, thick sensor walls, controller scan time, communication latency, PWM/actuator quantisation.

Lag (τ)

Phase lag approaching −90° maximum per pole, with gain reduction of −20 dB/decade. Far more forgiving: the gain falls as the phase turns, which is exactly what stability wants.

Learn to distinguish them on a trend. Dead time is the flat portion where nothing happens; lag is the curved portion. Confusing them makes the process look far more controllable than it is, and produces tuning that oscillates.

Quick check: A pH loop has an analyser that reports every 30 s and a sample line adding 20 s of transport. The tank time constant is 40 s. Why will aggressive tuning fail, and what is the frequency at which the loop's phase lag reaches −180° from dead time alone?

Stability & Routh–Hurwitz

One question, three tools. The question: do all roots of 1 + L(s) = 0 lie in the left half plane? Routh–Hurwitz answers it without finding the roots.

What Stability Means
BIBO stability: every bounded input produces a bounded output. For an LTI system this is equivalent to: ALL poles of the closed-loop transfer function have Re(s) < 0. Re(s) < 0 → eσt decays → STABLE Re(s) = 0 → constant amplitude → MARGINALLY stable (oscillator) Re(s) > 0 → eσt grows → UNSTABLE Closed-loop poles are the roots of the CHARACTERISTIC EQUATION: 1 + C(s)·G(s)·H(s) = 0 ⇔ D(s) + N(s) = 0 after clearing fractions NOTE: open-loop poles do NOT decide closed-loop stability. An unstable plant can be stabilised (an inverted pendulum, an exothermic reactor), and a perfectly stable plant can be destabilised by too much gain. Stability is a property of the LOOP, not of the plant.
Necessary Conditions (the 5-second screen)
For the polynomial ansn + an−1sn−1 + … + a1s + a0 = 0 to have all roots in the LHP: ① All coefficients must be present (no missing power of s), and ② All coefficients must have the SAME SIGN. If either fails → definitely unstable (or marginal). If both hold → not yet proven stable; for n ≥ 3 you must build the Routh array. Examples: s³ + 2s² + 5s + 10 → passes the screen (build the array to be sure) s³ + 2s² − 5s + 10 → sign change → UNSTABLE, no further work needed s³ + 5s + 6 → missing s² term → NOT stable s² + 3s + 2 → n = 2: for a QUADRATIC the screen is sufficient. STABLE
The Routh Array
For s⁴ + a₃s³ + a₂s² + a₁s + a₀: s⁴ | 1 a₂ a₀ s³ | a₃ a₁ 0 s² | b₁ b₂ where b₁ = (a₃a₂ − 1·a₁)/a₃ , b₂ = (a₃a₀ − 0)/a₃ s¹ | c₁ where c₁ = (b₁a₁ − a₃b₂)/b₁ s⁰ | d₁ where d₁ = b₂ THEOREM: the number of sign changes down the FIRST COLUMN equals the number of roots in the right half plane. Quick check: A loop is stable at K = 20 and unstable at K = 90. A colleague concludes "so it is stable for all K below 90". What is wrong with that reasoning, and give a physical example that breaks it.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The reasoning assumes stability is a single interval with one upper limit. It need not be. The classic case is a conditionally stable system, where the loop is unstable at low gain and becomes stable as gain increases — physically this occurs when a lightly damped resonance or an RHP pole needs sufficient loop gain before feedback can control it (an inverted pendulum needs enough gain to catch the fall; some flexible-structure and reheat-turbine loops behave this way). Steady-State Error & System Type

Why proportional control always leaves an offset, why integral action removes it, and why even integral action cannot track everything.

System Type — Count the Integrators
Write the loop gain in the standard form: L(s) = K · Π(τzs + 1) / [ sN · Π(τps + 1) ] N = SYSTEM TYPE = the number of poles at the origin = the number of pure integrations in the loop (process integrators + controller integral action).     Read the diagonal. Each integrator you add buys you exact tracking of one more order of input — but each also adds −90° of phase lag at all frequencies, which spends stability margin. That is the fundamental trade: accuracy is bought with phase, and phase is the currency of stability. This is the single most important sentence on this page.
Why Proportional-Only Control Must Leave an Offset
A P controller produces u = Kc·e. If e = 0 then u = 0. But most processes need a NON-ZERO output to hold the setpoint — a heater needs power to overcome losses, a valve must stay open to maintain flow. Therefore e cannot be zero. The offset is exactly the error needed to generate the required output: ess = urequired / Kc EXAMPLE: a heater needs 40% output to hold 80 °C. With Kc = 4 %/°C: ess = 40/4 = 10 °C offset — it will sit at 70 °C forever. Raise Kc to 20 %/°C → offset 2 °C, but the loop is now 5× more aggressive and may oscillate. You cannot win this with gain alone. The classical fix before integral action existed: MANUAL RESET / BIAS. u = Kc·e + ubias Set u_bias to the output the process needs at the normal operating point, and the offset vanishes — at that operating point only. "Automatic reset" — i.e. integral action — is simply a bias that adjusts ITSELF. That is literally where the term "reset" in "reset time" comes from.
Integral Action: Zero Offset, at a Price
u = Kc·[ e + (1/Ti)∫e dt ] → C(s) = Kc·(1 + 1/(Tis)) = Kc·(Tis + 1)/(Tis) The 1/s adds a pole at the origin → raises the SYSTEM TYPE by one → steady-state error to a step becomes exactly ZERO. Cost, stated precisely: ∠(1/(jωTi)) = −90° at every frequency Near crossover the PI controller's phase is between −90° and 0°, approaching −90° when ω ≪ 1/Ti. → integral action ALWAYS reduces phase margin. Shorter Ti (more reset) costs more margin and eventually oscillates. Two notation conventions — know both, they are reciprocals: Ti = integral (reset) TIME, in seconds or minutes/repeat (smaller = MORE action) Ki = Kc/Ti or "repeats per minute" (bigger = MORE action) Mixing them up is the most common tuning error in the field, and it is usually catastrophic in the aggressive direction.

Removes offset

Any residual error accumulates until the output is whatever the process needs. Self-adjusting bias.

Costs phase

−90° lag. Directly reduces phase margin and slows the loop's usable bandwidth.

Can wind up

If the actuator saturates, the integral keeps accumulating with no effect — then overshoots badly on recovery. Needs anti-windup (Page 15).

Quick check: A CNC axis under PI control tracks a constant-feed straight line but lags 0.04 mm behind the commanded position throughout the cut. Adding more integral gain does not remove it. Explain, and state the two ways to fix it.
Tap to reveal ↓A: A constant-feed move is a ramp in position, and a Type 1 loop has a finite, non-zero steady-state error to a ramp: ess = 1/Kv, the classic following error. Note the axis already contains one integrator (velocity → position), so a PI position controller may be Type 2 or Type 1 depending on the architecture; in the common cascade (position P → velocity PI) the position loop is Type 1 overall and the ramp error is unavoidable. Feedforward is the professional answer because it reduces following error without touching stability at all: the feedback loop then only has to correct the residual. This is exactly why every CNC and servo drive has feedforward gain parameters (A09, A40), and why on a contour the visible symptom of too little Kv or feedforward is corner rounding and radius error on arcs.

Root Locus

A picture of where the closed-loop poles go as gain rises. It converts "how much gain?" into "where do I want the poles?".

The Idea
Closed-loop poles satisfy 1 + K·G(s)H(s) = 0, i.e. K·G(s)H(s) = −1 Split into two conditions on the complex plane: MAGNITUDE: |K·G(s)H(s)| = 1 → gives the value of K at a point ANGLE: ∠G(s)H(s) = ±180°(2k+1) → decides WHICH points are on the locus The root locus is the set of all points satisfying the ANGLE condition: the path traced by the closed-loop poles as K sweeps from 0 to ∞. At K = 0 the closed-loop poles sit at the OPEN-LOOP POLES. As K → ∞ they migrate to the OPEN-LOOP ZEROS (or to infinity along asymptotes).

Frequency Response & Bode Plots

Substitute s = jω and the transfer function becomes a measurement you can actually make on a running plant. This is the practitioner's domain.

Feed a stable LTI system a sinusoid and, once transients die, the output is a sinusoid at the same frequency, scaled by |G(jω)| and shifted by ∠G(jω). Sweep the frequency and you have measured the system completely — without ever writing a differential equation. This is why frequency response, not root locus, is the industrial method: it can be measured, it handles dead time exactly, and it yields the margins that quantify robustness.

Reading a Bode Plot
Magnitude in decibels: |G|dB = 20·log₁₀|G(jω)| 0 dB = gain 1 · +20 dB = ×10 · −20 dB = ÷10 · −3 dB = 0.707 Phase in degrees, both plotted against log ω. WHY dB AND log ω: multiplication becomes addition. A transfer function that is a product of factors becomes a SUM of straight-line asymptotes you can sketch by hand. ASYMPTOTIC BUILDING BLOCKS (the whole method): Constant K : +20log K dB, flat; phase 0° (or −180° if negative) Pole 1/(τs+1) : flat to ω = 1/τ, then −20 dB/decade; phase 0° → −90° Zero (τs+1) : flat to ω = 1/τ, then +20 dB/decade; phase 0° → +90° Integrator 1/s : −20 dB/decade everywhere; phase −90° constant Differentiator s : +20 dB/decade everywhere; phase +90° constant Dead time e−θs : 0 dB everywhere (no attenuation!); phase −57.3·ωθ° (unbounded) Complex pair (ζ) : −40 dB/decade after ωn; phase 0° → −180°; RESONANT PEAK of 1/(2ζ) at ω ≈ ωn when ζ < 0.707 At the corner frequency ω = 1/τ a single pole is exactly −3 dB and −45°.

The delay then adds −57.3·ωgc·0.2 degrees on top: at ωgc = 5 rad/s that is a further −57°, putting the loop firmly unstable. Expected phase margin: negative. Cheapest corrective action: reduce the gain. Lowering K slides the magnitude curve down, which moves ωgc to a lower frequency where both the rational phase lag and the delay's ωθ contribution are smaller — this buys margin for free, at the cost of a slower loop. Quantitatively, to reach 45° PM you need to find the frequency where total phase is −135° and set the gain so crossover happens there. The order of remedies in practice: (1) cut gain to restore ≥45° PM; (2) attack the dead time physically (sensor location, sample rate) since it is buying you nothing; (3) add derivative action or a lead compensator to inject phase lead near crossover — but note derivative action amplifies noise and cannot help much when the lag is dead time rather than a pole; (4) if the loop must be fast despite θ, move to dead-time compensation (Smith predictor) or MPC. Notice that (1) is free and instantaneous, and (2) is permanent — which is why they precede any clever algorithm.

Nyquist & Stability Margins

Margins are the language in which robustness is bought and sold. If you take one number from this module to site, take phase margin.

The Two Margins
Two special frequencies on the loop transfer function L(jω): Gain crossover ωgc : where |L| = 1 (0 dB) Phase crossover ωpc : where ∠L = −180° PHASE MARGIN: PM = 180° + ∠L(jωgc) = how much EXTRA PHASE LAG the loop can tolerate before instability. Extra lag comes from: added dead time, slower sensors, longer scan times, extra filtering, communication latency. GAIN MARGIN: GM = 1 / |L(jωpc)| (in dB: GMdB = −20log|L(jωpc)|) = how much the LOOP GAIN can increase before instability. Gain increases come from: process gain changing with load, valve characteristic, catalyst ageing, someone "helpfully" turning up K. The phase margin converts directly into a dead-time budget: allowable extra delay Δθ = PM(rad) / ωgc e.g. PM = 45° = 0.785 rad at ωgc = 5 rad/s → only 157 ms of extra delay available. Why Two Margins Are Not Enough — the Vector Margin

A loop can show a comfortable 8 dB gain margin and 50° phase margin, and still pass very close to the −1 point at some other frequency — the locus sneaks near −1 diagonally, where neither margin is looking. Such a loop is fragile to simultaneous gain and phase error, which is exactly what real plant variation produces.

The honest single number is the closest distance from the locus to −1: vector margin = min|1 + L(jω)| = 1/max|S(jω)| = 1/Ms where S = 1/(1+L) is the sensitivity function. Design target: Ms ≤ 1.4–2.0 (vector margin ≥ 0.5–0.7) Ms = 1.4 guarantees GM ≥ 3.5 and PM ≥ 45° simultaneously. Modern robust tuning rules (SIMC/lambda, AMIGO) are stated directly in terms of Ms for exactly this reason — one number, no blind spots.

Quick check: A loop is tuned with ωgc = 4 rad/s and PM = 50°. The plant is then moved onto a fieldbus that adds 60 ms of communication and scan latency, and the transmitter's damping filter is increased to 0.5 s. Will it still be stable?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Almost certainly not. Budget the phase you are about to spend. Available: PM = 50° = 0.873 rad, so the dead-time budget is Δθ = 0.873/4 = 218 ms. Spent by the network: 60 ms → 57.3 × 4 × 0.060 = −13.8°. Spent by the filter: a first-order lag of 0.5 s contributes −atan(ωτ) = −atan(4 × 0.5) = −atan(2) = −63.4°. Rules this establishes: (1) a measurement filter must be much faster than the loop — a common guideline is filter τ ≤ 0.1–0.2 × the loop's closed-loop time constant; (2) any added latency (filters, scan time, network, PWM, analyser cycles) must be paid for out of the phase margin, so re-tune whenever the signal path changes; (3) filters are frequently increased by well-meaning operators to "make the trend look smoother" — a nominally harmless act that quietly consumes the loop's entire stability budget. Always record filter settings as part of the loop's tuning documentation.

Controller Design — Where P, I and D Come From

Not three knobs to fiddle with, but three well-defined operations on the loop's frequency response. Once you see PID as a lead-lag network, tuning becomes design.

The Three Terms as Frequency-Domain Operations
Ideal (parallel / ISA) form: C(s) = Kc·[ 1 + 1/(Tis) + Tds ] P — proportional: gain Kc, phase 0° Raises |L| at ALL frequencies → moves ωgc right → faster, less margin. Reduces steady-state error to 1/(1+Kp) but never to zero. I — integral: |1/(Tis)| falls at −20 dB/dec, phase −90° Adds a pole at the origin → Type +1 → ZERO offset to a step. Costs phase margin. Acts mainly BELOW ω = 1/Ti. Set 1/Ti about a decade below ωgc so the −90° is largely spent where it does not threaten crossover. D — derivative: |Tds| rises at +20 dB/dec, phase +90° Adds a zero → bends the root locus LEFT → more damping, more phase margin, allows HIGHER Kc and therefore a faster loop. Costs noise amplification (gain rises with frequency, and so does noise). Set 1/Td near or just above ωgc to place the phase lead where it counts. PID as a LEAD-LAG network — combine the terms over a common denominator: C(s) = Kc·(TiTds² + Tis + 1)/(Tis) = an integrator × two zeros The integrator is the lag (accuracy); the two zeros are the lead (damping and speed). Every PID is therefore a lag–lead compensator with the lag pinned at the origin. That is the whole theory.
Practical PID — What Real Controllers Actually Implement
① Derivative filtering (always present, N typically 8–20): Tds → Tds / (1 + (Td/N)s) Pure derivative has infinite gain at infinite frequency — unusable with noise. The filter caps the D gain at N. This is why "D" almost never works on a noisy flow loop, and works beautifully on a smooth temperature loop. ② Derivative on measurement only (not on error): u = Kc[ e + (1/Ti)∫e dt − Td·dy/dt ] A setpoint STEP would otherwise differentiate to an impulse — the notorious "derivative kick" that slams a valve. Deriving on y removes it entirely. ③ Setpoint weighting (two-degree-of-freedom PID): u = Kc[ (β·r − y) + (1/Ti)∫(r − y)dt − Td·dy/dt ] β < 1 softens the setpoint response WITHOUT changing disturbance rejection or stability. β ≈ 0.5–0.7 is a common default. This is the clean answer to the servo-vs-regulator conflict of Page 3. ④ Series (interacting) form — many older/pneumatic controllers: C(s) = Kc'·(1 + 1/(Ti's))·(1 + Td's) Conversion to ideal form: Kc = Kc'·f, Ti = Ti'·f, Td = Td'/f with f = 1 + Td'/Ti' Never copy tuning constants between controllers without checking the form. Also check units: gain vs proportional band (PB% = 100/Kc), seconds vs minutes, Ti vs repeats-per-minute. Most "the new PLC tunes differently" complaints are unit or form mismatches.

Digital Control & Sampling

Every controller you will ever commission is a computer running at a finite rate. Sampling adds dead time and can invent frequencies that were never there.

A PLC or DCS does not compute continuously. It samples the PV, executes the algorithm, and holds the output constant until the next scan. That sample-and-hold is not a harmless implementation detail: it introduces an average dead time of about half the sample period and it makes any frequency above half the sampling rate unrecoverable. Both facts have destroyed loops that were correct on paper.

Sampling, Aliasing & the Nyquist Limit
Nyquist–Shannon: to reconstruct a signal you must sample at more than twice its highest frequency component. fNyquist = fs/2 (fs = 1/Ts, Ts = sample period) Any content above fs/2 does not simply disappear — it is ALIASED: it reappears as a false low-frequency signal indistinguishable from real data. Apparent (alias) frequency: falias = |f − k·fs| for the nearest integer k CLASSIC INDUSTRIAL EXAMPLE A pump produces a 51 Hz vibration-induced pressure ripple. The PLC samples that pressure every 20 ms (fs = 50 Hz). Alias: |51 − 50| = 1 Hz. The controller now sees, and faithfully fights, a 1 Hz oscillation that does not physically exist — and its output makes a real one. THE CURE IS HARDWARE, NOT SOFTWARE. An ANTI-ALIASING (analogue low-pass) filter must remove content above fs/2 BEFORE the ADC. No digital filter can undo aliasing, because the information is already destroyed.
The Zero-Order Hold and Its Hidden Dead Time
The DAC holds the computed output constant for a whole period. Averaged over time, the effective output lags the ideal continuous one by half a period: ZOH ≈ e−sTs/2 → effective added dead time ≈ Ts/2 Add the computation itself (output written at the end of the scan) and the practical budget for a PLC loop is: θtotal = θprocess + Ts/2 (hold) + Ts (scan/execution) + filter lag + fieldbus latency WORKED: a 100 ms PLC task, a 200 ms transmitter filter, 50 ms of bus latency → added θ ≈ 0.05 + 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.05 = 0.4 s of NEW dead time For a fast pressure or flow loop with θprocess ≈ 0.1 s, the CONTROL SYSTEM has become 4× more of a delay than the process. This is why fast loops belong in the drive or in a dedicated fast task — not in the 500 ms "slow" task. SAMPLE-TIME SELECTION RULES (use both): Ts ≤ 0.1·τ (at least 10 samples per process time constant) Ts ≤ 0.2·θ when dead time is small — sampling must resolve it Ts ≤ 0.05–0.1 × the closed-loop time constant you want Faster than 10× per τ buys almost nothing and costs CPU; slower than 10× degrades the loop measurably. Typical practice: flow/pressure 50–100 ms, level 250–500 ms, temperature 1 s, analytical 1–5 s.
The z-Domain
The discrete analogue of the Laplace transform, with z representing a one-sample advance: z = esTs ⇔ z−1 = a delay of one sample This maps the s-plane into the z-plane: LEFT half plane (stable) → INSIDE the unit circle |z| < 1 jω axis → the unit circle itself |z| = 1 RIGHT half plane → outside the unit circle DISCRETE STABILITY: all closed-loop poles must satisfy |z| < 1. s = −σ ± jω → |z| = e−σTs, ∠z = ±ωTs So a fast stable pole (large σ) maps near the ORIGIN of the z-plane, and a pole at the Nyquist frequency maps to z = −1 (alternating ±, "ringing every sample"). Discrete PID — the form actually coded in a PLC (velocity/incremental form): Δuk = Kc[ (ek − ek−1) + (Ts/Ti)·ek + (Td/Ts)(ek − 2ek−1 + ek−2) ] uk = uk−1 + Δuk Why the incremental form is preferred in industry: · Anti-windup is inherent — clamp uk and the integral cannot run away. · Bumpless manual→auto transfer is automatic (it starts from the current output). · Mode changes and output tracking are trivial to implement. Note the derivative term's Td/Ts factor: halving the sample time DOUBLES the derivative's noise gain. Derivative and fast sampling fight each other.

State-Space & Modern Control

Transfer functions describe input–output behaviour. State-space describes what is happening inside — and it scales to many inputs and outputs, which transfer functions do not.

The State-Space Representation
ẋ = A·x + B·u (state equation — n first-order ODEs) y = C·x + D·u (output equation) x = state vector (n × 1) — the minimum information needed to predict the future u = input vector (m × 1) · y = output vector (p × 1) A = system matrix (n × n) — the internal dynamics B = input matrix · C = output matrix · D = direct (feedthrough) term, usually 0 The state is the memory of the system. For a mass-spring-damper the state is {position, velocity}; for an RLC circuit {capacitor voltage, inductor current}; for a two-tank process {level 1, level 2}. One state per energy store — the same counting rule as Page 2. Link back to transfer functions: G(s) = C·(sI − A)−1·B + D and crucially: the poles of G(s) are the EIGENVALUES of A (roots of det(sI − A) = 0) So stability is: all eigenvalues of A have negative real parts.
EXAMPLE — mass–spring–damper as state-space. Let x₁ = position, x₂ = velocity: ẋ₁ = x₂ ẋ₂ = (1/m)(F − c·x₂ − k·x₁) A = [ 0 1 ] B = [ 0 ] C = [1 0] D = 0 [ −k/m −c/m ] [ 1/m ] det(sI − A) = s² + (c/m)s + k/m = 0 → the familiar second-order poles, with ωn = √(k/m) and ζ = c/(2√(km)). Same physics, different bookkeeping.
Controllability & Observability
CONTROLLABILITY — can the input reach every state? Controllability matrix P = [ B AB A²B … An−1B ] Controllable ⟺ rank(P) = n (P is full rank). If not: some internal mode cannot be influenced at all by your actuator. Physical meaning: you have the wrong actuator, or too few of them. OBSERVABILITY — can the output reveal every state? Observability matrix Q = [ C ; CA ; CA² ; … ; CAn−1 ] Observable ⟺ rank(Q) = n. If not: some internal state is invisible to your sensors, so no observer or estimator can ever recover it. Physical meaning: you have the wrong measurement, or too few of them. Why an engineer cares: these two tests tell you whether a control problem is solvable BEFORE you design anything. An uncontrollable or unobservable mode is an instrumentation problem, not a tuning problem — and no amount of clever algorithm will fix it. This is the formal version of "you cannot control what you cannot measure, and you cannot fix what you cannot reach."
State Feedback, Observers & LQR
State feedback: u = −K·x → closed loop ẋ = (A − BK)x You can place the closed-loop eigenvalues ANYWHERE you like, provided the system is controllable (Ackermann's formula / pole placement). Observer (state estimator): most states are not measured. Build a model that runs in parallel and is corrected by the measurement error: x̂̇ = A·x̂ + B·u + L·(y − C·x̂) Choose L so the estimation error decays quickly. With stochastic noise assumptions, the optimal L is the KALMAN FILTER gain. LQR — Linear Quadratic Regulator: instead of guessing pole locations, minimise J = ∫ (xᵀQx + uᵀRu) dt Q penalises state error, R penalises control effort. Larger R → gentler, cheaper control; larger Q → tighter, more aggressive control. Solving the Riccati equation gives the optimal K. This is the cleanest way to trade performance against actuator wear, and it extends naturally to MIMO.

Integral Windup and Its Cures
MECHANISM: the actuator is at its limit, the error persists, and the integral term keeps accumulating even though the output can no longer change. When the error finally reverses, the controller must "unwind" a large accumulated term before the output leaves the limit — producing a long, ugly overshoot that looks like bad tuning but is not. CURES, in order of preference: ① Incremental (velocity) form PID — clamp uk and windup is structurally impossible. Standard in modern PLC/DCS blocks. ② Conditional integration — freeze the integral whenever the output is saturated (integral "hold"). ③ Back-calculation / integrator clamping — set the integral to whatever value would make the computed output exactly equal the limit. ④ Output tracking — a valve-position or actuator-feedback signal fed back so the controller always knows what the actuator really did (essential for cascade and split-range, and the honest fix when a valve saturates often). RELATED IMPLEMENTATION MUST-HAVES · Bumpless transfer manual→auto: initialise the integral so the computed output equals the current manual output. Otherwise the process jumps. · Output rate limits to protect actuators and downstream units. · Correct action (direct/reverse) — verify by pushing the output manually and watching the sign of the PV. Getting this wrong makes a stable loop run away, and it is a genuinely common commissioning error. · Setpoint ramping instead of step changes, so the operator's convenience does not force the actuator into saturation.

The Eight Ideas That Carry Everything
  • 1
    Poles are response shapes. LHP = decay, RHP = growth, complex pair = ringing, at the origin = integration. Stability is simply "all closed-loop poles in the left half plane".
  • 2
    Closed-loop poles are the roots of 1 + L(s) = 0. Routh, root locus and Nyquist are three views of that one equation.
  • 3
    Feedback divides error and sensitivity by (1 + L). This is why control works on plants you barely understand — and why loop gain is the quantity that matters.
  • 4
    ζ and ωn convert specifications into geometry. %OS fixes ζ (0.707 → 4.3%), ts ≈ 4/(ζωn) fixes the distance from the axis. Design = putting the poles in the resulting wedge.
  • 5
    Every integrator buys one order of tracking accuracy and costs 90° of phase. Accuracy is paid for in stability margin — always.
  • 6
    Dead time is the fundamental limit. It adds unbounded phase lag with no attenuation. θ/τ predicts how hard a loop will be; reducing θ beats any retuning.
  • 7
    Phase margin is the working currency. PM ≥ 45°, GM ≥ 6 dB, Ms ≤ 2 — and PM/ωgc is your budget for every extra filter, scan and network hop.
  • 8
    Sampling is not free. Ts ≤ 0.1τ, add Ts/2 of dead time for the hold, and filter before the ADC or aliasing will invent oscillations you cannot remove.
⚠️ Six Ways Control Design Goes Wrong
① Tuning without a model

Twiddling gains until the trend "looks better", with no step test, no K/τ/θ and no record. The result is unrepeatable, unverifiable, and collapses at the next load change. Cure: a two-minute open-loop bump test buys everything downstream.

② Judging a loop by its step response alone

A response can look crisp and still sit at 15° phase margin, one plant change away from oscillation. Cure: verify margins, then deliberately raise the gain 50% and confirm it holds.

③ Fighting dead time with gain

Increasing Kc to "speed up" a loop with θ/τ ≈ 1 produces oscillation, because the controller is always acting on stale information. Cure: attack θ physically, or accept a slower λ; consider feedforward or dead-time compensation.

④ Derivative on a noisy signal

D differentiates noise, so the valve is agitated continuously and wears out — and the operator eventually turns the loop off. Cure: no D on flow/pressure; filter D with N = 8–20; derive on measurement, never on error.

⑤ Ignoring the actuator

No anti-windup, no output limits, no bumpless transfer, or a valve that spends its life at 95% open. The tuning is then irrelevant. Cure: check saturation frequency and valve travel before touching the algorithm.

⑥ Silently changing the signal path

A filter increased "to smooth the trend", a loop moved to a slower task, a transmitter replaced with a damped one. Each spends phase margin the loop cannot spare. Cure: record filters, scan times and tuning together, and re-tune whenever the path changes.

Final check 1: Explain in one paragraph why a loop with high gain and no dead time is stable, but the same gain becomes unstable when 0.5 s of dead time is added.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Instability requires the loop signal to arrive back at the summing junction inverted and undiminished — that is, phase lag of 180° while |L| ≥ 1. A single-pole plant can only ever contribute 90° of lag, so no matter how much gain you apply, the total phase never reaches −180° and the loop cannot oscillate (this is exactly the Page 3 result that a first-order plant just gets faster as gain rises). Dead time changes the situation qualitatively, not quantitatively: its phase lag is −ωθ radians, unbounded, and its magnitude is exactly 1 at every frequency — it removes no gain at all while turning the phase indefinitely. With θ = 0.5 s, the delay alone contributes −180° at ω = π/0.5 = 6.3 rad/s; if the loop's gain is still above unity at that frequency, negative feedback has become positive feedback and the oscillation sustains itself. The general principle: stability is a race between the gain falling below 1 and the phase reaching −180°. Poles help you win that race (they cut gain as they add phase); dead time is pure phase with no gain penalty, so it is the one element that always loses it.
Final check 2: A loop's step response overshoots 25% and settles in 8 s. Your specification is 10% overshoot and 4 s settling. State, in s-plane terms, exactly what must change — and which controller terms achieve it.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Convert both specifications into pole locations. Present: 25% overshoot → ζ = −ln(0.25)/√(π²+ln²0.25) = 1.386/√(9.87+1.92) = 1.386/3.434 = 0.404; ts = 4/(ζωn) = 8 → ζωn = 0.5, so ωn = 1.24 rad/s and the poles are at s = −0.5 ± j1.13. Required: 10% overshoot → ζ = 2.303/√(9.87+5.30) = 2.303/3.895 = 0.591; ts = 4 → ζωn = 1.0, so ωn = 1.69 rad/s and the target poles are s = −1.0 ± j1.36. So the poles must move left (twice as far from the jω axis, for speed) and rotate toward the real axis (for damping) — the classic "up and to the left within the design wedge" of Page 5. Which terms do it: moving left and adding damping simultaneously is what a zero does to a root locus, so the answer is derivative action (or a lead compensator) — D contributes +90° of phase, bending the locus left, which both damps the response and permits a higher Kc to deliver the extra speed. Proportional gain alone would increase ωn but move the poles along the existing locus, reducing ζ and making overshoot worse; integral action would add a pole and make matters worse still. Practical caveats: confirm the measurement is clean enough for D (filter with N = 8–20, derive on measurement), verify phase margin ends up ≥ 45°, and check the actuator can supply the faster response without saturating — a specification that requires the valve to slam is not a specification, it is a wish.
Final check 3: Given only a chart recording of an oscillating loop, what four measurements would you take with a ruler, and what would each tell you?
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Period between successive peaks, Td → the damped frequency ωd = 2π/Td, and if the loop is at its stability limit this is the ultimate period Tu, which with the ultimate gain gives you Ziegler–Nichols settings directly. A period of roughly 2–4× the known dead time points to a tuning-induced oscillation; a period unrelated to the process dynamics points to an external source. (2) Ratio of successive peak amplitudes → the decay ratio, and hence ζ (a quarter-amplitude decay is ζ ≈ 0.2; equal amplitudes mean ζ = 0, i.e. marginal stability — the loop is at its limit, not near it). (3) Shape of the waveform → sinusoidal implies linear dynamics, so tuning; a square wave or sawtooth with stepped MV is the fingerprint of valve stiction, which no tuning will cure; a slow, large triangular cycle on a level loop suggests excessive reset on an integrating process. (4) The MV trace alongside the PV → check whether the output is hitting 0% or 100% (saturation and possible windup), whether it moves in discrete jumps (stiction or quantisation), and its phase relative to PV (in a tuning-induced oscillation the MV leads the PV by roughly 180°). Then the one action that is not a measurement: put the loop in manual with a fixed output. If the cycle persists, everything above still applies to the process, not the controller — and you have just saved yourself from re-tuning an innocent loop.
Final check 4: Why does the SIMC rule make controller gain inversely proportional to process gain and proportional to τ? Argue from loop-gain first principles.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Stability and speed are both governed by the loop gain L = C·G, not by the controller alone, so what a designer really chooses is the product. Inverse proportionality to K: if the process converts 1% of valve travel into 2 °C, the controller needs half as much gain as it would if the process gave 1 °C per percent, in order to place the crossover at the same frequency and keep the same margins. Doubling K without halving Kc doubles L, moving ωgc to a higher frequency where more phase lag has accumulated — margin lost, oscillation gained. This is also why a nonlinear process (√ΔP flow, pH) needs linearisation or gain scheduling: K itself moves, so a fixed Kc cannot keep the loop gain constant. Proportionality to τ: a slow process attenuates the loop gain gently — its pole rolls |G| off at only −20 dB/dec starting at the low frequency 1/τ, so at the crossover region the plant has already contributed most of its available 90° of lag but the gain is still usable. Put differently, the target closed-loop speed is set by λ+θ; achieving a closed-loop time constant of (λ+θ) on a process whose own time constant is τ requires speeding it up by the factor τ/(λ+θ), and from Page 3 that speed-up factor is the loop gain: K·Kc = τ/(λ+θ), which rearranges to exactly Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)). So SIMC is not an empirical curve fit at all — it is the statement "choose the loop gain that gives the closed-loop time constant you asked for, and never let λ fall below θ, because dead time bounds what is achievable."
Final check 5: Name three loops in a real plant where you would not use PID, and say what you would use instead and why.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) A distillation column's composition control — strongly interacting multivariable dynamics (reflux and reboil each affect both product compositions), long dead times from analysers, and hard purity constraints. Use MPC, which handles coupling and constraints natively; PID loops here fight each other and must be detuned so far that they barely work. (2) An averaging surge-tank level — the objective is not to hold setpoint but to absorb flow variation and pass a smooth flow downstream. Use a deliberately loose P-only (or wide-band PI) controller with the setpoint at mid-tank; tight PID control here defeats the tank's entire purpose by transmitting every upstream disturbance onward. (3) A safety function — e.g. tripping a reactor on high temperature. Use a safety instrumented function per IEC 61511 with rated SIL, independent sensors and a final element that fails safe; PID is a control function and must never be relied on for protection (A52). Honourable mentions: a batch reactor's exothermic ramp, where split-range cooling/heating plus a cascade to jacket temperature beats a single PID; a very fast current or torque loop, which belongs inside the drive at kHz rates rather than in a PLC (A08/A09); and a deadband-limited on/off actuator (solenoid, single-speed pump), where the correct algorithm is hysteresis or duty-cycling, not a continuous PID whose output cannot be realised. The general test: ask whether the problem is single-loop, unconstrained, well-measured and modest in dead time. If yes, PID is almost always the right and cheapest answer; if no, name which of those four assumptions fails, and that failure names your alternative.

WE1 · Complete Second-Order Design from a Specification

Given: Plant G(s) = 10/(s(s+4)) under unity proportional feedback. Specify K to achieve 15% overshoot. Then find ωn, settling time, peak time, and the steady-state error to a unit ramp.

Step 1 — required damping ratio from the overshoot spec OS = 0.15 → ln(0.15) = −1.897 ζ = −ln(OS)/√(π² + ln²OS) = 1.897/√(9.870 + 3.599) = 1.897/√13.469 ζ = 1.897/3.670 = 0.517 Step 2 — closed-loop characteristic equation 1 + K·10/(s(s+4)) = 0 → s² + 4s + 10K = 0 Compare with s² + 2ζωns + ωn²: 2ζωn = 4 → ζωn = 2 (FIXED by the plant, independent of K!) ωn² = 10K Step 3 — solve for ωn and K ζωn = 2 and ζ = 0.517 → ωn = 2/0.517 = 3.87 rad/s 10K = ωn² = 14.98 → K = 1.50 Step 4 — resulting performance Poles: s = −ζωn ± jωn√(1−ζ²) = −2 ± j3.87·0.856 = −2 ± j3.31 ts = 4/(ζωn) = 4/2 = 2.0 s tp = π/ωd = 3.1416/3.31 = 0.95 s Damped period Td = 2π/3.31 = 1.90 s Step 5 — steady-state error to a unit ramp The plant already has one pole at the origin → Type 1 system. Kv = lims→0 s·L(s) = lims→0 s·(1.50 × 10)/(s(s+4)) = 15/4 = 3.75 ess(ramp) = 1/Kv = 0.267 (step error = 0, being Type 1)

Discussion / insight. The decisive observation is in Step 2: ζωn = 2 regardless of K. The real part of the closed-loop poles is fixed by the plant's own pole at −4 (they slide vertically off the real axis as K rises), so settling time cannot be changed by proportional gain at all — ts = 4/2 = 2 s no matter what K you choose. Gain buys you only ωn, i.e. more ringing frequency and less damping. If the specification had also demanded ts ≤ 1 s, proportional control would have been structurally incapable of meeting it, and you would need to add a zero (derivative/lead action) to pull the poles left. Recognising "this specification cannot be met with this controller structure" is worth more marks — and more engineering credibility — than computing a gain that silently misses one requirement.

WE2 · Routh–Hurwitz with a PI Controller

Given: G(s) = 2/((s+1)(s+3)) with a PI controller C(s) = Kc(1 + 1/(2s)). Find the range of Kc for stability, and the frequency of oscillation at the limit.

Step 1 — form the loop gain C(s) = Kc(2s + 1)/(2s) L(s) = Kc(2s+1)/(2s) · 2/((s+1)(s+3)) = Kc(2s+1)/(s(s+1)(s+3)) Step 2 — characteristic equation 1 + L = 0 → s(s+1)(s+3) + Kc(2s+1) = 0 Expand s(s²+4s+3) = s³ + 4s² + 3s s³ + 4s² + (3 + 2Kc)s + Kc = 0 Step 3 — Routh array s³ | 1 3 + 2Kc s² | 4 Kc s¹ | [4(3+2Kc) − Kc]/4 = (12 + 8Kc − Kc)/4 = (12 + 7Kc)/4 s⁰ | Kc Step 4 — positivity conditions on the first column (12 + 7Kc)/4 > 0 → Kc > −12/7 = −1.71 Kc > 0 → STABLE FOR ALL Kc > 0. No upper limit! Step 5 — sanity check by inspection As Kc → ∞ the closed-loop poles approach the open-loop ZEROS of L: the finite zero at s = −0.5, plus two branches to infinity along the ±90° asymptotes... with n − m = 3 − 1 = 2 branches to infinity. Centroid σa = (0 −1 −3 − (−0.5))/2 = (−4 + 0.5)/2 = −1.75 < 0 → the asymptotes are VERTICAL at Re = −1.75, i.e. they never cross into the RHP. Consistent with "stable for all Kc". ✓

Discussion / insight. This is the counter-example students need: adding integral action does not automatically create an upper gain limit, and a third-order loop is not automatically conditionally stable. The reason is the PI controller's zero at s = −1/Ti = −0.5, which reduces the relative degree from 3 to 2 and drags the asymptote centroid safely into the left half plane. Change Ti to a much larger value (say 20 s, putting the zero at −0.05, close to the origin) and the centroid moves toward −4/2 = −2 but the near-origin pole–zero pair makes the loop sluggish and prone to a long integral tail instead; make Ti very small and the zero moves far left, the asymptotes shift right, and an upper gain limit reappears. The general lesson: stability depends on the relative degree and the pole–zero pattern, not on a simple count of poles. And the practical corollary: "stable for all gain" is a mathematical statement about an idealised model — add the real plant's neglected fast poles and its dead time, and a finite limit always returns. Never ship a design on the strength of an unbounded stability range.

WE3 · Gain & Phase Margin by Hand

Given: L(s) = 8/(s(s+2)(s+8)). Find the gain crossover frequency, phase margin, phase crossover frequency and gain margin. Then state whether the loop meets a 45° PM specification and what gain change would achieve it.

Step 1 — put into Bode (time-constant) form L(s) = 8/(s·2(0.5s+1)·8(0.125s+1)) = 0.5/(s(0.5s+1)(0.125s+1)) Bode gain K = 0.5, corners at ω = 2 and ω = 8 rad/s. Step 2 — magnitude and phase expressions |L(jω)| = 0.5 / (ω·√(1+(0.5ω)²)·√(1+(0.125ω)²)) ∠L(jω) = −90° − atan(0.5ω) − atan(0.125ω) Step 3 — find ωgc where |L| = 1 (trial) ω = 0.5 : 0.5/(0.5 × 1.031 × 1.002) = 0.968 (just above 1... slightly under) ω = 0.48: 0.5/(0.48 × 1.0286 × 1.0018) = 1.010 ω = 0.49: 0.5/(0.49 × 1.0299 × 1.0019) = 0.989 → ωgc ≈ 0.485 rad/s Step 4 — phase margin ∠L(j0.485) = −90° − atan(0.2425) − atan(0.0606) = −90° − 13.63° − 3.47° = −107.1° PM = 180° − 107.1° = 72.9° Step 5 — phase crossover (∠L = −180°) Need atan(0.5ω) + atan(0.125ω) = 90°, which occurs when (0.5ω)(0.125ω) = 1 → 0.0625ω² = 1 → ω² = 16 ωpc = 4 rad/s (using the identity atanA + atanB = 90° ⟺ A·B = 1) Step 6 — gain margin |L(j4)| = 0.5/(4 × √(1+4) × √(1+0.25)) = 0.5/(4 × 2.236 × 1.118) = 0.5/9.999 = 0.0500 GM = 1/0.05 = 20 → 20·log₁₀(20) = 26.0 dB Step 7 — verdict PM = 72.9° and GM = 26 dB — both comfortably exceed the 45°/6 dB target. The loop is over-conservative: it is slower than it needs to be. To trade some margin for speed, raise the gain. Aiming at PM = 45°: find ω where ∠L = −135°: −90 − atan(0.5ω) − atan(0.125ω) = −135 atan(0.5ω) + atan(0.125ω) = 45° ω ≈ 1.55 rad/s (by trial: atan0.775=37.8°, atan0.194=11.0° → 48.8°; ω=1.40: atan0.70=35.0°, atan0.175=9.9° → 44.9° ✓) → target ωgc = 1.40 rad/s. |L(j1.40)| = 0.5/(1.40×1.221×1.015) = 0.288 Required gain multiplier = 1/0.288 = 3.47× → new K ≈ 0.5 × 3.47 = 1.74 i.e. L(s) numerator becomes ≈ 27.8 instead of 8, and ωgc nearly triples.

Discussion / insight. Two habits worth stealing. First, the identity atan A + atan B = 90° ⟺ A·B = 1 turns the phase-crossover search into one line of algebra — for a loop with an integrator and two lags, ωpc = 1/√(τ₁τ₂), here 1/√(0.5 × 0.125) = 4 ✓. Second, note that excess margin is a real cost, not just safety: 26 dB of gain margin means the loop is running at a fourteenth of the gain it could tolerate, so it responds roughly three times slower than necessary. Good engineering is not maximising margin; it is hitting a target margin deliberately — typically 45–60° PM and 6–12 dB GM — and documenting the choice. The final calculation shows exactly how that trade is priced: a 3.5× gain increase converts 28 dB of unused margin into a threefold bandwidth improvement.

WE4 · FOPDT Identification and SIMC Tuning

Given: A bump test on a heat exchanger outlet temperature loop: MV stepped from 45% to 55%, PV moved from 128 °C to 146 °C. The PV reached 28.3% of its change at t = 22 s and 63.2% at t = 46 s after the step. Identify the model, tune with SIMC, and predict the closed-loop response. Then check the sample time for a 500 ms PLC task.

Step 1 — process gain Δu = 55 − 45 = 10% Δy = 146 − 128 = 18 °C K = 18/10 = 1.8 °C per % MV Step 2 — time constant and dead time (two-point method) τ = 1.5(t₆₃ − t₂₈) = 1.5(46 − 22) = 1.5 × 24 = 36 s θ = t₆₃ − τ = 46 − 36 = 10 s Controllability ratio θ/τ = 10/36 = 0.28 → normal, comfortable PID territory Step 3 — SIMC PI tuning with λ = θ = 10 s (robust default) Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)) = 36/(1.8 × 20) = 36/36 = 1.00 %/°C Ti = min(τ, 4(λ+θ)) = min(36, 80) = 36 s (Proportional band equivalent: PB = 100/Kc = 100%) Step 4 — predicted closed-loop behaviour SIMC targets a first-order-plus-delay closed loop with time constant λ: closed-loop τc ≈ λ = 10 s, plus the unavoidable θ = 10 s of delay → the PV starts moving after ~10 s and is ~63% of the way in ~20 s, essentially settled in 4λ + θ ≈ 50 s, with little or no overshoot. Loop gain check: K·Kc = 1.8 × 1.00 = 1.8 = τ/(λ+θ) = 36/20 ✓ Step 5 — a more robust alternative (λ = 3θ = 30 s) Kc = 36/(1.8 × 40) = 0.50, Ti = min(36, 160) = 36 s Half the gain: gentler on the valve, more margin, roughly twice as slow. Step 6 — sample time check for a 500 ms task Rule Ts ≤ 0.1τ = 3.6 s ✓ and Ts ≤ 0.2θ = 2.0 s ✓ Added dead time ≈ Ts/2 + Ts = 0.25 + 0.5 = 0.75 s → θeffective = 10.75 s, only 7.5% worse. 500 ms is comfortably adequate for this loop; there is no benefit in a faster task.

Discussion / insight. This is the complete professional workflow in one example, and the last step is the one most often skipped. Notice how the numbers interlock: because τ is 36 s, half a second of scan time is irrelevant — but on a flow loop with τ = 2 s the identical PLC task would add 0.75 s to a 0.2 s dead time and quadruple it. The same controller in the same PLC is excellent for one loop and unusable for another, purely because of the ratio of scan time to process dynamics. Two further professional touches: repeat the bump test in the downward direction (a heat exchanger often has a different gain cooling than heating, because the flow regime and ΔT change), and record the operating point (45–55% MV, 128–146 °C) with the tuning, because K = 1.8 °C/% is only true near there.

WE5 · Digital Implementation, Aliasing and Phase Budget

Given: A pressure loop is to run at ωgc = 6 rad/s with 50° phase margin. The transmitter carries a 27 Hz pump-induced ripple. Choose a task period, specify the anti-aliasing filter, and verify the phase budget.

Step 1 — phase (dead-time) budget from the margin PM = 50° = 50 × π/180 = 0.873 rad Total allowable EXTRA delay Δθ = PM/ωgc = 0.873/6 = 145 ms This is the entire budget for scan, hold, filtering and network — spend it deliberately. Step 2 — choose the task period Closed-loop time constant ≈ 1/ωgc ≈ 167 ms. Rule Ts ≤ 0.05–0.1 × τclosed → Ts ≤ 8–17 ms Choose Ts = 10 ms (fs = 100 Hz) Cost: hold Ts/2 = 5 ms + execution ≈ 10 ms = 15 ms of the 145 ms budget. Step 3 — aliasing check on the 27 Hz ripple Nyquist frequency fs/2 = 50 Hz. Since 27 Hz < 50 Hz it is NOT aliased — it is sampled correctly and will appear as a genuine 27 Hz signal. ⚠ But had the task been 25 ms (fs = 40 Hz, Nyquist 20 Hz), the ripple would alias to |27 − 40| = 13 Hz — a false signal the controller would fight. This is why the task period must be chosen with the disturbance spectrum in hand. Step 4 — specify the anti-aliasing filter Must attenuate everything above 50 Hz, in ANALOGUE hardware, before the ADC. Choose a first-order RC with corner fc = 20 Hz (ωc = 126 rad/s): attenuation at 50 Hz = 1/√(1+(50/20)²) = 1/2.69 = −8.6 dB (a second-order filter would give −17 dB; specify 2nd order if the ripple is large) Phase cost AT THE LOOP'S CROSSOVER (the only place it matters): −atan(ωgc/ωc) = −atan(6/126) = −atan(0.0476) = −2.7° In time terms ≈ 2.7/57.3/6 = 7.9 ms of the budget. Cheap. ✓ Step 5 — total budget reconciliation scan + hold 15 ms anti-alias filter 8 ms network (assume) 20 ms valve/actuator 30 ms TOTAL 73 ms against a 145 ms budget → half the budget unused ✓ Remaining headroom absorbs future changes without re-tuning. Step 6 — the trap to avoid If someone later adds a 0.5 s "smoothing" filter in the transmitter: phase cost = −atan(6 × 0.5) = −atan(3) = −71.6° That alone exceeds the entire 50° margin → the loop becomes unstable. A digital filter in the transmitter is NOT a substitute for the analogue anti-aliasing filter, and it is not free.

Discussion / insight. The value of this example is the discipline of an explicit phase budget. Every element in the signal path — scan, hold, filter, network, actuator slew — consumes phase margin, and the budget Δθ = PM/ωgc lets you price each one in milliseconds before you commit. Two subtleties worth carrying to site. First, the anti-aliasing filter must be analogue and ahead of the ADC: aliasing destroys information, and no digital filter can restore what was never sampled. Second, the filter's corner should be placed far above the loop's crossover (here 126 rad/s versus 6 rad/s, a factor of 21) so it costs almost no phase — this is the standard resolution of the apparent conflict between "filter the noise" and "don't add lag". The failure in Step 6 is not hypothetical; increasing transmitter damping to make a trend look tidier is one of the most common ways a stable, well-engineered loop is destroyed months after commissioning.

P3. For L(s) = K/(s(s+6)(s+10)), find the maximum K for stability and the oscillation frequency at that gain.

Practical note: Ziegler–Nichols would give Kc ≈ 0.45 × 960 = 432 for PI — enormous, and a good illustration of why ZN's quarter-decay target (ζ ≈ 0.2) is rarely what you actually want.
P4. A Type 1 loop has Kv = 25 s−1. Find the steady-state error to a unit step, to a ramp of 4 units/s, and to a parabolic input.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Step: a Type 1 system has Kp = ∞, so ess = 1/(1+∞) = 0. Ramp at 4 units/s: the error scales with the ramp slope, so ess = slope/Kv = 4/25 = 0.16 units (the standard formula 1/Kv assumes a unit ramp — a very common exam trap). Parabola: Ka = 0 for Type 1, so the error is infinite — the output falls progressively further behind. Engineering reading: this is a servo axis that holds position perfectly at rest, lags 0.16 mm at a feed of 4 mm/s, and cannot track an acceleration at all. To fix the ramp error you either raise Kv (stiffer loop, limited by resonance) or add velocity feedforward; to track acceleration you need Type 2 or acceleration feedforward. Every CNC control does both, which is why its parameter list has feedforward gains next to the loop gains (A09/A40).
P5. A process is K = 3.2 °C/%, τ = 120 s, θ = 45 s. Compute SIMC PI settings for λ = θ and for λ = 3θ, and state the expected closed-loop settling time in each case.
Tap to reveal ↓A: λ = θ = 45 s: Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)) = 120/(3.2 × 90) = 120/288 = 0.417 %/°C; Ti = min(τ, 4(λ+θ)) = min(120, 360) = 120 s. Closed-loop τc ≈ λ = 45 s, so settling ≈ 4λ + θ = 180 + 45 = ≈ 225 s. λ = 3θ = 135 s: Kc = 120/(3.2 × 180) = 0.208 %/°C; Ti = min(120, 720) = 120 s. Settling ≈ 4 × 135 + 45 = ≈ 585 s, roughly 2.6× slower with half the gain. Interpretation: θ/τ = 0.375 is workable but the loop is genuinely dead-time-limited — the best achievable settling is about 3–4 minutes, and no tuning can beat that. Choose λ = θ if the process tolerates brisk valve movement and the gain is well known; choose λ = 3θ if K varies with load or the valve is worn. Notice Ti = τ in both cases: SIMC sets integral time from the process, not from the aggressiveness choice, so λ moves the gain only — a clean, one-knob trade.
P6. A loop is tuned to ωgc = 8 rad/s with PM = 55°. How much extra dead time can it tolerate? A colleague wants to add a 100 ms averaging filter to the transmitter — is that acceptable?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Budget: Δθ = PM(rad)/ωgc = (55 × π/180)/8 = 0.960/8 = 120 ms of extra pure delay before the phase margin is entirely consumed. A first-order 100 ms filter is not a pure delay; its phase cost at crossover is −atan(ωτ) = −atan(8 × 0.1) = −atan(0.8) = −38.7°. That leaves PM = 55 − 38.7 = 16.3° — technically still stable, but far below any sensible specification: expect a lively, ringing response, poor robustness to process-gain change, and a loop that will oscillate the first time the valve stiffens or the load shifts. Verdict: not acceptable as it stands. Options: (1) ask what problem the filter is solving — if it is measurement noise, a smaller filter (say 20 ms, costing atan(0.16) = 9°) may be enough; (2) if the noise is genuinely large, accept the filter and re-tune for the slower signal path, which means reducing gain and accepting a lower ωgc; (3) if the noise is at a specific frequency, a notch or a proper anti-aliasing design beats brute-force averaging. The governing principle: filters are never free, and "just add a bit of damping to the transmitter" is a tuning change disguised as a maintenance action.
P7. Convert a series-form controller (Kc' = 2.0, Ti' = 100 s, Td' = 25 s) into ideal parallel form. Why does this matter?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The interaction factor is f = 1 + Td'/Ti' = 1 + 25/100 = 1.25. Then Kc = Kc'·f = 2.0 × 1.25 = 2.50; Ti = Ti'·f = 100 × 1.25 = 125 s; Td = Td'/f = 25/1.25 = 20 s. Why it matters: if you copy the series numbers straight into a controller that implements the ideal form, you get 25% less gain than intended and a differently balanced I and D — the loop will be sluggish and the technician will conclude "the new PLC tunes differently". The reverse mistake (copying ideal settings into a series controller) makes the loop 25% more aggressive than intended, which is the dangerous direction. Note the interaction factor grows with Td/Ti: with Td' = Ti'/2 it reaches 1.5, a 50% discrepancy. Always check three things before transcribing tuning constants between platforms: the form (ideal/parallel, series/interacting, or independent-gains Kp/Ki/Kd), the units (gain versus proportional band PB = 100/Kc; seconds versus minutes; Ti versus repeats-per-minute, which are reciprocals), and whether derivative acts on error or measurement.
P8. A vibration signal at 33 Hz is sampled at 40 Hz by a PLC analogue card with no anti-aliasing filter. What does the controller see? Give two fixes and explain why one of them cannot work.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Nyquist frequency = 40/2 = 20 Hz, and 33 Hz is above it, so the signal aliases to |33 − 40| = 7 Hz. The controller sees a perfectly convincing 7 Hz oscillation that does not exist in the process, and if that frequency falls inside the loop's bandwidth the controller will fight it — modulating the valve and creating a real 7 Hz disturbance from an imaginary one. Fix 1 (correct): fit an analogue low-pass anti-aliasing filter ahead of the ADC, with a corner well below 20 Hz (say 5–8 Hz) so 33 Hz is heavily attenuated before sampling. Fix 2 (also valid): sample faster — at fs > 66 Hz the 33 Hz content is captured honestly and can then be removed digitally. The fix that CANNOT work: a digital filter in the PLC. Once aliasing has occurred, the 33 Hz and the false 7 Hz are mathematically indistinguishable — the information was destroyed at the moment of sampling, and no amount of software can recover it. This is the single most important practical consequence of the sampling theorem, and it is why analogue input cards for fast signals have hardware filters specified in their datasheets. Bonus diagnostic: if a mysterious oscillation frequency has no relationship to the process dynamics but does satisfy |fmachine − k·fs| for some machine frequency, you have found an alias, not a control problem (cross-reference A42's spectra).
P9. A loop shows a sustained oscillation of constant amplitude with a period of 90 s. The PV is a clean sine but the MV moves in visible steps. Diagnose, and explain why halving the controller gain would be the wrong first action.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The tell-tale is the combination: constant-amplitude (not decaying, not growing) oscillation with a stepped MV. A linear tuning-induced oscillation produces a smoothly varying MV and either decays or grows; a limit cycle of fixed amplitude with a stepped output is the classic signature of valve stiction — the integral term winds up until the static friction breaks, the valve jumps past where it was needed, the error reverses, and the cycle repeats. (A sawtooth or square-wave PV would confirm it further; a clean sine can still occur when the process filters the jumps heavily.) Why halving the gain is wrong: stiction is a nonlinear mechanical fault, and reducing gain does not remove it — it changes the cycle amplitude and period, often making the cycle slower and larger, and it degrades the loop's genuine performance for every other disturbance. Worse, it hides the fault: the plant now runs a permanently cycling, permanently degraded loop and nobody raises a work order. Correct sequence: (1) put the loop in manual with a fixed output — if the cycle stops immediately, the process is not oscillating on its own, which points at the controller/valve interaction; (2) perform a valve travel test (step the output in small increments and watch actual stem position) — stiction shows as no movement, then a jump; (3) reduce integral action temporarily to calm production while a work order is raised, and note it as a known defect; (4) repair the valve — packing friction, a failed positioner, or a sticking actuator. Stiction is the most common single cause of poor loop performance in process plants, and it is a maintenance problem masquerading as a tuning problem.
P10. Synthesis. You inherit a reactor temperature loop: bump test gives K = 4.5 °C/%, τ = 200 s, θ = 90 s. It is currently tuned Kc = 1.2, Ti = 60 s, Td = 0, running in a 1 s task with a 5 s transmitter filter, and it cycles with a period of about 6 minutes. The jacket valve spends much of its time above 90% open. Produce a full engineering assessment and plan.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Step 1 — is the tuning even plausible? Controllability θ/τ = 90/200 = 0.45, a genuinely dead-time-limited loop. SIMC with λ = θ = 90 s gives Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)) = 200/(4.5 × 180) = 0.247 and Ti = min(200, 720) = 200 s. The installed tuning is Kc = 1.2 (≈5× too high) with Ti = 60 s (≈3× too fast) — both errors in the aggressive direction, which alone explains a sustained cycle. Step 2 — check the cycle period against theory. The predicted ultimate period for a loop like this is roughly 3–4θ, i.e. 270–360 s; the observed 360 s fits, confirming a tuning-induced oscillation rather than an external disturbance. Verify by putting the loop in manual: the cycle should stop. Step 3 — account for the signal path. The 1 s task is fine (Ts ≤ 0.1τ = 20 s), but the 5 s transmitter filter adds real lag; at the intended crossover (ωgc ≈ 1/(λ+θ) ≈ 0.0056 rad/s) it costs only atan(0.028) ≈ 1.6°, so it is tolerable here — but it should still be reduced to ~1 s and recorded, since it buys little and costs margin. Step 4 — the valve is the real red flag. Sitting above 90% open means the loop is near or at saturation, so (a) it has little authority to increase cooling when it needs to, (b) integral windup is likely — and a windup-driven overshoot cycle can coexist with the tuning problem, and (c) the jacket circuit may be genuinely undersized or fouled. Check anti-windup configuration and the incremental-form implementation, then investigate the cooling capacity: a valve that lives at 90% is a process-design finding, not a tuning finding. Step 5 — the plan, in order. (i) Retune to SIMC λ = θ: Kc = 0.25, Ti = 200 s, Td = 0 (no derivative — the measurement is filtered and this is an exothermic reactor where D on a noisy signal risks valve agitation). (ii) Verify anti-windup and output limits; confirm bumpless transfer and correct (reverse) action. (iii) Reduce the transmitter filter to ~1 s and re-check the response. (iv) Verify robustness: raise Kc 50% to 0.37 and confirm no sustained cycling. (v) Address the dead time — 90 s on a reactor usually means a poorly located or heavily pocketed thermowell, or jacket transport delay; halving θ would let λ halve and the loop roughly double in speed, which is worth more than any further tuning. (vi) Address the cooling capacity so the valve operates in the 40–70% band. (vii) Given θ/τ = 0.45 and an exothermic reaction, evaluate a cascade (reactor temperature → jacket temperature) which puts a fast inner loop on the jacket and dramatically reduces the outer loop's effective dead time — the standard solution for reactor temperature, and the reason cascade is the first topic in the Advanced Process Control module. (viii) Finally, note the safety dimension: an exothermic reactor's high-temperature protection must not depend on this PID at all but on an independent safety instrumented function (A52). Document the model, operating point, λ choice, final settings and filter values with the date.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Four derivations you should be able to produce from memory, a symbol glossary, and the seven mistakes that cost marks and plant.

Derivation 1 · The Overshoot Formula
Goal: %OS = 100·exp(−ζπ/√(1−ζ²)) for a standard second-order step response. Step 1 — the step response. For G(s) = ωn²/(s²+2ζωns+ωn²) with a unit step, partial fractions and inverse transform give: y(t) = 1 − (e−ζωnt/√(1−ζ²))·sin(ωdt + φ), ωd = ωn√(1−ζ²), cos φ = ζ Step 2 — locate the peak. Differentiate and set dy/dt = 0. The algebra collapses because the derivative of the product reduces to a single sine term: dy/dt = (ωn/√(1−ζ²))·e−ζωnt·sin(ωdt) = 0 → sin(ωdt) = 0 → ωdt = 0, π, 2π, … The first maximum is at tp = π/ωd (peak time — derived for free) Step 3 — evaluate y at tp. At ωdt = π: sin(π + φ) = −sin φ = −√(1−ζ²) y(tp) = 1 − (e−ζωnπ/ωd/√(1−ζ²))·(−√(1−ζ²)) = 1 + e−ζωnπ/ωd Step 4 — simplify the exponent. ωn/ωd = 1/√(1−ζ²), so y(tp) = 1 + exp( −ζπ/√(1−ζ²) ) Step 5 — overshoot is the excess above the final value 1: OS = exp( −ζπ/√(1−ζ²) ) ∎ Invert for design (take ln, square, solve for ζ): ln(OS) = −ζπ/√(1−ζ²) → ln²(OS)(1−ζ²) = ζ²π² → ζ = −ln(OS)/√(π² + ln²(OS)) ∎

What it teaches. Two structural facts fall out of the algebra rather than being asserted. First, overshoot depends on ζ ALONE — ωn cancels completely, so scaling a system in time changes its speed but never its overshoot. That is why "%OS" and "ζ" are interchangeable in specifications. Second, peak time depends only on ωd, so overshoot and peak time are set by different parameters and can be specified independently — which is exactly what makes the s-plane design wedge of Page 5 a rectangle-like region rather than a single point. The derivation also shows why ζ = 1 gives precisely zero overshoot: the exponent → −∞ only in the limit, and the underlying sinusoid degenerates, so the peak moves to t = ∞.

Derivation 2 · Why Feedback Divides Sensitivity by (1 + L)
Goal: show that a fractional change in the plant produces a much smaller fractional change in the closed loop. Closed-loop transfer function (unity feedback): T = G/(1 + G) Step 1 — differentiate T with respect to G (quotient rule): dT/dG = [(1+G)·1 − G·1]/(1+G)² = 1/(1+G)² Step 2 — form the RELATIVE (fractional) sensitivity, which is what engineering cares about: dT/T = (dT/dG)·(dG/T) = [1/(1+G)²]·dG·[(1+G)/G] = dG/[G·(1+G)] (dT/T) = [1/(1+G)]·(dG/G) ∎ So the fractional change in the closed-loop gain is the fractional change in the plant, DIVIDED BY (1 + L). The quantity S(s) = 1/(1 + L(s)) is called the SENSITIVITY FUNCTION for exactly this reason. It is simultaneously: · the factor by which plant uncertainty is attenuated, · the transfer function from disturbance to error, · and (with T = L/(1+L)) bound by the identity S + T = 1. Numerical illustration: L = 50, plant gain changes by +40% closed-loop change = 40%/51 = 0.78%

What it teaches. This one derivation is the entire economic case for feedback control, and it explains a paradox beginners notice: how can we control a process nobody has modelled accurately? Because accuracy of the model is divided by the loop gain. But read the identity S + T = 1 carefully — it is a conservation law. Wherever |S| is small (good disturbance rejection and low sensitivity) |T| must be near 1 (good tracking, but also full transmission of measurement noise); wherever |T| is small (noise rejected) |S| approaches 1 (no disturbance attenuation at all). You cannot have both at the same frequency — only at different frequencies. That is why every loop is designed as high loop gain at low frequency, low loop gain at high frequency, with the crossover placed where the compromise hurts least, and why Bode's integral constraints mean pushing |S| down at one frequency pushes it up at another. Feedback does not create performance from nothing; it relocates the difficulty in frequency.

Derivation 3 · Routh's Critical Gain for the Canonical Third-Order Loop
Goal: for L(s) = K/(s(s+a)(s+b)), show Ku = ab(a+b) and ωu = √(ab). Step 1 — characteristic equation s(s+a)(s+b) + K = 0 s(s² + (a+b)s + ab) + K = s³ + (a+b)s² + ab·s + K = 0 Step 2 — Routh array s³ | 1 ab s² | (a+b) K s¹ | [(a+b)·ab − 1·K]/(a+b) s⁰ | K Step 3 — stability conditions (a+b) > 0 ✓ (both poles stable) K > 0 (a+b)·ab − K > 0 → K < ab(a+b) Ku = ab(a+b) ∎ Step 4 — frequency at the limit. At K = Ku the s¹ row vanishes; the auxiliary polynomial is the row above: (a+b)s² + Ku = 0 → s² = −Ku/(a+b) = −ab(a+b)/(a+b) = −ab ωu = √(ab) ∎ Cross-check by substituting s = jω directly into the characteristic equation: −jω³ − (a+b)ω² + jab·ω + K = 0 Real part: K = (a+b)ω² Imaginary part: −ω³ + ab·ω = 0 → ω² = ab → ω = √(ab) ✓ Substituting back: K = (a+b)·ab ✓ Two independent routes, same answer.

What it teaches. Beyond a memorable pair of formulas (worth carrying into an exam: Ku = ab(a+b), ωu = √(ab)), the derivation demonstrates the general technique of substituting s = jω to find where a locus crosses the imaginary axis — separating real and imaginary parts gives two equations in two unknowns (K and ω), which is how every jω-crossing is computed. Note also the physical reading of ωu = √(ab): the oscillation frequency at the stability limit is the geometric mean of the two plant poles, which is why widely separated poles (a ≪ b) produce an ultimate frequency far from either — and why a loop's oscillation period, measured with a ruler, is genuine information about the plant's pole locations, not just about the tuning.

Derivation 4 · Phase Margin as a Dead-Time Budget
Goal: show that the extra pure delay a loop can absorb is Δθ = PM/ωgc. Step 1 — the definitions. At the gain crossover frequency ωgc: |L(jωgc)| = 1 and ∠L(jωgc) = −180° + PM Step 2 — add a pure delay of θextra seconds. The new loop is L'(s) = L(s)·e−θs: |L'(jω)| = |L(jω)|·|e−jωθ| = |L(jω)|·1 → the magnitude curve is UNCHANGED, so ωgc does not move. ∠L'(jωgc) = ∠L(jωgc) − ωgc·θextra (radians) Step 3 — the instability condition. The loop becomes marginally unstable when the phase at the (unmoved) crossover reaches exactly −180°: (−180° + PM) − ωgc·θextra·(180/π) = −180° → PM(degrees) = ωgc·θextra·(180/π) θextra,max = PM(radians) / ωgc ∎ Step 4 — the same idea for a first-order lag (a filter, not a delay). A lag of time constant τf costs atan(ωgcτf) of phase but ALSO reduces gain, so it moves ωgc slightly left — the pure-delay result is therefore CONSERVATIVE (pessimistic) for a filter, which is exactly what you want in a safety budget: phase cost of a filter ≈ atan(ωgc·τf) τf = 0.1/ωgc → 5.7° · τf = 0.5/ωgc → 26.6° · τf = 1/ωgc → 45° Rule of thumb that follows: keep any filter time constant below ~0.1/ωgc and it will cost under 6° — a fair price. At τf = 1/ωgc you have spent 45°, which is most of a well-tuned loop's entire margin.

What it teaches. This turns an abstract stability number into a currency you can spend on site. Phase margin is not a badge of quality; it is a budget in milliseconds, and every element in the signal chain draws on it: scan time, sample-and-hold, transmitter damping, anti-aliasing filters, network latency, PWM period, actuator slew, analyser cycle time. The derivation also explains the asymmetry engineers observe in practice — delays are worse than lags. A lag pays for its phase by also cutting gain (which helps stability), while a pure delay takes the phase for free. That is the formal reason why "move the sensor closer" outranks "add a filter" and why fast loops must live in fast tasks. Keep the table in Step 4 in your head: it converts any proposed filter into degrees before you commit to it.

Seven Exam & Field Traps
  • 1
    Applying the Final Value Theorem to an unstable system. lims→0 s·Y(s) returns a confident, finite, completely wrong answer. Check stability first — always. The same caution applies to steady-state error formulas, which presuppose a stable loop.
  • 2
    Confusing lag with dead time. A lag contributes at most −90° per pole and attenuates; dead time contributes unbounded phase with no attenuation. Modelling 40 s of dead time as an extra 40 s lag makes a hard loop look easy and produces tuning that oscillates.
  • 3
    Treating gain and phase margin as independent guarantees. A loop can show 8 dB and 50° and still pass close to −1 diagonally. Quote the vector margin / Ms when robustness matters, and remember that conditionally stable loops have a lower gain limit too — reducing gain is not always the safe direction.
  • 4
    Mixing controller forms and units. Ideal vs series form differ by f = 1 + Td'/Ti'; Ti (seconds) and repeats-per-minute are reciprocals; gain and proportional band are reciprocals (PB = 100/Kc); minutes vs seconds is a factor of 60. Most "the new controller behaves differently" reports are one of these four.
  • 5
    Forgetting that ζωn may be fixed by the plant. As in WE1, proportional gain sometimes moves poles only vertically, so settling time is untouchable by K. Before promising a settling time, check whether the controller structure can even move the poles in the required direction.
  • 6
    Ignoring the sampled-data reality. Ts must be ≤ 0.1τ; the hold adds Ts/2 of dead time; anti-aliasing must be analogue and before the ADC; and the discrete derivative's noise gain is Td/Ts, so faster sampling makes D noisier, not better.
  • 7
    Tuning a mechanical or valve fault. Stiction, backlash, saturation and resonance are not tuning problems, and gain changes only redistribute the symptom. Put the loop in manual first: if the oscillation persists, the controller is innocent and the work order belongs to maintenance.

Eight-Step Hands-On Ladder
  • 1
    Do a real bump test. On any loop you are allowed to touch, step the MV in manual and extract K, τ and θ by the two-point method. Repeat downward and at a second load. This single skill underpins everything else.
  • 2
    Verify a hand calculation in software. Take WE3, enter L = tf(8, [1 10 16 0]) and run margin(L). Confirm ωgc = 0.485, PM = 72.9°, ωpc = 4, GM = 26 dB. Once your hand method and the software agree, you can trust both.
  • 3
    Simulate the design wedge. Sweep gain on a simple loop, plot the root locus, and watch the step response change as the poles cross the ζ = 0.707 ray. Seeing the two pictures move together is what makes the s-plane intuitive.
  • 4
    Measure a frequency response. Use a relay autotune (or a swept-sine on a bench rig) to find Ku and Tu experimentally, then compare with the value predicted from your FOPDT model. The gap tells you how much the model omitted.
  • 5
    Break a loop deliberately, in simulation. Add dead time until it oscillates and check that instability arrives at the frequency your phase budget predicted (Δθ = PM/ωgc). Nothing else builds respect for latency this quickly.
  • 6
    Implement a discrete PID yourself — in Python, on a microcontroller, or in PLC code (A36). Include the incremental form, derivative filtering, derivative-on-measurement, anti-windup and bumpless transfer. You will never again treat these as optional details.
  • 7
    Demonstrate aliasing. Sample a 33 Hz signal at 40 Hz and watch a 7 Hz ghost appear; then add an RC filter ahead of the sampler and watch it vanish. Ten minutes of bench work that prevents a career of misdiagnosis.
  • 8
    Write a loop-tuning report for one real loop: model, operating point, objective, λ choice, settings, measured margins, a robustness check at +50% gain, and the filter/scan settings recorded. That document is what separates a control engineer from someone who adjusts controllers.
Self-Assessment — Do You Own This Subject?

Foundation

Can you convert a differential equation to a transfer function, read response shape from pole locations, and derive T = G/(1+GH) from scratch?

Analysis

Given a loop transfer function, can you find ωgc, PM, ωpc, GM and the stability range of K by hand — and predict overshoot and settling time from them?

Design

Can you turn "10% overshoot, 4 s settling" into target pole locations, then choose the controller structure and settings that put the poles there and verify the margins?

Practice

Can you bump-test a real loop, tune it by SIMC, budget the phase for scan/filter/network, configure anti-windup and bumpless transfer — and diagnose whether an oscillation is tuning, valve or alias?

When all four are true you can walk up to any loop in a plant, decide in minutes whether its problem is the model, the tuning, the signal path or the hardware, and defend the answer with numbers.
