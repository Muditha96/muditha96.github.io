Cold Open — Four Faults, One Bill, One Missing Subject

THE SITE CORE

A rubber-goods factory in Homagama. Twelve compression-moulding presses, a chilled-water plant across the yard, a screw compressor, a packing hall. The plant runs 06:00 to 22:00, 26 days a month — 416 hours. You are the mechanical engineer. In March the electrical engineer left, and the general manager handed you the brief with the sentence every mechanical engineer in Sri Lanka eventually hears: “It is all machines anyway, no?”

Four things are wrong. You can see three of them on paper before you touch a single terminal, and the fourth is the one that could kill somebody. All four are the same subject wearing four costumes.

 SymptomWhat everybody saysWhat it actually is
F1The CEB bill has a maximum demand charge of LKR 264,000 and nobody can explain it“Electricity is expensive now”Power factor 0.72 — you are billed in kVA and only paid for kW (p. 8)
F2The 22 kW chilled-water pump trips its overload about three times a month“The motor is old, rewind it”6.5 % volt drop in 165 m of 10 mm² cable (p. 10)
F3The packing-line RCD trips six times a month, always when the VFD starts“That RCD is faulty, bypass it”Earth leakage from the drive — and the wrong RCD type (p. 13)
F4Nothing. No symptom at all.“Chiller house is fine”Earth fault loop impedance 3× too high. A fault there holds the enclosure at 124 V for tens of seconds (p. 12)

Read that table again and notice the shape of it. The three faults with symptoms cost money. The fault with no symptom costs a life. That asymmetry is the whole reason electrical engineering has a culture of paperwork, test certificates and periodic inspection that mechanical engineering does not — a bearing tells you it is failing for weeks, and an earth fault loop tells you nothing at all until the day somebody touches the pump body.

💼

The sentence that gets a mechanical engineer promoted into an electrical brief is not “I can fix it.” It is “here is what I measured, here is the limit it must meet, and here is the gap.” That sentence needs exactly the six numbers this primer teaches you to produce.

Energy is billed in kWh. Demand is billed in kVA. That single mismatch is fault F1, and it is the most common avoidable cost in Sri Lankan manufacturing. kWh measures the work you took out of the system. kVA measures the current the system had to be built to carry. They are the same number only when the power factor is 1.

An honest note on this library. Earlier modules — A12 Pneumatics in particular — use a round LKR 45/kWh as a single blended tariff. That was a deliberate simplification for arithmetic that was already heavy. It is not the current published rate, and B01 uses the real time-of-use table instead. When the two disagree, believe the tariff printed on your own bill, and take from every module the method rather than the number.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

The plant's real power at the metered maximum demand is 172.8 kW, and the power factor is 0.72. What is the plant being charged maximum demand on?

240 kVA — you divide, you do not multiply. Power factor is the fraction of the apparent power that does work, so the apparent power is always the larger number: S = P / pf = 172.8 / 0.72 = 240.0 kVA. The bill is 240 × 1,100 = LKR 264,000 a month, of which LKR 73,900 buys nothing at all — it is the charge for current that goes out and comes straight back.

The instinct to multiply is exactly the mechanical instinct: efficiency multiplies. But power factor is not an efficiency, it is a phase angle, and it works the other way round. That distinction is page 8, and it is the single most profitable page in this primer.

The Analogy, Properly — and the Four Places It Breaks

THIS IS NOT A TEACHING CRUTCH CORE

The hydraulic–electrical analogy is not a simplification invented for beginners. It is a formal isomorphism: the differential equations that govern a hydraulic circuit and an electrical circuit are the same equations with the symbols renamed. Control engineers move back and forth across it deliberately, and so does every simulation package you will ever open.

Two rows deserve a pause. The power row is not an analogy at all — pressure times flow and voltage times current both give watts, in the same SI units, and a 22 kW motor driving a 22 kW pump is 22 kW on both sides of the shaft. There is no conversion factor and no fudge. That is the strongest possible evidence that you are looking at one subject and not two.

And the water-hammer row is the gift your background gives you. An unterminated communication cable is water hammer. A pressure wave runs down a pipe, meets a closed valve, and returns; an electrical edge runs down a cable, meets an unmatched end, and returns. The 120 Ω termination resistor that B05 spends a page on is an orifice sized to swallow the wave. You have been sizing those since your first year.

SEEING IT AS ONE CIRCUIT CORE
WHERE THE ANALOGY BREAKS — AND WHY IT MATTERS STRETCH

Most primers hand you the analogy and stop. That is where mechanical engineers get into trouble, because the four places it fails are precisely the four places electricity is dangerous or expensive. Know the boundaries and the analogy becomes a tool; ignore them and it becomes a trap.

⚠ BREAK 1 — the earth is not a tank

Hydraulic return goes to a tank you can see, and if the return line is missing the machine simply does not move. Electrical return goes to the star point of a transformer possibly a kilometre away, and the earth is a parallel path of a few ohms. A missing return does not stop the circuit — it makes the metalwork live. This is fault F4.

⚠ BREAK 2 — there is no reactive power in a pipe

Hydraulic power is p×Q, one number. AC electrical power splits into real (kW), reactive (kvar) and apparent (kVA) because voltage and current are sinusoids that can be out of step. Nothing in steady hydraulics behaves this way. This is fault F1 and it is on your bill.

⚠ BREAK 3 — the speed is absurd

A pressure wave travels at roughly 1,400 m/s in oil. An electrical edge travels at about 200,000,000 m/s — 140,000 times faster. Anything you think of as instantaneous in hydraulics has structure in electricity, and a 2 m cable stub can be a real problem. This is most of B05.

⚠ BREAK 4 — a human is not a leak

A hydraulic leak wastes oil. A human touching a live conductor becomes a 1,000 Ω branch to earth carrying 230 mA — five times the fibrillation threshold — while the system carries on as though nothing happened, because 230 mA is invisible to a 63 A breaker. This is fault F3 and page 13.

  • 1Use the analogy for intuition, never for a limit. It will correctly tell you which way a quantity moves and roughly how much. It will not give you a disconnection time, a cable size or a touch voltage. Those come from tables and from measurement.
  • 2Pressure and voltage are both differences. “The pressure at that port” always means relative to atmosphere; “the voltage at that terminal” always means relative to something, usually earth or neutral. When an electrician says “230 V to earth,” they are quoting a gauge pressure.
  • 3Current is the dangerous quantity; voltage is only what drives it. Exactly as flow does the work in hydraulics and pressure only enables it. A 10 kV static shock from a nylon overall carries microamps and hurts; 230 V through a wet hand carries 300 mA and stops a heart.

❓ Quick check. Your hydraulic instinct says a bigger accumulator smooths pressure ripple better. What is the electrical statement of the same fact, and where do you meet it in a factory?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: A bigger capacitor smooths voltage ripple better — I = C dV/dt, so for a given ripple current the voltage wobble falls as C rises, exactly as Q = Ch dp/dt says for the accumulator. You meet it as the DC-bus capacitor inside every VFD (A08) and in every switch-mode power supply (A28). And the analogy keeps paying: an accumulator that has lost its nitrogen charge lets pressure ripple through, and a dried-out DC-bus capacitor lets voltage ripple through — both are the commonest failure in their respective machines, and both are diagnosed by measuring the ripple.

Ohm and Kirchhoff — Two Laws You Already Use Daily

THE FOUR QUANTITIES, DEFINED PROPERLY CORE

Charge  Q [C]

The fluid itself. One coulomb is about 6.24×1018 electrons. You will almost never use it directly — but it is what is actually moving, and everything else is defined from it.

Current  I [A]

Flow rate of charge. One amp = one coulomb per second. Exactly analogous to l/min. This is the quantity that heats cables and stops hearts.

Voltage  V [V]

Energy per unit charge — one volt = one joule per coulomb. In fluid terms, pressure is energy per unit volume. Same idea, different denominator.

Resistance  R [Ω]

How much push you must spend to get flow. One ohm = one volt per amp. An orifice, with one crucial difference below.

The one crucial difference. A hydraulic orifice is not linear — flow through a sharp-edged orifice goes as Q ∝ √Δp, which is why you spent so long with discharge coefficients. An electrical resistor is linear: I ∝ V, exactly, over an enormous range. This makes electrical circuit arithmetic dramatically easier than fluid circuit arithmetic, and it is the reason a page of electrical algebra replaces a chapter of fluid mechanics. Take the win.

OHM'S LAW V = I R I = V / R R = V / I Fluid twin: Δp = Rh Q (laminar / linear-resistance regime) Worked, from the plant: the 22 kW chilled-water pump cable is 165 m of 10 mm² copper copper at 70 °C: ρ = 0.0216 Ω·mm²/m R = ρ L / A = 0.0216 × 165 / 10 = 0.356 Ω per conductor at 44.4 A that conductor drops V = 44.4 × 0.356 = 15.8 V and there are three of them carrying current.

Notice how ordinary that calculation is. It is the same shape as working out the pressure loss along a pipe run — a property of the material, times a length, divided by an area. ρL/A for a cable and 128μL/πd⁴ for a laminar pipe are cousins. If you can size a pipe you can size a cable, and page 10 proves it.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A 230 V socket circuit feeds a 2 kW kettle through a cable with 0.4 Ω total resistance. Someone plugs a second identical kettle into the same circuit. What happens to the voltage at the sockets?

It sags by about 7 V and both kettles slow down. One kettle draws 2000/230 = 8.7 A and drops 8.7 × 0.4 = 3.5 V in the cable, so the socket sits at 226.5 V. Two kettles draw about 17.4 A and drop about 7.0 V — the sockets sit near 223 V, and each kettle now delivers (223/230)² = 94 % of its rated power.

This is the exact mechanism of fault F2, scaled down to a kitchen. The supply is a source in series with a resistance you cannot see, and every extra amp costs you volts at the far end. In fluid terms it is completely familiar: open a second branch and the pressure at both drops, because the shared line was never a perfect source. The only reason it surprises anyone in electricity is that we draw the supply as a clean symbol with no internal resistance — a lie of the diagram, not of the physics.

Power, Energy and Heat — Where the Money Actually Goes

THE THREE FORMS OF ONE EQUATION CORE

Electrical power is P = VI. Substituting Ohm's law gives two more forms, and choosing the right one is most of the skill — because each form tells you which quantity you can actually change.

P = V I use when you know the supply and the current P = I² R use for losses in a conductor ◄ the important one P = V² / R use for a fixed resistive load on a known supply Energy: E = P t , billed as kWh (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ) The plant's blended tariff: LKR 13.08 per kWh so one continuous watt, wasted, for 416 h/month costs 1 W × 416 h = 0.416 kWh = LKR 5.44 per month, per watt.

Burn P = I²R into your memory. The square is everything. It says that cable losses do not scale with load — they scale with the square of load. A cable at 80 % of its current runs at 0.8² = 64 % of its losses; a cable at 120 % runs at 144 %. It is the reason a slightly overloaded cable gets hot alarmingly quickly, and the reason the single most effective way to cut copper losses is to cut current, not resistance.

❓ Quick check. A cable is running at exactly its rated current and is comfortably warm. Production asks to add 15 % more load. What happens to the heat generated, and roughly what does that do to conductor temperature rise?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Heat generated goes as I², so it rises by 1.15² = 1.32 — 32 % more heat, not 15 %. Since the temperature rise above ambient is roughly proportional to the heat dissipated, the rise above ambient also goes up about 32 %. A cable designed for a 40 °C rise (30 °C ambient to 70 °C PVC limit) now wants a 53 °C rise and sits near 83 °C — above the insulation limit, where PVC begins to soften and its life falls sharply. A widely used rule of thumb is that insulation life roughly halves for every 8–10 °C of over-temperature, so a 13 °C excess costs you something like two-thirds of the cable's design life. “Only 15 % more” is never only 15 % more.

The Closed Loop — and Why Earth Is Not a Return Path

THE MISCONCEPTION THAT KILLS PEOPLE CORE

Almost everyone who learns electricity informally acquires the belief that current flows “to earth” and is consumed there, the way oil drains to a tank. It is a comfortable picture and it is completely wrong, and the difference between the picture and the physics is the difference between a safe installation and a fatal one.

Current flows in a loop. It leaves the source, passes through the load, and returns to the same source. In a factory that source is the secondary winding of the distribution transformer, and the current returns to its star point — the physical junction where the three windings meet. Nothing is consumed. Nothing drains. The earth is not a destination; it is a possible alternative conductor back to that same star point, and a poor one.

THE ONLY THING THAT MAKES EARTH SPECIAL The transformer's star point is deliberately bonded to earth. That single bond is what makes earth a return path at all. Cut that bond and the earth stops being a return path entirely, and touching a live wire while standing on wet ground does nothing — because there is no loop back to the source. This is exactly how an IT system works, and why isolation transformers are used on machine control circuits. It is not a safety accident, it is a design choice.

Sit with that. In the standard TN system used across Sri Lankan industry, the reason you get a shock standing on the floor is not that electricity likes the ground. It is that somebody, correctly and deliberately, connected the transformer star point to the ground — so the ground under your feet and the neutral of the supply are the same electrical node, and your body bridges live to that node. Change that one decision and the entire hazard changes character.

A 400 V motor's winding chafes through and touches the frame. The frame is earthed through a good CPC back to the transformer, total loop 0.3 Ω. What roughly happens?

About 770 A, and the breaker clears it in milliseconds. The fault is line-to-earth, so the driving voltage is the phase voltage 230 V, not the 400 V line value: I = 230 / 0.3 = 767 A. That is far above any magnetic trip setting, so the device operates in its instantaneous region and the frame is live for perhaps 10–20 milliseconds.

This is the earth conductor doing exactly the job it exists for. It did not “drain” the fault — it made the fault enormous, and enormous faults are the only ones a breaker can recognise. Option 1 is the intuition that kills: a small fault current is the dangerous case, because nothing trips and the frame stays live indefinitely. That is precisely the chiller-house situation in fault F4, where the loop is 1.10 Ω instead of 0.3 Ω and the current is only 209 A.

DC, AC and RMS — the Equivalent Steady Value

WHY THE GRID IS AC AT ALL CORE

Direct current goes one way. Alternating current reverses — in Sri Lanka, 50 times a second, so the current in your cable changes direction 100 times per second and the electrons themselves barely move a fraction of a millimetre. The energy travels; the charge mostly vibrates.

The grid is AC for one reason, and it is a good one: a transformer only works on changing current. Transformers let you send power at 132 kV and 100 A instead of 400 V and 33,000 A, and since losses go as I²R, that is a factor of 330² ≈ 109,000 reduction in transmission loss for the same power. Nothing else in the whole subject buys that much. Everything else about AC — the phase angles, the reactive power, the power factor on your bill — is the price you pay for that one enormous advantage.

DC — where you actually meet it

Battery systems, the DC bus inside every VFD (A08), 24 V control circuits, solar arrays before the inverter, PLC I/O (A07), EV traction (A38). More of a modern plant is DC than most people realise.

AC — where it dominates

The supply, every motor of any size, every transformer, every heater and every distribution board. If it is more than a few kilowatts and it is not inside a drive, it is AC.

RMS — THE IDEA YOU ALREADY USE CORE

An AC voltage is a sine wave. Its average over a cycle is zero — it is above the line as much as below it. That is useless for engineering, so we need a single number that says “this wobbling thing heats a resistor as much as such-and-such a steady DC would.” That number is the root mean square, and you have met the same construction before: it is exactly how an equivalent stress amplitude, an equivalent steady load, or an r.m.s. surface roughness is defined.

WHY IT IS root-mean-SQUARE AND NOT JUST AVERAGE Heating goes as the SQUARE of current: p(t) = i(t)² R So the fair average is the average of the square: mean power = R × mean( i(t)² ) Define Irms so that mean power = R × Irms² therefore Irms = √( mean of i² ) — root, of the mean, of the square. For a pure sine wave: Vrms = Vpeak / √2 = 0.7071 Vpeak So Sri Lanka's 230 V socket: peak = 230 × √2 = 325.3 V peak-to-peak = 650.5 V and the 400 V line-to-line peaks at 565.7 V The insulation in your panel must survive 566 V, not 400 V. That is not pedantry — it is why components are rated 690 V.

The crucial word is “for a pure sine wave”. The √2 factor is a property of the shape, not of AC in general. A square wave has Vrms = Vpeak. A triangle has Vpeak/√3. And the current drawn by a VFD, an LED driver or a switch-mode power supply is not remotely sinusoidal — which is why a cheap multimeter, which measures average and multiplies by 1.11 assuming a sine, can under-read a distorted current by 30 % or more. That is the entire practical case for buying a true-RMS meter, and it is page 14.

WHY A MOTOR'S CURRENT LAGS ITS VOLTAGE CORE

An induction motor is, electrically, a big inductor with a resistor bolted on. It must draw a magnetising current to build the rotating magnetic field before it can produce any torque at all — and that magnetising current is 90 ° out of step with the voltage, because that is what an inductor does. The magnetising current does no work; it builds a field in one quarter-cycle and collapses it in the next, pushing the energy back out to the supply.

Here is the sharpest way to see it. Think of an unloaded flywheel on the end of a crank. You push it up to speed, it gives the energy back as it slows, and over a full cycle you have done no net work — but your arm still got tired, and the connecting rod still had to be sized for the peak force. The cable is the connecting rod, and the kVA on your bill is the size of rod the utility had to install. That is reactive power, and it is why you are billed for something you did not consume.

  • 1A motor's power factor is worst when it is lightly loaded. The magnetising current is roughly constant — it depends on voltage and frequency, not on load — while the working current falls with the load. A motor at 25 % load may show a power factor of 0.4. Oversized motors are the single largest cause of poor plant power factor, and correcting the power factor treats the symptom while the oversized motor keeps causing it.
  • 2Reactance depends on frequency, resistance does not. This is why a VFD's output at 5 Hz sees a motor with one-tenth the reactance it has at 50 Hz, and why the drive must reduce voltage in proportion to frequency (constant V/f) or the motor will saturate and draw enormous current. That single sentence is the heart of A08.
  • 3An inductor's stored energy has to go somewhere when you open the circuit. V = L dI/dt — force dI/dt to be huge by snapping a switch open and V becomes huge, which is an arc across the contacts. It is water hammer, exactly: slam a valve and the moving column's momentum turns into a pressure spike. This is why every relay and contactor coil in a control panel has a flyback diode or RC snubber across it. (A51)
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A 400 V contactor coil carrying 0.15 A is switched off by a PLC output. The coil's inductance is about 2 H and the contacts open in roughly 1 ms. What voltage appears across the opening contacts?

Hundreds of volts, and with a genuinely fast semiconductor switch, thousands. V = L dI/dt = 2 × 0.15/0.001 = 300 V for a 1 ms opening — and a PLC transistor output switches in microseconds, so the same coil against a 10 µs edge would try to produce 2 × 0.15/0.00001 = 30,000 V. In practice the arc or the output's avalanche limits it, but that is exactly the point: something has to absorb the energy, and if you did not specify what, it will be the switching device.

Mechanically this is water hammer with a different fluid. The stored kinetic energy of the column has to go somewhere when you shut the valve; the stored magnetic energy ½LI² has to go somewhere when you open the contact. You fit a surge vessel in the pipe and a flyback diode across the coil, and the two are the same engineering decision. Unsnubbed relay coils are the commonest cause of destroyed PLC outputs and the commonest source of electrical noise on a control network — B05's fault 3 traces back to exactly this.

Power Factor and the CEB Bill — Fault F1 Resolved

THREE KINDS OF POWER, ONE TRIANGLE CORE

In a DC circuit there is one power. In an AC circuit there are three, and they are related by a right-angled triangle you can draw on the back of an envelope. This is the single most commercially valuable page in the primer, and the arithmetic is trivial once the picture is right.

THE THREE POWERS P real / active power kW the part that does work and heats things Q reactive power kvar the part that sloshes back and forth S apparent power kVA what the cable and the utility must be sized for S² = P² + Q² Pythagoras. That is the whole of it. pf = P / S = cos φ power factor — the cosine of the phase angle Q = P tan φ the form you actually use to size a capacitor THE PLANT, AS FOUND P = 172.8 kW , pf = 0.72 → φ = arccos 0.72 = 43.9° S = P/pf = 172.8/0.72 = 240.0 kVA Q = P tanφ = 172.8 × 0.9639 = 166.6 kvar demand charge = 240.0 × 1,100 = LKR 264,000 per month TO REACH pf 0.95 Qnew = 172.8 × tan(arccos 0.95) = 172.8 × 0.3287 = 56.8 kvar capacitor needed = 166.6 − 56.8 = 109.8 kvar → specify a 110 kvar bank

Read the triangle geometrically and it stops being algebra. The horizontal side is the work you actually did — the capacitor cannot change it and does not try. All the capacitor does is supply that sloshing energy locally, from the switchboard, instead of dragging it up and down the cable from the transformer sixty metres away. The hypotenuse — the current the cable and the utility actually carry — shortens, and the hypotenuse is what you are billed for.

THE FOUR THINGS NOBODY TELLS YOU ABOUT CORRECTION STRETCH
  • 1You will not achieve your target power factor. A capacitor bank switches in discrete steps — typically six or eight stages — and a controller with a deliberate delay to avoid hunting. The metered maximum demand is the highest 15-minute average in the month, and that block very often catches a moment when the bank was one stage behind a load change. Design for 0.95 and budget on 0.92. In this plant that difference alone is LKR 5,969 per month of promised saving that never arrives.
  • 2Capacitors and harmonics are a dangerous combination. A capacitor's reactance falls with frequency, so it is a low-impedance path for every harmonic in the plant — and the bank plus the supply transformer's inductance forms a resonant circuit. If that resonance lands near the 5th or 7th harmonic (250 or 350 Hz), currents can be amplified several-fold and capacitors fail within months. With more than about 20 % of the load on drives, specify detuned reactors (usually 7 % or 14 %). See A56 before you buy anything.
  • 3Never fit fixed capacitors on a lightly loaded plant. At night, with the plant off and the bank still connected, the capacitors overcorrect and the power factor goes leading — which raises the voltage, stresses insulation and can be penalised too. This is what automatic (APFC) control exists to prevent, and it is why fixed correction belongs at individual large motors and nowhere else.
  • 4Correction does not reduce your kWh. Not by a meaningful amount. It reduces the current in the plant's own cables, so it does shave a little I²R loss internally — but the meter measures kW, and kW is the side of the triangle the capacitor does not touch. Anybody promising a 15 % energy saving from power-factor correction is either confused or selling. The saving is on the demand charge, and only on the demand charge.

Three Phase in One Page — Why √3 Keeps Appearing

THREE PUMPS ON ONE CRANKSHAFT CORE

A single-phase supply delivers power in pulses — twice per cycle it passes through zero, and a single-phase motor's torque pulsates with it. Three-phase is three supplies, each 120 ° apart, and their total instantaneous power is perfectly constant. You have built this machine: it is a three-cylinder pump with the cranks at 120 °, which delivers smooth flow where a single cylinder delivers pulses.

WHERE √3 COMES FROM Two phases 120° apart, each of magnitude V. The voltage BETWEEN them is the vector difference: |VL1 − VL2| = 2 V sin(120°/2) = 2 V sin 60° = 2 V (√3/2) = √3 V So with 230 V from each line to neutral: line-to-line = √3 × 230 = 398.4 V — called "400 V" and written 400/230 V THE THREE FORMULAE YOU WILL USE FOREVER S = √3 × Vline × Iline apparent power, kVA P = √3 × Vline × Iline × cosφ real power, kW I = P / (√3 × Vline × cosφ × η) the current a MOTOR draws Worked — the chilled-water pump 22 kW shaft, efficiency 0.90, power factor 0.85 input electrical power = 22 / 0.90 = 24.44 kW apparent power S = 24.44 / 0.85 = 28.76 kVA I = 28,760 / (√3 × 400) = 41.5 A ◄ this is the number every calculation on page 10 uses.

Do not skip the efficiency. A “22 kW motor” means 22 kW at the shaft — it is a mechanical rating, exactly like a pump's duty. The electrical input is larger by the efficiency, and the apparent power larger again by the power factor. Engineers who forget this undersize cables by 20 % and cannot work out why. Your mechanical instinct is already correct here: nobody sizes a pump's driver on its hydraulic output either.

A 6 kA domestic MCB fitted in an industrial main board will weld its contacts shut and turn into a bomb. The rating is stamped on the front (Icu, Ics) and it is not optional. Prospective fault current must be calculated or measured at every board.
  • 2Discrimination (selectivity) — a fault on one machine should trip that machine's device and nothing upstream. It is the electrical version of designing a shear pin to fail before the gearbox. Achieved by ratios of rating (roughly 1.6:1 between successive devices) and by deliberate time grading in larger installations. A plant where a single motor fault drops the whole shed has no discrimination, and the fix is design, not bigger breakers.
  • 3Motor protection is a separate specialism. An MCB protects the cable; it does not properly protect a motor. Motors need a thermal overload matched to full-load current, and for anything valuable a proper motor-protection relay adding phase failure, unbalance, stall and earth fault. The rule to remember: the short-circuit device protects the cable, the overload protects the motor, and they are two different settings. (A51, A55)
  • 4Arc flash is the hazard mechanical engineers underestimate most. A short circuit inside a panel releases enormous energy as an arc — temperatures around 19,000 °C, a pressure wave, and molten metal. It does not require contact with a live part; opening the door of an energised board at the wrong moment is enough. This is why live working is controlled, why arc-rated PPE exists, and why “just have a quick look with it running” is the most dangerous sentence in a plant.
💼

If you take one habit from this page: whenever you specify or replace a protective device, write down four numbers, not one — rating, curve type, breaking capacity, and the resulting maximum Zs. Engineers who quote only “a 63 amp breaker” are the reason installations drift out of compliance.

❓ Quick check. A 63 A type C MCB keeps tripping when a large machine starts. A contractor suggests changing it to a 63 A type D. What has he solved, what has he broken, and what should you check before agreeing?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Solved: the magnetic threshold moves from 630 A to 1,260 A, so the starting inrush no longer trips it. Broken: the maximum permitted earth fault loop impedance halves, from 230/630 = 0.365 Ω to 230/1260 = 0.183 Ω. If the circuit only just complied before, it certainly does not now — and the failure is silent, because the circuit works perfectly until somebody has an earth fault.

Check before agreeing: measure Zs at the far end of that circuit with a loop tester. If it exceeds 0.183 Ω, the type D is only acceptable if you also add an RCD to provide the disconnection. Better still, ask why the inrush is so large: a soft starter or a VFD removes the inrush at source and leaves the protection design intact — and usually pays for itself in mechanical shock loading avoided on the driven machine, which is your territory.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A remote pump house is fed from a TT supply — its own earth electrode, measured at 42 Ω. It is protected by a 32 A type C MCB and no RCD. What is the fault current if a live conductor touches the pump frame?

Two facts worth carrying. First, a Sri Lankan plant is a humid environment: body impedance figures for “dry” conditions are optimistic for someone who has been working in a 32 °C process hall for six hours. Second, the current path matters — hand-to-hand across the chest is far more dangerous than hand-to-same-side-foot, which is why the old advice to keep one hand in your pocket while testing is not folklore but a deliberate reduction of the heart current factor.

HOW AN RCD WORKS — A BALANCE, NOT A THRESHOLD CORE

That is a beautiful piece of engineering and it is worth admiring. The RCD passes the line and neutral conductors through a common magnetic core; in a healthy circuit their fields cancel exactly, whatever the load. Any current that leaves by a third route — the earth conductor, a person, damp insulation — breaks the cancellation, and a tiny secondary winding sees it. It is mass-balance instrumentation: flow in minus flow out equals leak, exactly as you would find a buried pipe leak with two flow meters.

⚠ THE BLINDING EFFECT

A VFD's rectifier can produce a smooth DC residual current under fault. Smooth DC does not just go undetected by a Type AC or Type A device — it magnetically saturates the toroidal core, so the RCD then fails to detect the AC residual currents it was designed for. The device is still there, still closed, still showing green, and it has stopped protecting anybody. A silently disabled RCD is worse than no RCD, because everyone believes it is working.

⚠ THE TEMPTATION

Six nuisance trips a month is genuinely disruptive, and bypassing the RCD makes them stop instantly. It is one of the most common unsafe acts in industry precisely because it is so effective at removing the symptom. The correct response to a nuisance-tripping RCD is never removal — it is find out where the leakage is coming from, because leakage is a real measurement telling you something real.

WHERE THE LEAKAGE ACTUALLY COMES FROM A VFD switches its output at a few kHz with very fast edges. The screened motor cable has capacitance from each core to the screen — typically 150–300 pF per metre. Every switching edge pushes a current through that capacitance to earth: i = C dv/dt 40 m of screened cable at ~250 pF/m → ~10 nF per core edges of ~540 V in ~100 ns → dv/dt ≈ 5.4 × 10⁹ V/s The peaks are enormous but very short. What the RCD integrates is the RMS, and the practical rule of thumb is roughly 0.5 to 1 mA of earth leakage per metre of screened motor cable at a 4 kHz carrier — so 40 m gives 20 to 40 mA. A 30 mA RCD is being asked to ignore a signal the same size as the one it exists to detect. It cannot, and it should not.
  • 1Fix chosen, part 1 — change the type. Replace with a Type B RCD, which is what IEC 60364 and the drive manufacturer's own manual require downstream of a three-phase drive. Cost is several times a Type A, and it is not optional.
  • 2Fix chosen, part 2 — reduce the leakage at source. Drop the carrier frequency from 8 kHz to 4 kHz (halves the leakage; costs a little more audible motor noise), and shorten the motor cable by moving the drive to the machine. Both are free or nearly free.
  • 3Fix chosen, part 3 — put the RCD in the right place. A 30 mA RCD directly upstream of a drive is poor design. Use a 300 mA Type B for fire protection at the drive, and reserve 30 mA devices for the socket outlets and portable equipment where a person can realistically make contact. Protection should be selected for the hazard it addresses, not applied uniformly.
  • 4Test buttons are not optional and not sufficient. The button proves the mechanism releases; it does not measure the trip current or the trip time. Both should be measured with an RCD tester at commissioning and periodically. And the test button on a blinded Type A device will still work perfectly — which is exactly how false confidence is manufactured.

❓ Quick check. A 30 mA RCD protects a distribution board serving twelve machines, each with a few milliamps of normal leakage. It trips intermittently for no obvious reason. What is the likely cause and what is the correct fix?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Cumulative standing leakage. Twelve machines each leaking 2–3 mA through EMC filters, cable capacitance and switch-mode supplies gives 24–36 mA of perfectly normal leakage, and an RCD is permitted to trip anywhere between 50 % and 100 % of its rating — so a 30 mA device may legitimately trip at 15 mA. The board is sitting on the edge, and any small extra pushes it over.

The correct fix is to divide the board, giving each group of machines its own RCD, rather than fitting a larger one. Replacing the 30 mA with a 100 mA device makes the tripping stop and removes personal-shock protection from every circuit downstream. This is the general principle: when an RCD trips because of accumulated healthy leakage, split the circuits; when it trips because of a fault, find the fault. Increasing the rating is almost never the answer, and a leakage clamp meter tells you which of the two you have in about five minutes.

THE FIVE INSTRUMENTS AND WHAT EACH IS ACTUALLY FOR CORE

True-RMS multimeter

Voltage, resistance, continuity. Must be true-RMS and must be CAT III 600 V or CAT IV for use on a distribution board — the category rating describes its ability to survive a transient without exploding in your hand, and it is not a marketing number.

Clamp meter

Current without breaking the circuit — the single most useful measurement in a plant. Clamp all three phases together and you read the imbalance, exactly as an RCD does. A leakage-capable clamp resolves milliamps and diagnoses fault F3 in minutes.

Loop impedance tester

Measures Ze and Zs directly by drawing a controlled test current. The only way to know fault F4 exists. No other instrument and no calculation from drawings can substitute for it.

Insulation resistance tester

Applies 500 V or 1 kV DC and measures leakage — the standard test for a suspect motor or cable. Isolate first, and discharge after: the capacitance you just charged will hold that voltage.

Two-pole voltage indicator

Proves dead. Not a multimeter — a multimeter on the wrong range, with a flat battery or a broken lead, reads zero volts on a live conductor and reads it very convincingly. A two-pole tester is designed so its failure modes are visible.

Thermal camera

Finds loose terminals, unbalanced phases and overloaded cables from two metres away, with the panel closed. The most effective non-contact electrical diagnostic there is. (A41)

SAFE ISOLATION — THE SEQUENCE THAT DOES NOT VARY CORE

This is a procedure, not a preference, and its steps are in this order for reasons that have all been paid for by somebody. It is the electrical equivalent of the lockout/tagout you already apply to stored pneumatic and hydraulic energy — and it is the same discipline, extended to a form of stored energy you cannot see or hear.

  • 1Identify the correct circuit and the correct isolation point. Labelling in real plants is unreliable; verify by operation, not by the sticker.
  • 2Isolate — open the isolator, and make sure it is a device intended for isolation, with a visible break or a positive indication. A contactor is not an isolator: it can be closed by a control signal from anywhere.
  • 3Secure — lock off with your own padlock and apply your own tag. One person, one lock, one key, and the key stays in your pocket. If several people work on the circuit, several locks.
  • 4Prove the tester works on a known live source or a proving unit. Before you use it.
  • 5Test for dead — all combinations: each line to earth, each line to neutral, and every line to every other line. Not just the one you think is live.
  • 6Prove the tester still works on the known source again. This step catches the tester that failed between steps 4 and 5, which is exactly the failure that kills people.
  • 7Discharge and earth where stored energy exists — capacitor banks, VFD DC buses, long cables, PV arrays. A VFD's DC bus holds a lethal charge for several minutes after isolation, and the manual states the waiting time. Honour it.
⚠ THE BACKFEED YOU DID NOT EXPECT

Isolating the supply does not always make a panel dead. Control circuits are often fed from a separate supply; interlocks can be energised from another machine; a UPS or a rooftop PV inverter can backfeed; and a large motor coasting down is briefly a generator. “I switched it off” and “it is dead” are different claims, and only step 5 turns one into the other.

⚠ THE MECHANICAL ENGINEER'S BLIND SPOT

You are trained to make a system safe by removing the motion — block the ram, chock the load, bleed the accumulator. Electrical energy has no equivalent visible cue: a dead panel and a live panel look identical, sound identical and feel identical until the instant they do not. The whole of safe isolation exists to replace a sense you do not have. Treat the procedure with the respect you would give a pressure test.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You isolate a VFD at its main switch, wait ten seconds, and open the enclosure to change a fan. Your two-pole tester reads zero volts on the incoming terminals. Are you safe to work?

No. The DC bus is the hazard, and you did not test it. A VFD rectifies the incoming AC to a DC bus of roughly 400 × √2 = 566 V, smoothed by large electrolytic capacitors. Those capacitors store ½CV² — for a few millifarads at 566 V that is hundreds of joules, enough to be lethal — and they discharge through a bleed resistor over several minutes, not seconds. Every drive manual states a minimum waiting time, typically 5 to 15 minutes, and it is on a warning label on the cover.

Your test read zero because you tested the incoming AC terminals, which genuinely were dead. The energy was sitting somewhere you did not look. This is the electrical version of bleeding the pump line and forgetting the accumulator — a mistake you would never make in hydraulics, because there you can see the accumulator on the drawing. Learn where the stored energy lives in an electrical system, and the discipline you already have transfers intact.

EXAM TRAPS — THE MISTAKES THAT COST MARKS AND MONEY CORE
⚠ TRAP 1 — multiplying by power factor

To get kVA from kW you divide by the power factor. Multiplying gives a smaller apparent power, which is comfortingly wrong, and it is the single commonest arithmetic error in this subject. Check: S must always be ≥ P.

⚠ TRAP 2 — forgetting motor efficiency

A 22 kW motor takes 24.4 kW electrical input, not 22. The plate is a shaft rating. Skipping this undersizes cables and protection by roughly 10 %, and the error is invisible until it is not.

⚠ TRAP 3 — using 400 V for an earth fault

A line-to-earth fault is driven by the phase voltage, U0 = 230 V, not the line value. Using 400 V overstates the fault current by 74 % and turns a failing circuit into a passing one on paper.

⚠ TRAP 4 — sizing a cable on current alone

Test 1 of four. Fault F2 passed the current test comfortably and failed on volt drop; fault F4 passed both and failed on loop impedance. All four tests, every time.

⚠ TRAP 5 — changing a curve without recomputing Zs

B → C halves the permitted loop impedance; C → D halves it again. Swapping a curve to stop nuisance tripping can silently disable automatic disconnection on a circuit that used to comply.

⚠ TRAP 6 — treating an RCD trip as a device fault

An RCD that trips is reporting a measurement. Find the leakage. Raising the rating or bypassing it removes the report and keeps the hazard — and in the VFD case, the wrong RCD type is silently blind long before it ever nuisance-trips.

⚠ TRAP 7 — assuming Ze instead of measuring it

The external loop impedance is whatever the utility's network happens to be at your site, and it changes when they change a transformer or a feeder. Assumed values in a design must be replaced by measured values at commissioning — and re-measured periodically.

⚠ TRAP 8 — averaging meters on distorted currents

A non-true-RMS clamp under-reads a VFD supply current by 25–30 %. Since heating goes as I², a 28 % under-read means a cable running at nearly twice the loss you calculated.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

The general manager reads your report and asks: “If we only have budget for one item this quarter, which one?” What do you answer?

F4 — and the reasoning matters more than the answer. F1, F2 and F3 are investments: they compete with every other use of capital and it is entirely reasonable for a general manager to defer them. F4 is not an investment. It is an installation that does not comply and cannot protect a person, and deferring it is a decision to accept a specific, identified risk of electrocution — a decision no engineer should make on a manager's behalf by staying quiet.

At LKR 145,000 it is also the cheapest item on the list, which is why the correct presentation puts it above the financial table with no payback column at all. Then, having settled it, you can have a genuinely useful commercial conversation about the other three — and F2, at a 4.7-month payback, is the right commercial answer among those.

This is the habit that separates an engineer from a technician: knowing which items are negotiable and which are not, and structuring the document so the difference cannot be missed.

PROBLEM SET — TEN PROBLEMS, ALL FROM THE SAME PLANT CORE

Do these with a pencil before revealing anything. Every one uses only what is on the previous fifteen pages, and together they are the working vocabulary of an industrial electrical brief. Blended tariff LKR 13.08/kWh, demand charge LKR 1,100/kVA, 416 running hours a month.

P1 — full-load current. A 45 kW motor, efficiency 0.93, power factor 0.86, on a 400 V three-phase supply. What is its full-load line current?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: S = 45 / (0.93 × 0.86) = 56.26 kVA, then I = 56,260 / (√3 × 400) = 81.2 A. Both divisions matter: forgetting efficiency gives 75.6 A, a 7 % undersize that propagates into the cable, the overload setting and the protective device.

P2 — volt drop. That motor is 120 m from the board on 35 mm² copper (1.1 mV/A/m). Does it comply with the 5 % limit?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: ΔV = 1.1 × 81.2 × 120 / 1000 = 10.72 V, which is 10.72/400 = 2.68 % — comfortably compliant. Note it also leaves room for the upstream sub-main's own drop, since the 5 % is measured from the origin of the installation, not per cable.

P3 — power-factor correction. A neighbouring plant runs 340 kW at power factor 0.79. Size the capacitor bank for 0.95 and value the saving on tariff I-2.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Q₁ = 340 × tan(arccos 0.79) = 340 × 0.7763 = 263.9 kvar; Q₂ = 340 × 0.3287 = 111.8 kvar; bank = 152.1 kvar, so specify 150 or 160 kvar. Demand falls from 430.4 to 357.9 kVA, a saving of 72.5 × 1,100 = LKR 79,734 per month. Budget on 0.92 achieved and it is nearer LKR 67,000 — quote that figure.

P4 — fault loop. A 90 m circuit, 16 mm² line and 10 mm² CPC, protected by a 40 A type C MCB. Measured Ze = 0.25 Ω. Does it comply, and how much margin is there?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: R₁+R₂ = 90 × (1.35 + 2.16)/1000 = 0.316 Ω, so Zs = 0.25 + 0.316 = 0.566 Ω. Limit: 230 / (10×40) = 0.575 Ω. It complies — by 1.6 %. That is not a pass in any practical sense: a loop tester's own uncertainty is several percent, conductor resistance rises with temperature, and the utility's Ze can change without notice. Treat any margin under about 20 % as a failure and either upsize the CPC or add an RCD.

P5 — cable losses in money. What does the P1/P2 cable cost you in I²R losses per month? Copper at 70 °C is 0.0216 Ω·mm²/m.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: R = 0.0216 × 120 / 35 = 0.0741 Ω per conductor. Ploss = 3 I²R = 3 × 81.2² × 0.0741 = 1,465 W, which over 416 h is 609.5 kWh and LKR 7,972 per month — about 3 % of the motor's own energy. Real, worth knowing, and not on its own a reason to upsize: at LKR 693,000 for 50 mm² the payback would be years.

P6 — the wrong meter. An averaging clamp reads 22 A on a VFD supply whose true form factor is 1.52. What is the real RMS current, and what does that do to your loss calculation?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: An averaging meter displays mean|i| × 1.11 (the sine form factor). So mean|i| = 22/1.11 = 19.8 A and the true RMS is 19.8 × 1.52 = 30.1 A. Since heating goes as the square, the real loss is (30.1/22)² = 1.88× what you calculated — the cable runs at nearly twice the losses you designed for. This one error accounts for a great many warm panels in plants that have added drives over the years.

P7 — the touch voltage. In fault F4 the chiller-house metalwork sits at 124 V. A fitter with typical body impedance (1,300 Ω) touches it. What current flows, and which IEC 60479 zone is that?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: I = 124 / 1300 = 95 mA — well into zone AC-4, where ventricular fibrillation is possible and becomes more likely the longer it lasts. And in fault F4 the breaker takes tens of seconds to respond thermally, so the duration is effectively unlimited. Note that even the conventional 50 V limit would give 50/1300 = 38 mA — the limit is not a comfortable margin, it is the edge of the survivable region for a short duration. That is why disconnection time is written into the standard alongside the voltage.

P8 — neutral current. Three single-phase lighting and socket circuits draw 30, 22 and 18 A from the three phases of a balanced 400/230 V supply. What current flows in the neutral?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: For linear loads at unity power factor, IN = √(I₁²+I₂²+I₃² − I₁I₂ − I₂I₃ − I₃I₁) = √112 = 10.6 A — far less than any individual phase, because the three currents are 120 ° apart and largely cancel. Perfectly balanced loads give exactly zero neutral current, which is the whole point of three-phase distribution.

The trap: this cancellation fails completely for triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th, 15th), which are in phase with each other and therefore add in the neutral. With a large population of LED drivers and switch-mode supplies the neutral can carry more than any phase, and a half-sized neutral — still common in older installations — then overheats invisibly. See A56.

P9 — capacitance. The 152 kvar bank from P3 is delta-connected on 400 V, 50 Hz. What capacitance is needed per phase?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Q = 3 × V² × 2πfC for a delta bank, so C = 152,000 / (3 × 2π × 50 × 400²) = 1,008 µF per phase. Two things follow. First, these are enormous capacitors that store real energy — they must be discharged before touching, and bank discharge resistors are a safety component, not an accessory. Second, at 250 Hz (the 5th harmonic) that capacitance presents one-fifth the impedance it does at 50 Hz, which is exactly why detuning reactors exist.

P10 — design synthesis. A new 30 kW motor (η 0.91, pf 0.86) sits 200 m from the MCC. Ze = 0.30 Ω. Specify the cable, the CPC and the protective device so that all four tests pass.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: S = 30/(0.91×0.86) = 38.3 kVA → Ib = 55.3 A, so a 63 A device.

Volt drop: 16 mm² gives 2.4×55.3×200/1000 = 26.6 V = 6.64 % — fails. 25 mm² gives 16.6 V = 4.15 % — passes but thin. 35 mm² gives 12.2 V = 3.04 % — comfortable.
Fault loop with a 16 mm² CPC: 25 mm² gives Zs = 0.743 Ω; 35 mm² gives 0.693 Ω. A 63 A type C allows only 0.365 Ω — both fail. A 63 A type B allows 0.730 Ω — 35 mm² passes, 25 mm² does not.

Answer: 4-core 35 mm² with a 16 mm² CPC, 63 A type B MCB — then verify the motor's starting inrush against the type B's 315 A threshold, because a direct-on-line start of a 30 kW motor will exceed it. So the honest full answer is: 35 mm²/16 mm², a type C device for the inrush, plus a 300 mA time-delayed RCD to provide the disconnection — or fit a soft starter or VFD, remove the inrush entirely, and use the type B.

That is the real shape of the job. The four tests interact, the answer is a system rather than a cable, and the best solution is often to change the load's behaviour rather than to keep enlarging the supply. If you got here, you can read a cable schedule.
