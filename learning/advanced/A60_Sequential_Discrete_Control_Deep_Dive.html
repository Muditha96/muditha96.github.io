Sequential Control Is a Different Discipline

Everything in A57 and A58 was about holding a number steady. This module is about deciding what happens next — and it uses completely different mathematics.

THE DEFINITION

Sequential (discrete-event) control decides which action happens next, based on the state the machine is in and the events that have occurred. Its variables are Boolean and enumerated, its time base is event-driven, and its mathematics is logic and automata theory — not differential equations.

A PID loop asks "how far am I from setpoint, and how hard should I push?". A sequence asks "the clamp is closed and the guard is shut — may I start the spindle now, and what do I do if the spindle does not confirm within four seconds?". The first question is answered by calculus; the second by a state machine. Confusing the two is the single most common structural mistake in industrial software.

The economics matter. In a typical machine or plant control system, 60–80 % of the code is sequential, interlock and mode logic, and only a small fraction is regulatory loops. Almost all commissioning time, almost all unplanned downtime attributable to controls, and almost all of the “it stopped and nobody knows why” incidents live in that 60–80 %. Yet it is the part engineers are least formally trained in, because university control courses spend their time in the Laplace domain.

Notice the pattern: every item above is where the plant is changing from one condition to another. Continuous control assumes a steady operating point exists and stays near it. Sequential control owns everything else — which, on a real plant, is where the money, the risk and the engineering hours actually are.

Quick check: A machine occasionally “misses” a cycle: the part is clamped, but the drill never starts, and the operator has to reset. The transition condition is clamp_closed AND guard_shut, and both sensors test good. What class of fault is this, and why is retuning or replacing sensors the wrong response?
Tap to reveal ↓A: This is a race condition — a timing fault in the logic, not a hardware fault. Both conditions must be true at the same instant that the transition is evaluated. If clamp_closed goes true and, a fraction of a second later, the guard bounces open for two scans while the clamp cylinder settles, the two signals are never simultaneously true during a scan in which the sequence is looking at them, so the transition never fires. The machine sits, correctly, in a step whose exit condition has genuinely never been satisfied. Nothing is broken: the sensors are fine, the actuators are fine, and no amount of replacing them will help, because the defect is that the design assumed two independent asynchronous events would coincide. The correct responses are structural. (1) Latch the events: use a set-dominant latch on each condition, cleared on step entry, so that “the clamp has closed at some point during this step” is remembered rather than sampled. (2) Debounce or filter the guard signal with an on-delay of a few tens of milliseconds so a mechanical bounce cannot deassert it. (3) Add a step timeout (Page 14) so the machine tells you “step 2 waited 5 s for clamp+guard” instead of standing silently — the diagnostic is worth more than the fix. (4) Sequence the conditions: if the guard must be shut before clamping starts, make that its own step with its own confirmation, rather than asking for a coincidence at the end. The general principle: in discrete-event systems, never require two independent asynchronous events to be true in the same scan. Either sequence them or latch them.

Boolean & Combinational Logic

Before state machines there is plain logic — and most industrial interlock networks are plain logic written badly.

COMBINATIONAL VS SEQUENTIAL

A combinational network's output depends only on the present inputs. A sequential network's output depends on the inputs and on its own past — it has memory.

This distinction is the whole of digital design in one line, and it has a direct engineering consequence: a combinational network can be completely specified by a truth table and completely tested by exercising every input combination. A sequential network cannot — you must also enumerate its states, which is why Pages 3 to 6 exist. So the first design decision on any piece of logic is: does this need memory, or am I about to add memory because I have not thought clearly?

A permissive network (“may the pump start?”) should be pure combinational logic — it is a statement about the present condition of the plant. A start/stop station (“the pump has been told to run and stays running”) is inherently sequential, because “the operator pressed start three minutes ago” is a memory. Mixing the two into one rung is how you get a pump that will not stop.

CANONICAL FORMS & MINIMISATION

Any Boolean function can be written as a sum of products (SOP — one AND term per row of the truth table where the output is 1, ORed together) or a product of sums (POS — one OR term per zero row, ANDed). These canonical forms are correct but grotesquely inefficient: a 4-input function may need 8 four-input AND terms where 2 two-input terms would do.

Minimisation matters less than it did in the relay era — a PLC scan does not care about a few extra contacts — but it matters enormously for human comprehension, testability and maintenance. A minimal expression tells you which inputs genuinely influence the outcome; a bloated one hides that. And in hardwired safety circuits, FPGA logic and ASIC design, the contact and gate count is still real money and real failure rate.

  • 1Karnaugh map — up to 4–5 variables, done by eye. Cells are arranged in Gray code so that physically adjacent cells differ in exactly one variable, which makes the adjacency law visible. Group 1s in rectangles of 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 cells; each group kills log₂(size) variables.
  • 2Quine–McCluskey — the same idea made algorithmic, so a computer can do 8, 12 or 20 variables. The demo below runs it for four variables in your browser.
  • 3Don't-care conditions (X) — input combinations that cannot physically occur (both limit switches on at once) or whose output nobody cares about. Marking them X rather than 0 lets the minimiser absorb them into groups and often halves the result. But: an “impossible” combination that later occurs will produce whatever the minimiser happened to choose. In safety-related logic, never use don't-cares — force the impossible combinations to the safe value explicitly.
  • 4Prime implicant — a group that cannot be made any larger. Essential prime implicant — a prime implicant that is the only one covering some particular 1. Every essential prime implicant must appear in the minimal solution; the remaining 1s are then covered as cheaply as possible.

CONTACT LOGIC & FAIL-SAFE WIRING
The Boolean expression and the fail-safe question are independent. You can write perfect logic on top of a wiring convention that makes a broken wire invisible.

The wiring convention determines what a wire fault means, and no amount of software logic can recover the information a fail-danger convention throws away. The rule is: the signal that permits motion is the one that must be actively present. Stop buttons, e-stops, guard switches, level trips and pressure trips are wired normally closed and read as “1 = healthy”; start buttons and demands are wired normally open. In the PLC you then write IF NOT EstopOK THEN trip, and the same code covers a pressed button, a cut cable and a dead power supply.

A subtlety worth knowing: this makes the software read slightly counter-intuitively, because the e-stop input is high in the safe state. Engineers “fix” that by inverting it in software so it reads more naturally — and in doing so destroy the diagnostic. Resist. Name the tag EstopOK or EstopHealthy instead, so the polarity is documented in the name.

Quick check: An engineer simplifies a burner permissive from Purge·Flamē·GasOK + Purge·Flamē·GasOK̄ to Purge·Flamē, correctly applying the adjacency law. Why might this be a serious mistake even though the algebra is right?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The algebra is impeccable and the engineering is dangerous, for two separate reasons. First, the original expression was probably wrong, and simplification has hidden the error rather than exposed it. The fact that GasOK appears in both a true and a complemented form, with the same result, says the design does not care whether the gas supply is healthy — which for a burner permissive is almost certainly a specification defect, not a redundancy. The right response to “this variable cancels out” in a safety-related expression is go back to the cause-and-effect specification and find out why, not to delete the variable. Minimisation is a tool for implementing a correct function efficiently; it is not a tool for discovering what the function should be. Second, even where the reduction is genuinely intended, it destroys diagnostic and traceability value. In safety-related logic the expression is required to be demonstrably traceable to the safety requirements specification (IEC 61511 / IEC 62061), and an assessor comparing the cause-and-effect matrix to the code needs to see every input the matrix names. A minimised expression in which two named causes have vanished cannot be traced without redoing the algebra. Common practice in SIS work is therefore the opposite of minimisation: write the logic in the same shape as the cause-and-effect matrix, one row at a time, even when it is redundant, and let the compiler or the scan waste the microseconds. Third, and more subtle: if GasOK can ever be indeterminate or stale (a fieldbus dropout leaving the last value), the two forms are not equivalent in the real system even though they are equivalent in Boolean algebra, which assumes every variable has a definite value at the moment of evaluation. The general rule: minimise combinational logic for clarity in non-safety code; in safety code, mirror the specification and never let algebra remove a named cause.

Finite State Machines

The single most valuable structure in industrial software — and the one most often reinvented badly.

THE FORMAL DEFINITION

A finite state machine is a five-tuple: a finite set of states, a finite input alphabet, a transition function, an initial state, and an output function. Everything else is notation.

Moore machine M = (S, Σ, Λ, δ, ω, s₀) S finite set of states Σ input alphabet (the events / conditions) Λ output alphabet δ : S × Σ → S transition function (state, event) → next state ω : S → Λ output depends on the STATE ONLY s₀ initial state Mealy machine output function becomes ω : S × Σ → Λ output depends on state AND current input

The definition contains one requirement engineers routinely violate: δ must be a total function. For every state and every event, the next state must be defined — including “stay here and ignore it”. A machine in which some (state, event) pairs are simply not mentioned is not a finite state machine; it is a program with undefined behaviour, and the behaviour it exhibits will be whatever the implementation language happens to do. In a PLC, that is normally “stay in this state forever with the outputs still on”.

MOORE VS MEALY
Every Mealy machine has an equivalent Moore machine (add a state per distinct output-on-arrival) and vice versa. The choice is engineering, not mathematics.

For industrial sequential control, use Moore. The reason is diagnostic, not theoretical: if outputs are a pure function of state, then knowing the state tells you everything about what the machine is doing, and a single INT on the HMI documents the entire output picture. With Mealy outputs you must also know which transition just fired, which is not visible in a trend. Mealy's advantage — reacting in the same cycle rather than the next — is worth having in an FPGA running at 100 MHz and worth nothing in a PLC scanning at 10 ms, where the field device takes 200 ms to respond anyway.

The practical compromise most PLC code uses is a Moore machine with entry and exit actions: outputs are set by the state, plus a one-shot pulse on entering or leaving a state (fire the ejector, increment the counter, stamp the timestamp). That is formally a Mealy element, but a disciplined one — the action is tied to the state change, not to an arbitrary input.

STATE EXPLOSION & HIERARCHY

The flat FSM has one fatal scaling property: independent binary conditions multiply. A machine with a 6-state cycle that must also work in three modes, with a hold facility and a two-position tool changer, is not 6 states — it is 6 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 72, and every one of them needs its transitions defined. Draw that and you will not draw it twice.

Flat FSM state count for k independent concerns N = n₁ × n₂ × ... × nₖ (multiplicative — explodes) With orthogonal regions (Harel statecharts / SFC parallel branches) N = n₁ + n₂ + ... + nₖ (additive — tractable)

The three classical cures, all from Harel's statecharts (1987) and all present in one form or another in IEC 61131-3 SFC:

  • 1Hierarchy (nested states). A superstate “RUNNING” contains the six cycle states. A transition drawn from the superstate to FAULT applies to all six, replacing six arrows with one. In SFC this is a macro step; in code it is a nested CASE or a called state-machine function block.
  • 2Orthogonal regions (concurrency). Mode, cycle and tool position are independent concerns running side by side, each with its own current state, communicating by shared variables. This converts the multiplication into an addition. In SFC these are simultaneous branches; in code, separate function-block instances.
  • 3History. Re-entering a superstate resumes the sub-state you left rather than restarting — the formal basis of “resume after hold”. This is exactly what ISA-88's HELD/RESTARTING pair provides (Page 12), and getting it right is most of what makes a hold facility usable.
Quick check: A colleague implements a 12-step sequence as twelve latched coils (one-hot), each rung setting the next step and resetting itself. On site, the machine occasionally ends up with two step bits set and behaves nonsensically. What is the root cause class, how would you detect it, and what would you change?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The root cause class is an illegal state encoding, made possible because one-hot encoding has 2¹² = 4096 representable patterns of which only 12 are legal. Any mechanism that sets a step bit without clearing the previous one — a forced bit left in from commissioning, a retentive memory that survived a power cycle in a bad combination, an online edit that ran a rung out of order, an HMI writing directly to a step bit, a transition condition true for two adjacent steps simultaneously, or a communication block writing a stale image — leaves the machine in a state the designer never enumerated, and the output logic (which ORs the contributions of both active steps) energises a combination that no state table ever authorised. Detection. Add a continuously evaluated invariant: count the set step bits every scan and require the count to be exactly one whenever the sequence is running (and exactly zero when it is not). nActive := BOOL_TO_INT(S1)+BOOL_TO_INT(S2)+..., then IF nActive <> 1 THEN GotoSafeState(); RaiseAlarm('sequence encoding fault'); END_IF. This is cheap, runs in microseconds and converts a baffling intermittent into a specific alarm with a timestamp. It is the discrete-event equivalent of a range check on an analogue input, and it belongs in every one-hot implementation. What to change. Move to an integer or enumerated step variable with a CASE statement, where being in two states at once is not representable — the illegal states cease to exist rather than being detected. Assign the step in exactly one place in the whole program, so there is a single writer. Keep the one-hot bits if the HMI or the output logic wants them, but derive them from the integer every scan rather than latching them independently: S1 := (Step = 1); S2 := (Step = 2); .... Add the range check IF Step < 0 OR Step > 9 THEN Step := FAULT for the corrupted-integer case, make the step variable non-retentive so a power cycle starts from a known state, and forbid the HMI from writing it directly — the HMI sends a command, and the state machine decides whether to honour it. That last point is the general principle: a state machine must have exactly one writer of its own state, and every external influence must arrive as an event, not as an assignment.

GRAFCET & SFC: the Sequence Language

One graphical notation, two standards, and a distinction that most engineers have never been told about.

TWO STANDARDS, TWO PURPOSES

GRAFCET (IEC 60848) is a specification language: it says what the system must do, independently of the technology that will do it. SFC (IEC 61131-3) is a programming language: it says how a specific controller will execute it. They look almost identical and are not the same thing.

GRAFCET — GRAphe Fonctionnel de Commande Étape–Transition — came out of the French AFCET working group in 1977 as a way for process engineers, electrical engineers and software engineers to agree on machine behaviour without arguing about implementation. It became IEC 60848, now in its third edition (2013), which was a full technical revision chiefly to formalise the treatment of variables (input, internal and output variables became explicitly defined). Crucially, IEC 60848 states that methods of implementing a GRAFCET specification are outside its scope, and points at IEC 61131-3's SFC as one such method — the relationship is spelled out in its Annex C.

Why does this matter on a real project? Because it gives you a legitimate, standard-backed answer to the eternal problem of who owns the sequence. The process or machine engineer writes the GRAFCET; it is a specification document, reviewable and signable by people who do not program. The controls engineer implements it as SFC, ladder, ST or anything else. When behaviour is disputed at commissioning, the GRAFCET is the contract. Teams who skip this step end up with the PLC code as the only description of intended behaviour, which means the specification and the implementation can never disagree — and therefore can never be checked.

THE ANATOMY
One line = choose one (selective). Two lines = do all (simultaneous). Getting these two symbols the wrong way round is the most common SFC drawing error, and it changes the machine completely.

The S/R rule Every S must have a matching R somewhere in the chart, on a path that is guaranteed to be reached — INCLUDING the abort path, the fault path and the power-up path. An S with no R is not an action. It is a latent bug that will surface on the day somebody aborts mid-cycle.

The row that bites is the time model. GRAFCET's idealised semantics say that when a transition's receptivity becomes true the transition fires immediately and, if that makes another receptivity true, that one fires in the same instant — a whole cascade resolves in zero time. A PLC cannot do that; it evaluates the chart once per scan. Most runtimes therefore advance one transition per branch per scan, which means a chain of four instantly-true transitions takes four scans. On a 10 ms task that is 40 ms — usually irrelevant, occasionally not. Check your vendor's documented evolution semantics before you rely on cascade timing.

Quick check: A machine's SFC uses S (set) to energise the clamp solenoid in step 2 and R (reset) to release it in step 7. During commissioning an operator hits Abort in step 4. What happens, and what are the three ways to fix it properly?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The clamp stays energised. The S qualifier stored the command; the only thing that clears it is the R in step 7, and Abort jumped out of the chain before step 7 was ever reached. The chart is now inactive (or sitting in an abort branch) while a solenoid it no longer models is still on. Depending on the machine that means a part clamped indefinitely, a cylinder held under pressure, an air leak nobody can find, or — if the clamp is what holds a load — a stored-energy hazard during the maintenance that follows. Note how badly this fails: the state of the chart no longer tells you the state of the machine, which is the one property the whole discipline exists to preserve. Three proper fixes, in order of preference. (1) Use N instead of S. If the clamp must be energised for steps 2 to 6, put an N action on each of those steps, or better, wrap steps 2–6 in a macro step / enclosing step that carries the N ClampSolenoid action for as long as any of its sub-steps is active. The output then ceases automatically whenever the chart leaves that region, by construction, with no bookkeeping. This is the Moore principle from Page 3 applied to SFC: outputs should be a function of which steps are active, not of history. (2) If a stored action is genuinely required — because the output must survive a step the chart passes through quickly, or must persist across a hold — then every exit path from the region must carry the matching R: the normal path (step 7), the abort branch, the fault branch, and the initialisation logic that runs on power-up or on chart reset. Write them all down at the moment you write the S, not later. (3) Make the abort path a real part of the chart, not an external jump. A well-formed sequence has an abort/hold branch drawn as a selective divergence from an enclosing step, leading to a defined SAFE step whose actions explicitly de-energise everything the sequence owns — the ISA-88 ABORTING state (Page 12) is precisely this concept formalised. Then “what happens if I abort here” has a drawn answer for every value of “here”. The generalisable rule: a stored action creates an obligation that lives outside the chart's normal flow, so it must be discharged on every path out, including the ones you hope never happen. If you cannot enumerate those paths, you should not be using S.

Evolution Rules, Divergence & Hierarchy

Five rules define the entire semantics. Learn them and most “mysterious” sequence behaviour stops being mysterious.

THE FIVE EVOLUTION RULES (IEC 60848)
  • 1Initial situation. At start, exactly the initial steps are active. Everything the chart does afterwards is defined relative to this known starting marking — which is why a chart with no initial step, or with an initial step reachable only by luck, is not a valid GRAFCET.
  • 2Transition clearing (firing). A transition is enabled when all of its immediately preceding steps are active. An enabled transition is cleared when its receptivity is also true. Both halves matter: a true condition on a transition whose upstream step is inactive does nothing at all, which is why events must be latched (Page 1).
  • 3Evolution of active steps. Clearing a transition simultaneously deactivates all immediately preceding steps and activates all immediately following steps. Simultaneously — not “deactivate then activate”. This is what makes a simultaneous divergence create several tokens from one, and a convergence consume several to make one.
  • 4Simultaneous clearing. All transitions that can be cleared at a given instant are cleared at that instant. There is no arbitration and no ordering — GRAFCET does not have a “first one wins” rule. If two branches of a selective divergence are both true, both fire, and you now have two tokens where the designer expected one.
  • 5Simultaneous activation and deactivation. If a step would be simultaneously activated and deactivated by the same evolution, it remains active. This resolves the self-loop case cleanly and prevents a one-scan output dropout when a step re-enters itself.

Rule 4 is the one that catches people. It means the mutual exclusivity of selective branch conditions is your responsibility, not the standard's. If you write Temp > 80 on one branch and Pressure > 5 on another and both can be true, the chart is malformed — and it will misbehave only occasionally, when both happen to be true in the same scan, which is the worst possible failure schedule. The disciplined form is A on one branch and NOT A (or an explicit priority, A and B AND NOT A) on the other.

HIERARCHY: MACRO STEPS, ENCLOSING STEPS & FORCING

Macro step

A single step symbol on the parent chart that expands into a whole sub-chart with a defined entry step and exit step. The parent's transition after the macro fires when the macro's exit step is active. Pure decomposition — the semantics are identical to substituting the sub-chart inline.

Enclosing step

A step that, while active, activates one or more enclosed partial charts. Deactivating the enclosing step deactivates everything inside it. This is the structure that makes hold, abort and mode changes clean: the enclosing step owns its children's lifetime.

Forcing order

A GRAFCET-specific hierarchy mechanism: a higher-level chart can force a lower-level chart into a specified marking (or its initial marking, or an empty marking), overriding its own evolution. Used for emergency stop and mode management — the safety chart forces the production chart to a safe marking.

Partial GRAFCET

The full specification is split into named partial charts (safety, modes, production, auxiliaries) that run concurrently and communicate through variables and forcing orders. This is how you avoid one enormous unreadable drawing.

A word of caution on forcing: it is elegant in IEC 60848 and not part of IEC 61131-3 SFC. If your specification uses forcing orders, the implementer must realise them some other way — typically by an enclosing step whose deactivation kills the sub-chart, plus explicit initialisation logic. Specify the forcing in GRAFCET by all means, but agree the implementation mechanism before someone invents one at 11 p.m. during commissioning.

Quick check: A packaging machine has a simultaneous divergence into three branches: fill, cap and label. The convergence waits for all three. In production, whenever the label printer runs out of ribbon, the whole machine stops dead with no useful alarm, and the operator must power-cycle it. Diagnose it and propose a correct structure.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Diagnosis: an AND-convergence deadlock caused by a branch that has no completion path under fault conditions. The label branch's token has reached a step whose exit transition needs LabelApplied, which will never become true while the ribbon is out. Rule 2 says the convergence transition is enabled only when all its preceding steps are active; two of the three are, one is not, so the convergence is permanently disabled. The fill and cap tokens sit in their final steps forever, doing nothing, with their outputs (per the N qualifier) already off, so the machine looks idle rather than faulted. There is no alarm because no timeout was ever defined, and no restart because the initial marking is unreachable from this marking — hence the power cycle, which is the operator's way of forcing the initial marking by brute force. Note carefully what is not wrong: the ribbon sensor works, the logic is “correct” in the sense that it does what it was drawn to do, and no code is buggy. The defect is structural — a concurrent structure was used without providing an exit for every branch. The correct structure has four elements. (1) A timeout on every step in every concurrent branch, with a defined destination — not just an alarm. Each branch step becomes a small selective divergence: normal exit on its completion condition, fault exit on Step.T > T#limit. Now the label branch reaches a defined FAULT step within seconds and the machine can say “label branch timed out waiting for LabelApplied”, which names the fault precisely. (2) An enclosing step around the whole concurrent region whose deactivation kills all three branches at once. The fault path from any branch signals the enclosing step to terminate, and the enclosing step's exit deactivates every token inside it — so there are no orphans and the marking becomes well-defined again. This is the structural answer to “how do I abort out of the middle of a parallel region”, and it is the same mechanism ISA-88 uses for HOLDING and ABORTING (Page 12). (3) A defined recovery path back to the initial marking: the FAULT step's exit transition requires operator reset plus “cause cleared”, and leads to a SAFE/RESET step whose actions put every actuator and every stored variable back to the initial condition, then returns to the initial step. Reachability of the initial marking must be a design requirement, not an accident. (4) Consider whether the concurrency is real. Three branches that must all finish before anything continues, on a machine where they physically occur at different stations, may be better modelled as three independent charts with their own cycles, coordinated by handshakes, rather than one chart with a hard AND-convergence. Concurrency in the chart should mirror concurrency in the steel; if the label station can legitimately run one cycle behind, forcing them into lockstep converts an independent stoppage into a whole-machine stoppage — which is precisely the availability loss the plant is complaining about (A22 OEE).

Petri Nets: Concurrency & Deadlock

The one piece of formal method that pays for itself on ordinary industrial projects — because it turns “I think it cannot deadlock” into a proof.

THE DEFINITION
Petri net PN = (P, T, F, W, M₀) P finite set of places (conditions, resources, buffers) T finite set of transitions (events, actions) F ⊆ (P×T) ∪ (T×P) directed arcs — never place→place or transition→transition W : F → {1,2,3,...} arc weights (default 1) M : P → {0,1,2,...} a marking — how many tokens in each place M₀ the initial marking Firing rule transition t is enabled at M ⇔ M(p) ≥ W(p,t) for every input place p firing t gives M′(p) = M(p) − W(p,t) + W(t,p) Tokens are removed from all input places and added to all output places atomically. That atomicity is what makes a Petri net able to express synchronisation, which a plain state machine cannot.

The relationship to everything on the previous pages is exact and worth stating clearly. A finite state machine is a Petri net in which every transition has exactly one input place and one output place — so the token count is conserved at one and there is no concurrency. A marked graph is the dual: every place has one input and one output transition, giving concurrency but no choice. A safe (1-bounded) Petri net with both features is essentially what an SFC is, and the SFC's simultaneous divergence is a transition with one input place and several outputs; its convergence is a transition with several inputs and one output. Learning Petri nets therefore does not add a new formalism to your life — it gives you the analysis machinery for the one you already draw.

THE CLASSIC: TWO UNITS, TWO SHARED RESOURCES
One rule — “always acquire resources in the same global order” — breaks the circular-wait condition and removes the deadlock structurally, at zero runtime cost.

Coffman's four conditions must all hold for deadlock: mutual exclusion (a resource cannot be shared), hold and wait (a process keeps what it has while asking for more), no pre-emption (a resource cannot be taken away), and circular wait (a cycle of processes each waiting on the next). Break any one and deadlock becomes impossible. In industrial systems mutual exclusion and no-pre-emption are usually physically fixed — you cannot half-share a transfer pump, and you cannot snatch it back mid-transfer — so the two available levers are ordered acquisition (kills circular wait) and all-or-nothing allocation (kills hold-and-wait: acquire every resource the phase needs in one atomic step, or acquire none).

THE ALGEBRA: INCIDENCE MATRIX & INVARIANTS

Hand-simulation does not scale, and the reachability graph grows exponentially. The linear-algebraic tools do scale, and they give you proofs rather than samples.

Incidence matrix C = Post − Pre (|P| rows × |T| columns) C[p][t] = (tokens t puts into p) − (tokens t takes from p) State equation M′ = M₀ + C · σ σ is the firing count vector — how many times each transition fired. NOTE: this is a necessary condition for reachability, not sufficient. A solution σ may exist for a marking that is not actually reachable, because the equation ignores the ORDER of firing. P-invariant yᵀ · C = 0, y ≥ 0 ⇒ yᵀ · M = yᵀ · M₀ for EVERY reachable marking — a conserved weighted token count. Proves boundedness and mutual exclusion. T-invariant C · x = 0, x ≥ 0 ⇒ firing the transitions x times returns the net to the same marking. Proves the existence of a cycle — the basis of reversibility.

The P-invariant is the workhorse. Take the resource place R1 and the two places that represent “somebody is holding R1”. The vector y with 1 in each of those three positions and 0 elsewhere satisfies yᵀC = 0, so M(R1) + M(A has R1) + M(B has R1) = 1 in every reachable marking, forever. That single line proves mutual exclusion — the pump can never be allocated twice — without simulating anything, and it stays true no matter how you later extend the rest of the net. It also proves those three places are 1-bounded, so they are plain BOOLs in the PLC.

EXTENSIONS YOU WILL MEET

Timed Petri nets

Transitions carry a firing duration or delay. Turns the net into a throughput and cycle-time model — the same drawing now predicts parts per hour and identifies the bottleneck place.

Coloured Petri nets (CPN)

Tokens carry data (part type, recipe ID, destination). One net models a whole product mix instead of one net per variant. Tool support: CPN Tools.

Stochastic (SPN/GSPN)

Firing delays are random variables, giving a Markov chain. Used for availability, buffer sizing and reliability — the bridge to RCM and MTBF work.

Hierarchical / modular

Substitution transitions expand into sub-nets, exactly like SFC macro steps. Essential for anything above about thirty places.

Inhibitor arcs

An arc that enables a transition only when the place is empty. Adds expressive power (Turing-complete) but destroys most of the analysis theorems — use sparingly and knowingly.

Tools

Quick check: Two mixing vessels share one CIP (clean-in-place) skid and one transfer pump. Each vessel's cleaning phase needs both. Occasionally the plant stops with both vessels “waiting for CIP” and neither ever proceeding. Give the Petri-net diagnosis and three structurally different cures, ranked.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Diagnosis: circular wait between two resource places. Model each vessel as a small chain (idle → holding one resource → holding both → cleaning → release both → idle) and each resource as a place holding one token. Vessel 1's phase logic happens to request the pump first and the CIP skid second; vessel 2 requests the CIP skid first and the pump second — usually because the two phases were written by different people, or by the same person on different days. The deadlock marking is: pump token held by vessel 1, CIP token held by vessel 2, and both vessels sitting in their “holding one, requesting the other” place. No transition is enabled, so the net is dead: all four Coffman conditions hold simultaneously. It is intermittent because it requires the two requests to interleave in a particular order, which happens only when the two vessels' cycles drift into a particular phase relationship — hence “occasionally”, and hence its survival through commissioning. Cure 1 (best): ordered acquisition. Impose a global total order on all shared resources — alphabetical, numerical, whatever, provided it is written down and enforced by code review: every phase must acquire in that order and release in the reverse order. Circular wait becomes structurally impossible, the fix costs nothing at runtime, and the P-invariant proof still holds. This is the standard solution in operating-system design for the same reason. Its weakness is that it relies on discipline across every phase anyone ever writes, so it must be embodied in a reusable acquire-resources function block rather than left to individual programmers. Cure 2: all-or-nothing (atomic) allocation. Kill hold-and-wait instead: the phase asks an arbitrator for the complete set {pump, CIP} in a single indivisible operation. If both are free it gets both; if not, it gets neither and waits holding nothing. In Petri-net terms this is one transition with both resource places as inputs, which cannot fire partially. This is exactly ISA-88 resource arbitration (Pages 12–13) and is what a proper batch engine does. It is more code than cure 1 but it scales to many resources and many units, and it makes the allocation policy explicit and tunable (FIFO, priority, shortest-job-first). Its weakness is potential starvation — a unit needing three resources may never find all three free while units needing one keep grabbing them — so the arbitrator needs an ageing or reservation rule. Cure 3 (weakest): timeout and back-off. Give each acquisition a timeout; on expiry, release everything held, wait a randomised interval, and retry. This does not prevent deadlock; it detects and recovers from it. It is worth having as a backstop even with cures 1 or 2 in place — because it also catches deadlocks introduced by a resource that is unavailable for a non-software reason, such as a manual valve left shut — but as a primary strategy it is poor: it converts a hard stop into a livelock risk, it wastes cycle time, and it hides a design defect behind an apparently self-healing machine. What to add regardless of the cure chosen: instrument the arbitration. Log every acquire, every release and every wait with a timestamp and a requester, and alarm on “waiting for resource > N seconds” naming both the requester and the current holder. That single alarm turns this entire class of fault from a multi-day investigation into a one-line answer, and it is the discrete-event analogue of a first-out alarm.

The RETAIN decision on a step variable deserves a paragraph of its own, because both answers are defensible and one is usually wrong for your machine. If the step is retained, the machine wakes after a power failure believing it is halfway through a cycle — while the physical world has moved (the cylinder drifted, the part fell out, someone opened the guard). If the step is not retained, the machine wakes in step 0 and demands a homing sequence, which is safe but costs a restart procedure you must actually write. A task is what actually causes code to run. This is where sequential and continuous control meet again. A57 showed that a sampled loop pays Tₛ/2 of dead time; here the same arithmetic decides whether your sequence can catch a 30 ms proximity pulse from a part flying past on a conveyor. It cannot, if the task runs at 50 ms — the pulse can fall entirely between two input reads and be invisible. The cures are a faster task, a hardware latch input, a high-speed counter/interrupt input, or a sensor with a pulse-stretch setting. Never solve it by “the PLC missed it, add a bigger flag”; if the event is shorter than two task periods, no amount of software will see it reliably.

Quick check: A sequence function block is instantiated three times, once per station. During testing, station 2 occasionally jumps to a step that only station 1 should reach. The FB code is identical for all three. Where do you look first, and what are the two most likely causes?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Look first for state that is shared rather than instanced — that is, for anything the FB touches that is not in its own VAR section. The symptom “identical code, one instance affects another” is almost by definition a violation of instance encapsulation, because if every byte the FB used were in its own instance data, the instances could not possibly interact. Cause 1: a global variable used as if it were local. Somewhere inside the FB (or in a function it calls) there is a VAR_GLOBAL — a shared step number, a shared timer instance, a shared “request” flag, a shared handshake word with the robot, or a scratch variable someone declared globally “temporarily” during commissioning. All three instances write it every scan; the last writer wins, and since the execution order is fixed, station 2 reads whatever station 1 wrote a few microseconds earlier. The tell-tale is that the fault correlates with the order of the instance calls. Fix: move the variable into VAR, or if it genuinely must be shared, pass it explicitly as VAR_IN_OUT so the sharing is visible in the interface and reviewable. A subtler version of the same cause is a FUNCTION that is not actually pure — a “helper function” that writes to a global or to a static buffer is a function block wearing a disguise, and it will alias between callers. Cause 2: a located (%M / %I / %Q) address hard-coded inside the reusable FB. If the FB body refers to %MW100 or %QX2.3 directly, then all three instances address the same physical bit no matter which station they belong to, and the encapsulation is broken at the I/O layer instead of the data layer. This is extremely common in code ported from an older non-instanced program. Fix: the FB must receive its I/O through its interface (inputs as VAR_INPUT, outputs as VAR_OUTPUT, or a device structure by VAR_IN_OUT), and the mapping from %I/%Q to instances happens once, in the calling PROGRAM. Two further candidates worth checking in the same pass. (a) Cross-task pre-emption on shared data: if the three instances are called from tasks of different priority and share any multi-word structure, a higher-priority task can pre-empt a partially completed write and read a torn value; the cure is a copy-in/copy-out buffer or a proper interlock, not a retry. (b) An array indexed by station number with an off-by-one or an uninitialised index — StepArray[stn] with stn occasionally zero or out of range writes into a neighbour's element. Range-check every computed index; in ST, declare the array with an explicit subrange type so the runtime traps it. The generalisable rule: a function block is only reusable to the extent that all of its state and all of its I/O pass through its declared interface. The moment it reaches outside that interface, you no longer have three instances — you have one program with three entry points.

A conveyor may run when the guard is shut, the e-stop chain is healthy, the downstream is not full, and either auto mode with a demand or manual jog is selected:

THE LAYERED CONVENTION THAT WORKS
  • 1Device layer — ST function blocks. One reusable FB per device class: Valve, Motor, AnalogIn, Axis. Each owns its own interlocks, simulation flag, fault handling, feedback confirmation and command interface. Written once, instantiated hundreds of times, tested once.
  • 2Interlock layer — LD. One rung per row of the cause-and-effect matrix, in the same order as the matrix, with no algebraic simplification (Page 2). An auditor with the matrix in hand can verify it line by line without understanding programming.
  • 3Sequence layer — SFC (structure) with ST (actions and conditions). The chart mirrors the signed GRAFCET; the actions call device-layer FB methods. Nobody writes a solenoid address here — they command a device object.
  • 4Regulatory layer — FBD. Loops, filters, cascades and split ranges, laid out to resemble the P&ID so that A58's strategies are visible as drawings.
  • 5Coordination layer — ST. Recipes, resource arbitration, batch/phase management, communications, diagnostics and logging — all of it data manipulation, all of it best as text.

The reason this convention survives contact with reality is that it aligns each language with the audience for that layer. The maintenance electrician at 3 a.m. needs the interlock layer, and it is in ladder. The process engineer needs the sequence layer, and it matches the drawing they signed. The controls engineer needs the device and coordination layers, and they are in text, in version control, with meaningful diffs.

PORTABILITY: THE HONEST POSITION

IEC 61131-3 makes programs portable in principle. In practice, moving a project between vendors is a rewrite, for reasons that are mostly not the standard's fault:

Vendor libraries

Motion, comms, safety and HMI blocks are proprietary. The standard defines the language, not the block library.

Dialects & extensions

Pointer syntax, string handling, task configuration, structured addressing and online change behaviour all differ.

I/O and hardware config

Never part of Part 3. Every migration re-does the hardware tree, the fieldbus and the addressing.

PLCopen XML (61131-10)

A real, working exchange format for logic — the best available bridge, and increasingly the basis of automatic code generation and documentation tooling.

What the standard does buy you is far more valuable than file portability: engineer portability. Someone fluent in ST, FBD and SFC can be productive on Siemens, Rockwell, Beckhoff, Schneider, Omron, Mitsubishi or CODESYS within days rather than months, because the concepts — POUs, instances, tasks, variable scoping, action qualifiers — are the same everywhere. That is worth more to a career than any single vendor certification. PLCopen adds the complementary pieces: Motion Control (the MC_ function blocks used across vendors), Safety (certified block libraries for A52-style logic), OPC UA information models, and a widely used set of coding guidelines that are worth adopting wholesale rather than inventing your own.

Quick check: A site standard mandates “all code in ladder, no exceptions” because the maintenance team can only read ladder. You must implement a 20-step batch sequence with recipe parameters, resource arbitration and timeouts. Argue both sides, then say what you would actually do.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The case for the standard is strong and should not be dismissed. Availability is a maintenance property, not a programming property: a plant whose technicians can open the logic, follow power flow and find why a valve did not open is a plant that restarts in ten minutes rather than waiting for a contractor. Ladder's online monitoring is genuinely excellent — you see conducting contacts in real time, which is a form of live debugging most text languages cannot match on a running machine. A single language also means one skill set to hire for, one set of standards to police, and no arguments at handover. If the site's actual failure statistics show that most downtime is diagnosis time by maintenance staff, the standard is defensible on hard numbers. The case against is equally hard. Recipe handling means arrays and structures; arbitration means comparisons, queues and loops; timeouts mean per-step time bases; a 20-step sequence means either 20 rungs of latched step bits (with the illegal-encoding exposure of Page 3) or a large integer comparison tree. Ladder can express all of it, but the result is several hundred rungs where forty lines of ST would do, it cannot be diffed meaningfully in version control, unit testing is impractical, and the very maintainability the standard was written to protect is destroyed by sheer volume. There is also a safety dimension: logic that is too big to review is logic that is not reviewed. What I would actually do — satisfy the intent, not the letter. The intent is “maintenance must be able to diagnose the machine”, and that intent is served by what maintenance sees, not by what the whole program is written in. So: (1) Keep the interlock and device-command layer in ladder, one rung per interlock, named clearly. This is the layer maintenance actually opens, and it is where 90 % of diagnosis happens — “why won't valve 7 open” is answered by one rung. (2) Write the sequence engine in ST inside a function block, and expose its state through plain, well-named Boolean and integer tags: Step, StepName, StepElapsed, WaitingFor, TimeoutPending. (3) Provide a ladder “mirror” page: one rung per step, each a simple comparison Step = 7 driving a named bit, annotated with the step's purpose and its exit condition. This costs 20 trivial rungs and gives maintenance exactly the visual, monitorable, familiar view they need — without any logic living there. (4) Put the real diagnostics on the HMI, not in the code: a sequence page showing current step, elapsed time, the specific condition being waited for, and the last ten step transitions with timestamps. This is more useful than reading any language, and it serves operators too. (5) Negotiate the standard formally, with evidence: propose “ladder for interlocks and device layer; ST permitted for sequence, recipe and communications, subject to a documented tag interface and a ladder mirror page”, and offer to train two maintenance staff in reading ST CASE statements — which takes an afternoon, because a CASE statement is the most readable construct in programming. The principle worth carrying away: when a rule and its purpose conflict, engineer for the purpose and then get the rule amended, in writing, before you commission. Quietly ignoring a site standard is how you become the reason the standard exists.

THE CANONICAL STATE MACHINE

Six properties make this code professional rather than merely working: an enumerated step with names; a single writer of Step (only inside this CASE); an ELSE branch that catches the impossible; one timeout mechanism serving every step rather than five timer instances; outputs assigned in exactly one place as a function of state, so no output can be commanded from two rungs; and the step exposed as an output, so the HMI and the historian see the machine's mind, not just its muscles.

TEN RULES OF SEQUENCE CODE
  • 1One writer per variable. The step is written only by its own state machine; an output is assigned in exactly one statement. If you cannot name the single writer of a variable, you have a defect waiting.
  • 2Outputs at the bottom, unconditionally. Assign every output every scan, as a function of state. Never IF something THEN Out := TRUE; END_IF with no else — that is a latch you did not mean to write.
  • 3Never wait inside a step. No WHILE NOT ClampClosed DO. The PLC has one thread and a watchdog; blocking is a crash. Waiting is what states are for.
  • 4Every wait has a timeout with a destination. Not an alarm — a destination state. An alarm nobody acts on leaves the machine hung anyway.
  • 5Edge-detect deliberately. Use R_TRIG/F_TRIG instances or an explicit “last value” variable. Never rely on a bit being true for exactly one scan by accident.
  • 6No floating-point equality. IF Temp = 80.0 will be false forever. Use a band: IF ABS(Temp − SP) < 0.5, and give it a debounce time.
  • 7Bound every loop. FOR with a constant limit, never WHILE on a field condition. A loop whose exit depends on the plant is a watchdog trip.
  • 8Range-check every computed index. Recipe[Step] with a corrupted Step reads or writes arbitrary memory on some runtimes.
  • 9Confirm, never assume. Commanding a valve is not the same as the valve moving. The step exits on the feedback, and the timeout catches the difference (Page 14).
  • 10Comment the why, not the what. // wait for clamp is noise; // 3 s covers worst-case 2.1 s at min air pressure + 40 % margin is engineering.

The set-dominant versus reset-dominant distinction is not pedantry. A start/stop station written the wrong way round produces a motor that keeps running while the stop button is held, then stops when it is released — or, on a machine with a sticking start contact, one that cannot be stopped at all. The reliable form is: the stop condition appears last and is ANDed with everything, so it can override any combination of start conditions. And the stop signal itself is normally closed and read as StopOK (Page 2), so a broken wire also stops the motor.

WHERE ST GOES WRONG

The 600-line CASE

One state machine that has absorbed device logic, alarming, recipe handling and comms. Split it: the sequence commands device objects, and the device objects own their own detail.

Step assigned in five places

Someone added a “quick jump” outside the CASE. Now no one can reason about the machine. Enforce the single writer by code review; it is the one rule worth being rigid about.

Hidden latches

IF cond THEN Out := TRUE; END_IF with no corresponding clear. The output stays on across a step change, an abort and a power-up.

Timer reused across steps

One TON instance shared by three states never resets cleanly. One timer per purpose, or use the step-elapsed pattern.

Copy-paste state blocks

Twelve near-identical steps that differ in two constants. Make it data: an array of step records (target, timeout, output mask) and one generic engine.

No simulation path

Code that only runs with real I/O cannot be tested before commissioning. Every device FB should have a Simulate input that fakes its feedback after a realistic delay.

That last one is the highest-return habit on this page. A device function block with a Simulate input — which, when set, echoes the command back as feedback after the device's typical travel time — lets you run the entire sequence on a desk, in a test task, with no hardware. You will find perhaps 70 % of your sequencing defects there, at a cost of about ten lines per device class, instead of finding them on a machine that is now made of steel and has a delivery date.

Quick check: A step reads IF Level >= Target THEN Step := NEXT; END_IF. The vessel fills, the step advances, and everything works — until a day when the level transmitter briefly spikes during pump start and the sequence advances with the vessel nearly empty. List four independent defences, and say which one you would put in first.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The defect is that a transition condition was allowed to fire on an instantaneous sample of an analogue signal, with no requirement that the condition be sustained, plausible or corroborated. Four independent defences, each addressing a different layer: (1) Confirmation time (persistence). Require the condition to hold continuously for a defined period before the transition fires: LevelOK( IN := Level >= Target, PT := T#2s ); IF LevelOK.Q THEN Step := NEXT; END_IF. A pump-start spike lasts tens or hundreds of milliseconds; two seconds of sustained level cannot be faked by a transient. This is the cheapest, most general and most effective defence, and it costs one timer. (2) Signal validation at the source. The analogue input function block should reject and flag out-of-range values (below 3.6 mA or above 20.5 mA per NAMUR NE 43 — see A50), apply a first-order filter with a time constant matched to the real process, and impose a rate-of-change limit: a vessel that physically cannot fill faster than 5 %/s should have any jump beyond that rejected as invalid and alarmed. Sequences should consume validated engineering values with a quality flag, never raw counts, and should refuse to transition on a value whose quality is bad. (3) Corroboration — do not trust one sensor for a consequential decision. If the transition matters (overfill, transfer to the wrong vessel, a reaction step), check a second, physically different indication: a high-level switch, the totalised flow into the vessel, the weigh cell, or the elapsed fill time against the expected fill rate. Agreement between two independent measurements defeats a single-sensor spike; disagreement is itself a valuable alarm. This is the discrete-event version of the 2oo3 voting logic on Page 2. (4) Plausibility bounds on the step itself — the timeout's mirror image. Every step should have not only a maximum duration but, where physics allows, a minimum: if the fill step completes in 4 s when the pump cannot deliver the batch volume in less than 90 s, the completion is impossible and the sequence should fault rather than proceed. A minimum-time check catches an entire family of “the sensor said we were done” failures that no amount of filtering will, because it tests the claim against the process model rather than against the signal. Which first? The confirmation time — it takes two lines, needs no extra instrumentation, is impossible to get wrong, and eliminates the specific failure that occurred. Then the minimum-time plausibility check, because it is also free and catches the general class. Signal validation is next and belongs in the shared analogue-input block so every loop benefits at once. Corroboration comes last only because it may require capital: a second transmitter or a level switch. But note the ranking is about speed of deployment, not importance — if the consequence of a false advance is overfilling a vessel with a flammable liquid, this is not a sequencing problem at all: it is a protection-layer problem, and it belongs in an independent high-level trip designed to IEC 61511 (A52), not in the basic process control sequence. Never let the sequence be the only thing standing between the plant and a hazard.

Ladder, Latching & Scan Order

Ladder is not a schematic. It is a sequential program pretending to be a schematic, and the difference bites exactly here.

THE EXECUTION MODEL

A relay circuit is concurrent — every contact acts on every coil continuously and simultaneously. A ladder program is sequential — rungs are solved one at a time, top to bottom, left to right, once per scan, using the process image captured at the start of the scan.

Almost every classic ladder bug follows from that sentence. In a relay panel, moving a relay from one position in the cabinet to another changes nothing. In a PLC, moving a rung changes the behaviour, because a rung reads the values written by the rungs above it in the same scan and the values written by the rungs below it in the previous scan. Ladder inherited the relay notation and dropped the relay semantics, and nobody put a warning label on it.

What a rung actually reads Rung 12 reads: physical inputs → as sampled at the START of this scan bits written by rungs 1..11 → this scan's values bits written by rungs 13..N → last scan's values ⇒ the same logic, reordered, is a different program.
THE SEAL-IN, DONE RIGHT
Start / stop with stop priority — the most-written rung in industry | StartPB StopOK Permissives MotorRun |----] [--------] [--------] [------+--------------( )---- | | | MotorRun | |----] [----------------------------+ Equivalent ST: MotorRun := (StartPB OR MotorRun) AND StopOK AND Permissives; Why this shape and not another • StopOK is a normally-CLOSED field contact read as 1 = healthy (Page 2), so a broken wire stops the motor. • StopOK and the permissives sit AFTER the OR junction, so they break BOTH the start path and the seal path. Put them before the junction and the seal keeps the motor running through an interlock trip. • Reset dominance: with Start and Stop both active, the motor stops. • The seal contact is the OUTPUT's own bit — that is the memory. The three ways this rung is written wrongly 1. Permissives only in the start branch → interlocks cannot stop a running motor. 2. Stop button wired NO and used as ] [ → broken wire = cannot stop. 3. Set/Reset instructions instead of a seal, with the Reset rung placed ABOVE the Set rung → set wins within the scan; stop is ignored.

Point 3 deserves elaboration because it is invisible in review. With separate SET and RESET (latch/unlatch) instructions, whichever executes later in the scan determines the outcome for that scan. Put the RESET rung after the SET rung and you have reset dominance, which is what safety-related logic requires. Put it before, and a simultaneously asserted stop is overwritten before the outputs are written. The seal-in form has no such ambiguity, which is one reason to prefer it.

STEP CHAINS & THE RUN-THROUGH BUG

The traditional one-hot ladder step chain uses one rung per step:

Rung n: Step[n] := ( Step[n−1] AND Cond[n] ) OR ( Step[n] AND NOT Step[n+1] ) ↑ enter from previous ↑ seal until the next step takes over
Identical logic, identical conditions, opposite rung order — one scan versus four. In descending order each rung reads the previous step's last-scan value, so the token can only advance one step per scan.

This is the run-through (or “fall-through”) bug, and it is the single most characteristic ladder defect. It usually hides because in normal operation the conditions become true one at a time, seconds apart. It surfaces on a dry run, after a fault reset, in simulation, or when a fast machine's conditions genuinely are all satisfied at once — and then the machine appears to skip half its cycle.

  • 1Write the chain in descending step order so that each rung reads the previous step's value from last scan. Cheap, effective, and completely non-obvious to the next engineer — so comment it prominently.
  • 2Use an integer step with a CASE (Page 9). Only one branch executes per scan, so run-through is structurally impossible. This is the real answer.
  • 3Require an edge on every transition condition, not a level. If the condition must become true, an already-true condition cannot advance the chain.
  • 4Insert a minimum step time — every step must be active for at least one task period, or for a defined minimum (Page 14), before its exit condition is even evaluated.
TWO MORE LADDER TRAPS

Dual coil

The same output coil appears on two rungs. The lower one wins, every scan, silently — the upper rung's logic is dead but looks alive when you monitor it. Most compilers only warn. Rule: one coil, one rung, ever. If two conditions must drive an output, OR them in one rung.

MCR / JMP skipped rungs

Non-retentive outputs inside a skipped region typically go off, but latched outputs, timers and set/reset bits hold their last state. Engineers assume “skipped means safe”; it does not. Anything that must be de-energised in a skipped region must be explicitly reset outside it.

Timers inside skipped code

A TON that is not scanned does not time. It also does not reset. When the region is re-enabled the timer resumes from where it was — producing a “5 second” delay that took four minutes of wall-clock time.

Online edits and step chains

An online edit that inserts a rung changes the scan order and can trigger the run-through bug on a running machine. Test edits on a simulator; never insert into a live step chain during production.

WHERE LADDER IS GENUINELY THE RIGHT ANSWER

Nothing above is an argument against ladder — it is an argument against using ladder for sequencing. For the interlock layer, ladder is not merely acceptable but superior, for four concrete reasons:

1. One rung per cause-and-effect row — an assessor with the C&E matrix can verify the implementation line by line without reading code. 2. Power flow is visible online — the one language where "why is this output off" is answered by looking, in seconds, by a non-programmer. 3. No hidden execution order within a rung — a rung is a Boolean expression, evaluated once, with no side effects. The trap is BETWEEN rungs, not inside one. 4. It is the lingua franca — the person fixing your machine at 3 a.m. in ten years' time will read ladder. That is an engineering property, not a sentimental one.

So the division of labour from Page 8 stands: ladder owns the interlock and permissive layer; the sequence engine lives in ST or SFC and is mirrored into ladder for visibility. Use each language where its execution model is a help rather than a trap.

Quick check: After a fault reset, a 10-step ladder step chain sometimes ends up in step 10 immediately, with the machine having done nothing. Once running normally it never misbehaves. Explain precisely what happens and give the minimal, safe change — and the proper change.
Tap to reveal ↓A: This is the run-through bug, triggered by the fault-reset condition. The rungs are written in ascending step order (S1 at the top, S10 at the bottom). During normal running the transition conditions become true one at a time as the machine physically moves, so each scan advances at most one step and nothing is noticed. But after a fault, the machine has often been left in a position where several transition conditions are already satisfied — the cylinder is home so AtHome is true, the clamp is open so ClampOpen is true, the table is at position so InPosition is true, and so on. The reset sets S1. Rung 1 sets S1 this scan; rung 2 reads S1 (this scan's value, because rung 2 is below rung 1) sees it true and its condition true, so it sets S2 and, via the seal term, clears S1; rung 3 reads S2 from this same scan and does the same; and the cascade continues to the bottom of the chain within a single scan. The outputs are written once at the end of the scan, so all the intermediate steps' outputs are on for exactly zero milliseconds — the machine physically does nothing, and the chain arrives at step 10 looking as though nine steps “completed”. It is deterministic, not intermittent: it happens precisely when the leftover physical state makes the conditions true. The minimal safe change is to reverse the rung order so the chain is written S10 at the top down to S1 at the bottom. Then rung for S(n) reads S(n−1) as written last scan, and the token can advance exactly one step per scan no matter how many conditions are true. This is a pure reordering — no logic changes, so the review burden is small — but it must be accompanied by a large comment block explaining why the order is reversed, because the next engineer's instinct will be to “tidy” it back into ascending order and reintroduce the bug. A complementary minimal change is to make each transition condition edge-triggered rather than level-triggered, so a condition that was already true when the step became active cannot fire it; this also fixes the case where the chain is later extended by someone who does not read comments. The proper change is to replace the one-hot chain with an integer or enumerated step driven by a CASE statement (Page 9). Only one CASE branch executes per scan, so multi-step run-through is not merely unlikely but structurally impossible; the illegal-encoding exposure of Page 3 disappears at the same time; the ELSE branch catches corruption; and one timeout mechanism can serve every step. Alongside that, three habits prevent the whole family of faults: (1) define the reset path as a real homing sequence rather than a jump to step 1 — after a fault the machine's physical state is unknown, so it should drive to a known state and confirm it, not assume it; (2) enforce a minimum time in every step so that even a legitimate fast transition is observable and loggable; and (3) log every step transition with a timestamp, because the evidence that ten transitions occurred inside one millisecond is what turns this diagnosis from an afternoon's guessing into a thirty-second read.

THE LAYERS, AND WHERE SEQUENCE LOGIC SITS
Sequence and interlock logic is a basic process control (BPCS) function. It is a protection layer, but only one, and only if it is independent of what it protects against.

The hard rule that follows: a software interlock in the same controller as the sequence it constrains is not independent of it. A CPU fault, a corrupted variable, an online edit, a forced bit or a firmware defect takes both out together. That does not make software interlocks useless — they prevent the overwhelmingly common failures, which are logic and operator errors, not CPU failures — but it does mean that when the consequence is injury or a major loss, the interlock must move to an independent safety system with its own sensors, logic solver and final elements, designed to IEC 61511 or IEC 62061/ISO 13849 (A52, A35). The question is never “hardwired or software?” in the abstract; it is “what risk reduction does this layer have to deliver, and what integrity is therefore required?”

BYPASS MANAGEMENT — THE HONEST PART

Interlocks will be bypassed. During commissioning, during maintenance, during a start-up when a transmitter is out of range, and at 2 a.m. when production is losing money. Designing as though bypasses will not happen guarantees that they happen informally, with a jumper wire or a force, and that nobody removes them. The professional response is to make the legitimate route easier than the illegitimate one:

  • 1Provide an engineered bypass facility for every interlock that has a foreseeable need — typically start-up overrides and maintenance overrides — instead of leaving forces as the only option.
  • 2Authorise it. Key switch, password level, or a permit referenced by number. The system should record who enabled it.
  • 3Time-limit it. A bypass that expires after a defined period and must be deliberately renewed cannot be forgotten. An automatic expiry with an alarm beats a procedure every time.
  • 4Make it loud. A standing, non-suppressible indication on the HMI overview: “3 interlocks bypassed”, with a list one click away. Bypasses belong on the shift handover report.
  • 5Log it. Enabled by, at, reason, expiry, removed by. This is both an operational and an audit requirement, and it is trivial once the bypass is an engineered object rather than a force.
  • 6Never allow a bypass of a safety-instrumented function from the BPCS. SIS bypasses are a separate, more strongly controlled mechanism with their own procedure — and forcing an SIS I/O point is a reportable event, not a workaround.

The corollary is a rule about forces: a forced I/O point is an undocumented, unauthorised, unlimited, invisible bypass. Every project should have a forces policy — forces are permitted during commissioning only, must be listed daily, and the system must alarm continuously while any force is present. A great many incidents have a forced bit somewhere in the causal chain.

Quick check: A dryer's burner is interlocked on airflow. During a brief fan speed dip the interlock trips, the burner shuts down, and the batch is ruined. Production asks you to “add a 30-second delay so it does not nuisance trip”. Give your answer, and what you would do instead.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The answer is no — not as asked — and the reason must be stated in terms of the hazard, not of process discipline. Airflow interlocking on a burner exists because insufficient airflow means incomplete combustion, accumulation of unburnt fuel and carbon monoxide, and a potential explosion on re-ignition; on a solids dryer it also risks a dust-cloud ignition. The trip's whole purpose is to act before a fuel-rich condition can develop, and the time available is a physical property of the chamber volume and firing rate — typically a very few seconds, not thirty. A 30-second delay does not remove a nuisance trip; it removes the protection for thirty seconds every time the condition occurs, which is precisely when protection is needed. If this airflow interlock is part of a burner management system it will additionally be a safety-instrumented function under IEC 61511 (and probably designed to EN 746 / NFPA 86 requirements), in which case changing its trip delay is a modification to an SIF: it requires a management-of-change, a review against the safety requirements specification, re-validation, and it is very likely prohibited outright. What I would do instead, in order. (1) Find out why the airflow dips — that is the actual fault, and everything else is treating a symptom. Candidates: a fan VFD ride-through or supply dip (A56 — a voltage sag on the fan drive will show as a speed dip; the cure is a drive with ride-through, a DC bus buffer, or correcting the supply), a blinding filter raising system resistance, a damper actuator hunting because its own loop is badly tuned or its valve is sticking (A59), or an airflow measurement problem — a blocked impulse line, a fouled orifice, or a transmitter with an unfiltered signal reading turbulence rather than flow. Trend the fan speed, the drive DC bus voltage, the differential pressure and the trip signal together at high resolution; the shape of the dip usually names the cause immediately. (2) Fix the measurement, not the trip. If the dip is measurement noise rather than real airflow loss, the correct response is a properly engineered signal path: correct sensing element and impulse lines, a filter time constant matched to the physical dynamics, validity checking to NAMUR NE 43, and possibly a second measurement for corroboration. A trip that fires on noise is an instrumentation defect, and fixing it there restores protection instead of removing it. (3) Distinguish the alarm from the trip. Add a low-airflow alarm at a higher setpoint with a modest delay, so the operator gets warning and time to act before the trip point is reached (A53). Most “nuisance trips” are really “no early warning”. (4) Re-examine the setpoint, formally. If the trip point was set with no margin above the normal operating band, it may be genuinely too tight — but changing it is an engineering decision requiring the combustion calculation and the same management-of-change process, not a control-room adjustment. Any small delay that survives that review must be justified against the chamber's fuel-accumulation time, documented, and typically will be on the order of one or two seconds, not thirty. (5) Protect the batch a different way. Production's real requirement is “do not ruin the batch”, which is not the same as “do not trip”. A controlled hold state that preserves the product — maintain circulation, hold temperature with a lower-hazard heat source, or design the recipe with a legitimate HOLD/RESTART capability (Page 12) — converts a trip from a batch loss into an interruption. That is where the engineering effort belongs. The principle: when someone asks you to delay, raise or bypass a protective function to solve a production problem, they have correctly identified a real problem and proposed the one solution that must not be used. Take the problem seriously and refuse the solution.

ISA-88 Batch Control

The standard that separated what to make from how the equipment works — and the best-designed state model in industrial automation.

THE PROBLEM IT SOLVED

Before ISA-88, a recipe change meant a code change. The knowledge of what the product needs and the knowledge of what this particular vessel can do were tangled together in the same ladder, so a new product required a programmer, a shutdown and a revalidation.

ANSI/ISA-88.00.01 (first approved 1995, revised 2010; adopted internationally as IEC 61512-1 in 1997) breaks that tangle with one idea: the recipe holds the product knowledge and the equipment holds the equipment knowledge, and they meet at a defined interface called the phase. A recipe says “charge 250 kg of material A, heat to 65 °C, hold 30 minutes”. The phase logic knows which valve, which pump, which agitator speed, which timeouts, and what to do if the transfer stalls. Change the product and you change data; change the plant and you change code; and neither forces the other.

The standard is in four parts — Part 1 models and terminology (the part everyone means when they say “S88”), Part 2 data structures and language guidelines, Part 3 general and site recipe models, Part 4 batch production records — plus the widely used technical report ISA-TR88.00.02 (2015, revised 2022), which applies the same thinking to discrete machinery as PackML.

THE TWO MODELS
A Unit Procedure runs on one Unit and owns it for the duration. A Phase runs on an Equipment Module and is the smallest thing a recipe can command.

Two definitions do the heavy lifting. A Unit is a collection of equipment in which one batch is processed at a time — a reactor, a blender, a dryer. That “one batch at a time” is not a description but a constraint: it is what makes a unit the natural granularity for allocation and arbitration (Page 13). A Phase is the smallest element of procedural control that can be independently commanded, and it is where the recipe's parameters land: Charge(material := A, quantity := 250 kg, tolerance := 1 %).

Each recipe carries five components: header (identity, version, approvals), formula (process inputs, parameters and outputs), equipment requirements (what kind of unit this needs — jacketed, 5 m³, glass-lined), procedure (the ordered structure of unit procedures, operations and phases), and other information (compliance and documentation). In a regulated industry the version control and approval of the master recipe is the whole of the compliance story; in an unregulated one it is still what stops last month's product being made this month by accident.

THE PHASE STATE MODEL — THE HEART OF IT
Six quiescent states where the phase waits, six transient states where it acts. Every command has a defined effect from every state — the total transition function of Page 3, done properly.

The distinction that makes this model excellent engineering rather than bureaucracy is HOLD versus STOP versus ABORT. HOLD is recoverable: the phase runs its holding logic to put the process into a safe, preservable condition (keep circulating, keep the jacket at temperature, close the feed), waits in HELD indefinitely, and on RESTART runs restarting logic to resume from where it was. STOP is orderly termination: finish safely, but this phase is over. ABORT is fast termination: get to a safe state as quickly as possible, accepting product loss. A batch system without all three ends up using its emergency stop as a pause button, which is how transmitters get damaged and product gets scrapped for want of thirty lines of holding logic.

PackML (ISA-TR88.00.02, latest revision 2022) applies the same architecture to discrete machinery, where it has become the de-facto interface standard for packaging lines. It defines a state model of seventeen states — the four quiescent states IDLE, STOPPED, ABORTED, COMPLETE and HELD/SUSPENDED, plus transients such as STARTING, EXECUTE, COMPLETING, HOLDING, UNHOLDING, SUSPENDING, UNSUSPENDING, RESETTING, STOPPING, ABORTING, CLEARING — three modes (Producing, Maintenance, Manual), and a standard tag set (PackTags) for commands, status and administration. The pay-off is that a line built from five vendors' machines exposes the same states, the same modes and the same counters, so line-level coordination and OEE reporting (A22) become configuration rather than integration.

A distinction worth carrying: PackML's SUSPENDED means “stopped by external circumstances” (starved of product, blocked downstream) while HELD means “stopped by a deliberate command”. Keeping those separate is what makes automatic line-loss accounting possible — a machine that is idle because the machine before it is broken should not be charged with the downtime.

Quick check: A batch system's phases implement only IDLE, RUNNING, COMPLETE and ABORTED. Operations complains that any interruption — a raw material delivery delay, an operator needing to take a sample, a brief utility trip — scraps the batch. What is missing, what would you build, and what does “correct” hold logic actually contain?
Tap to reveal ↓A: What is missing is the entire recoverable-interruption branch of the state model: HOLDING, HELD and RESTARTING (and, for lighter interruptions, PAUSING/PAUSED/RESUME). With only ABORT available, every interruption — however trivial and however recoverable — is handled by the one mechanism designed for “get to a safe state fast and accept the loss”. The operators are behaving rationally; the system has given them a fire axe and no door handle. The difference between PAUSE and HOLD is worth being precise about. PAUSE suspends the phase at a defined safe point in its own logic — it is intended for short, benign interruptions and typically does not change the process state at all (the agitator keeps turning, the temperature controller keeps controlling; the phase simply does not advance). HOLD is for interruptions that require the process to be actively put into a preservable condition, and it is the one that needs real engineering. What correct hold logic contains, phase by phase — and this is the part that cannot be generic: (1) Freeze the process safely. Close feeds, stop dosing, but keep whatever must keep running — agitation to prevent settling or a crust forming, jacket circulation to prevent a temperature excursion or a wax freeze-off, inert blanketing, seal flush. Deciding what must keep running is a process-chemistry question and belongs to the process engineer, not the programmer. (2) Record the exact point of interruption: which step, how much has been charged so far, the elapsed reaction time, the current setpoints. This is the “history” mechanism of Page 3, and without it RESTART cannot resume — it can only restart from the beginning, which for a partially charged vessel is wrong and possibly dangerous. (3) Bound the hold. Product does not wait indefinitely: a hold that exceeds the material's stability time must escalate — alarm, then a defined disposition (continue with adjusted parameters, or abort deliberately). A HELD state with no time limit is a slow-motion quality failure. (4) RESTARTING logic that re-establishes conditions before resuming: bring the temperature back to the setpoint and confirm it, re-establish flow and confirm it, verify the vessel state matches what was recorded, then return to RUNNING at the recorded point. Resuming straight into RUNNING because “the recipe says heat is on” is how a reactor gets a thermal shock. (5) Decide, per phase, whether the interruption is resumable at all. Some phases genuinely are not — a timed reaction with a critical addition profile may be spoiled by any interruption — and the honest design is for those phases to reject HOLD and say so, rather than to offer a hold that silently produces off-specification product. What I would build, and in what order. First, add HOLD/HELD/RESTART to the phase framework itself — the state machine, the command interface, the HMI buttons, the logging — so that the capability exists uniformly. Second, implement real holding and restarting logic for the phases where interruptions actually occur, which the downtime records will identify in an afternoon: charging phases (material delays), sampling points, and any phase spanning a shift change. Third, add PAUSE/RESUME for the short benign cases, so operators stop using HOLD for everything. Fourth — and this is the one people skip — test the hold and restart paths as first-class functionality, on every phase, with the same rigour as the normal path, including holding at the least convenient moment in each phase. The normal path gets tested a thousand times in production; the hold path gets tested once, by an operator, during the incident. The general principle, applicable well beyond batch: the value of a state model is not in the happy path but in the number of recoverable off-normal paths it provides. A machine with only “run” and “abort” converts every disturbance into a loss.

Arbitration & Deadlock in Practice

Page 6 proved it can happen. This page is how you stop it happening on a plant with eleven units and one CIP skid.

WHAT IS ACTUALLY SHARED

Transfer equipment

Pumps, headers, common pipework, manifolds. The classic shared resource, and the one where cross-contamination adds a quality dimension to the allocation problem.

Service skids

CIP/SIP, vacuum, chilled water at limited capacity, nitrogen. Often capacity-limited rather than strictly exclusive — two users at half rate may be legal.

Motion assets

Robots, gantries, cranes, AGVs, tool changers, shuttle cars. Here the shared resource is often space — two arms that can occupy the same volume.

Measurement

A shared weigh hopper, a single analyser on a multiplexed sample system, one vision camera serving two stations.

Buffers

Accumulators, staging tanks, WIP racks. Modelled as a place with k tokens; when full, the upstream blocks, when empty the downstream starves.

People

One operator for three machines is a resource constraint too — and the one most likely to be missing from the model, which is why the simulated throughput never matches reality.

Classify each resource before designing anything: is it exclusive (one user at a time, e.g. a transfer pump), capacity-limited (n simultaneous users, e.g. a chiller that supports two of five jackets at full duty), or shared-use (many users, e.g. a common header where simultaneous use is chemically acceptable)? ISA-88 makes this distinction explicitly, and the arbitration mechanism differs for each: a semaphore initialised to 1, a counting semaphore initialised to n, and no arbitration at all respectively.

Quick check: A plant has six reactors, two CIP skids and one shared transfer pump. Cleaning requires a CIP skid and the pump. Transfers require the pump only. Twice a month everything stops with several reactors waiting. Design the arbitration, and say what you would put on the HMI.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Model it first. Three resource pools: CIP (a counting semaphore with 2 tokens), Pump (exclusive, 1 token), and implicitly each reactor Unit (exclusive to its own batch, handled by the batch engine). Two requester classes: a clean phase needing {CIP, Pump} and a transfer phase needing {Pump}. The deadlock arises the moment a clean phase can take one of its two resources and then wait for the other: reactor A takes a CIP skid and waits for the pump, while reactor B holds the pump for a transfer and — here is the trap — queues for a CIP skid for its own subsequent clean before releasing the pump, or simply cannot complete its transfer because its destination is a vessel that is itself waiting. Both CIP skids can end up held by reactors waiting for the pump, so no clean can finish, so no pump release, so nothing progresses. It happens twice a month rather than daily because it needs a particular overlap of a clean and a transfer, which is exactly the intermittency that gets it dismissed as “a glitch”. The design. (1) Atomic, all-or-nothing allocation for multi-resource phases. The clean phase asks the arbitrator for the complete set {CIP, Pump} in a single indivisible request. If both are available it gets both; otherwise it gets nothing and queues holding nothing. This kills hold-and-wait, and it is the correct primary mechanism here because the phases genuinely know their full resource requirement in advance. (2) A fixed global acquisition order — say Pump before CIP — enforced by putting the ordering inside a shared FB_AcquireSet function block, so no future phase author can get it wrong. This is belt and braces alongside (1), and it protects the cases where atomic allocation is not possible. (3) FIFO queueing with ageing. A single queue per resource set, served in request order, with each waiter's effective priority rising with wait time so a reactor cannot be starved by a run of short transfers. Add an operator override that is logged with a reason. (4) Ownership watchdogs. Alarm if the pump is held beyond the longest legitimate transfer plus margin, or if a CIP skid is held beyond a full clean cycle plus margin, naming the holder and its phase state — this catches leaked resources from aborted phases, which is the other half of “everything stopped”. (5) Release on every exit path, including ABORT and mode change, verified by test. (6) Deadlock detection as a backstop: a wait-for graph cycle check every second, alarming the cycle explicitly, with a deterministic recovery (abort the lowest-priority participant, log the decision). It should never fire once (1) and (2) are in place — and if it does, that is a design defect report, not an operational event. (7) Re-examine the physical constraint. One pump serving six reactors is a single point of both failure and contention; the arbitration is engineering around a plant design decision. Quantify the loss (queue-wait hours per month × contribution per batch) and put the number in front of whoever decides on capital — a second pump may be cheaper than the arbitration's downtime, and that is a legitimate engineering conclusion to reach and to say out loud. What goes on the HMI — one page, always reachable, showing for each resource: current owner, that owner's phase and state, elapsed hold time, the queue in order with each waiter's wait time, and any active override with its reason and who set it. Plus a standing banner when any wait exceeds a threshold, and a trend of queue depth per resource so the contention is visible as a pattern rather than as an incident. That page turns the invisible into the obvious, and it is usually the difference between “the plant randomly stops” and “we need a second pump, here is the evidence”.

Timeouts, Confirmations & Diagnostics

The difference between a machine that stops and a machine that tells you why it stopped is about forty lines of code.

COMMAND IS NOT CONFIRMATION

A step must exit on evidence that the physical world changed, never on the fact that a command was issued. Everything else on this page follows from that one rule.

This is not the same as redundancy for reliability — it is diversity for validity, and it costs almost nothing when the signals already exist.

CHOOSING A TIMEOUT

A timeout that is too tight stops production for nothing. A timeout that is too loose leaves the machine standing for minutes while the fault does damage and the operator loses confidence. The choice is a statistics problem, not a matter of taste.

The wrong method (universal, unfortunately) "Set it to 5 seconds. That feels about right." The right method 1. MEASURE the step's actual duration over several hundred cycles. Every step's elapsed time should be logged anyway (see below). 2. Characterise the distribution: mean μ, standard deviation σ, and — more useful — the observed maximum. 3. Consider the WORST LEGITIMATE case, which is rarely in your sample: • minimum air/hydraulic pressure at shift start • cold oil on a Monday morning (viscosity — A47) • maximum product load, worst material batch • a component at the end of its wear life • supply voltage at the low end of tolerance (A56) 4. Set T_timeout = max(μ + kσ, worst legitimate case) × margin with k around 4–6 and margin 1.2–1.5. Then sanity-check the resulting nuisance-stop rate (demo below). 5. Set a MINIMUM step time too: T_min = physically fastest possible. Completing faster than physics allows is a sensor fault, not success. 6. Re-derive the timeout after any mechanical change. A timeout is a model of the machine, and the machine changes.

One nuance that matters on slow processes: for a step whose duration is genuinely variable — heating a vessel whose contents' initial temperature varies by 30 °C — a fixed timeout is the wrong model entirely. Use a rate-based check instead: fault if the temperature has not risen by at least x degrees in the last y minutes. That detects a failed heater in a couple of minutes regardless of how long the whole step legitimately takes, whereas a fixed timeout must be set to the slowest possible case and therefore detects nothing useful for an hour.

THE DIAGNOSTIC PACKAGE
  • 1“Waiting for” text. The single highest-value item on this list. When a step is active, publish a string naming the exact condition being awaited: “Step 4: waiting for ClampClosed (LS-12)”. Build it from the same expression that drives the transition, so it cannot drift out of date. Operators diagnose 80 % of stoppages from this line alone.
  • 2Step trace buffer. A ring buffer of the last 100 transitions: step number, name, entry time, duration, and the reason for exit (condition met / timeout / abort). This is the machine's black box, and it costs a few hundred bytes.
  • 3Elapsed time per step, trended. Not just for diagnosis — a clamp step that has crept from 1.2 s to 1.9 s over three months is a leaking seal or a failing valve, detected before it stops the line. This is condition monitoring for free (A42, A23).
  • 4First-out capture. When a trip cascade occurs, record which cause was first, latched at high speed, before the others follow. Without it, an operator sees eight alarms and no information (A53).
  • 5Rejected-event log. Every command or event that arrived in a state where it is not valid (Page 3). Reveals sensor glitches, HMI misuse and race conditions that otherwise leave no trace.
  • 6Fault codes with a destination. FaultCode = 104 should decode on the HMI to “timed out in step 4 (clamp), waiting for ClampClosed”, together with the recommended action. A number with no lookup table is not a diagnostic.
  • 7Resource ownership view (Page 13) and bypass view (Page 11) — the two states that are invisible until they cause a stoppage.

Note that six of these seven cost only software and produce no runtime burden worth measuring. The economics are unambiguous: a machine that says “waiting for ClampClosed” is repaired by the operator in two minutes; the same machine without that line waits for a technician, a laptop and a program listing. Over a year, the difference is measured in shifts.

RECOVERY & RESTART

The last piece is what happens after the fault is cleared. The failure mode to design against is the machine that cannot be restarted without a power cycle, because its logic assumes a physical state that no longer holds.

The homing (initialisation) sequence — not optional On reset, the machine must not ASSUME its position. It must: 1. Read every position sensor and classify the actual state. 2. If the state is recognised and safe → move deliberately, at reduced speed, to the home position, one axis at a time, in an order that cannot cause a collision. 3. If the state is NOT recognised (both limit switches on, or neither) → refuse to move automatically. Ask for manual intervention and say exactly which sensor pair is inconsistent. 4. Confirm home on every axis before allowing auto mode. Degraded modes — decide deliberately, in advance For each failure, choose ONE and write it in the specification: • stop immediately (safety-relevant) • stop at end of cycle (quality-relevant, GEMMA A2) • continue with a limitation (e.g. one of two heads disabled) • continue and alarm (non-critical instrument) The default of "stop immediately for everything" costs availability; the default of "continue for everything" costs product and equipment.

And the rule from Page 11, restated because it is where the two topics meet: clearing a fault restores permission, not command. The homing sequence itself is a deliberate operator action from a position where the machine is visible, and auto mode is reachable only after homing confirms.

Quick check: Maintenance reports that a machine “randomly faults on step 6” roughly twice a week, always at the start of the morning shift, never after lunch. The step 6 timeout is 4 s. How would you investigate, and what is the most likely answer?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The time-of-day pattern is the whole clue, and it says the fault is environmental or start-of-day, not random. “Random” faults that cluster at shift start are almost never random; something about the first hour is different. Investigation, in order. (1) Get the data before forming a theory. Pull the step trace buffer and extract step 6's elapsed time for every cycle over the last few weeks, then plot it against time of day. You are looking for the distribution, not the failures: if step 6 normally takes 1.8 s but takes 3.4 s for the first 200 cycles of each day, then the 4 s timeout is not tripping on a fault at all — it is clipping the tail of a legitimate but slower cold-machine distribution. That single plot usually ends the investigation, and it exists only because somebody logged step times (item 3 on the diagnostic list). (2) Read the “waiting for” string and the fault code from the stoppages: which specific condition failed to arrive? That names the physical device. (3) Trend the obvious environmental candidates against the same clock: compressed-air header pressure (lowest at shift start when every machine on site starts drawing and the receiver has been depleted overnight — A47), hydraulic or gearbox oil temperature (cold oil is far more viscous, so cylinders and slides move measurably slower until the machine warms), ambient temperature, and supply voltage (site load steps up at shift start, and a sagging supply slows a drive — A56). (4) Check what else starts at that time: a large compressor, a chiller, another line, a canteen load. (5) Confirm with a controlled test: run the machine cold, deliberately, and measure step 6. If it reproduces, you are done. The most likely answer is that step 6 is genuinely slower when the machine is cold and the air pressure is at its daily minimum, and the 4 s timeout was set by feel against warm, mid-shift performance — so it has no margin for the legitimate worst case. It is a timeout design defect masquerading as an equipment fault, and the tell is that it always occurs at the same point in the day and never in the afternoon. The fix has three parts, and only the first is code. (a) Re-derive the timeout from the measured distribution including cold-start cycles: T = max(μ + 5σ, worst cold-start observed) × 1.3 — likely 7–8 s rather than 4 s. Do this per step, not globally. (b) Fix the underlying physical cause where it is worth fixing: if the air pressure genuinely dips below the machine's specified minimum at shift start, that is a compressed-air system problem affecting every machine on site and worth quantifying (receiver sizing, sequencing of large users, leak survey); if it is cold oil, consider a warm-up cycle or a tank heater. (c) Add a cold-start / warm-up mode that runs the first cycles at a reduced rate with relaxed step timeouts and a clear HMI indication, then reverts — this is a legitimate, documented degraded mode, not a fudge, and it removes the failure without blinding the machine for the rest of the day. What I would refuse to do is simply raise every timeout to a large number to make the alarms stop. That is the equivalent of the burner-delay request on Page 11: it removes the symptom and the diagnostic capability together, and the next fault — a genuinely stuck cylinder — will now sit undetected for the full inflated timeout while it does damage. The principle: a timeout is a model of how long the step should take. When it fires often, first ask whether the model is wrong before assuming the machine is.

Verification, Testing & Recap

Sequential software cannot be tested by running it once and watching. Here is what actually works.

WHY IT IS HARD

A continuous loop can be characterised by a step test: one experiment, and you know the gain, the time constant and the dead time. A sequence has no such summary. Its behaviour is a function of state and history, so “it worked” means only “that one path worked, on that day, from that starting state”. The number of paths through a modest machine is large enough that exhaustive testing by hand is out of the question.

The coverage ladder — use the highest one you can afford 1. Happy-path one run through the normal cycle. Covers perhaps 20 % of the code. This is what most machines actually get, and it is why commissioning takes six weeks. 2. State coverage every state entered at least once. Necessary, nowhere near sufficient. 3. Transition coverage every arrow in the diagram traversed. The realistic professional target. If your table has 40 transitions, you have 40 tests. Write them down. 4. (state × event) coverage every cell of the full matrix, INCLUDING the illegal ones — because "what does it do if I press Start here" is exactly what an operator will do at 2 a.m. n states × m events tests. 5. Path coverage every route through the chart. Combinatorial; feasible only with a tool. 6. Model checking PROVE properties over ALL paths. Feasible today with free tools, for nets of realistic size.

THE SEQUENCE CODE REVIEW CHECKLIST
  • 1Is there one, named, enumerated state variable with exactly one writer?
  • 2Does every state have a defined action for every event, including an ELSE / default that goes somewhere safe?
  • 3Does every waiting state have a timeout with a destination and a minimum time?
  • 4Is every output assigned in exactly one place, as a function of state?
  • 5Does every step exit on confirmation, never on a command or a bare timer?
  • 6Does every S / SET have a matching R / RESET on every exit path, including abort, fault and power-up?
  • 7Is every acquired resource released on every exit path, in reverse acquisition order?
  • 8Are selective branch conditions mutually exclusive, and does every simultaneous branch have a completion path?
  • 9Is the initial marking reachable from every state — can the machine always get home?
  • 10Is there a homing sequence that verifies rather than assumes physical state?
  • 11Are step number, elapsed time, waiting-for text and the trace buffer exposed to the HMI and the historian?
  • 12Can the whole thing run at a desk with simulated devices?

Twelve questions, perhaps forty minutes for a machine-sized program, and in most reviews at least four of them come back “no”. That is not a criticism of the author — it is a description of what happens to any code written against a deadline without a checklist.

CHANGE CONTROL

Two practices are worth insisting on regardless of industry. Text-based source under version control: ST and PLCopen XML (IEC 61131-10) diff meaningfully, so a change review is a two-minute read rather than an act of faith; binary project files do not, and a project whose only history is Machine_v7_final_JOHN_USE_THIS.zip has no history at all. And a documented online-change policy: online edits are how commissioning gets done, but an online edit to a running step chain can trigger the run-through bug of Page 10, can leave a variable in a state no code path would produce, and is invisible afterwards unless it is uploaded and committed. Rule: online edits are permitted during commissioning, uploaded and committed the same day, and prohibited in production without a change record.

In regulated industries this is formalised — GAMP 5 categorises software and scales validation effort accordingly, with configured products and bespoke code attracting the most; ISA-88 batch records and electronic-signature requirements add their own burden. But the underlying engineering is the same in an unregulated plant: know what is running, know what changed, know who approved it, and be able to get back to what worked yesterday.

RECAP — THE EIGHT IDEAS

1 · Different discipline

Sequential control is logic and automata, not calculus. Its failures are stuck steps, races and deadlocks — and none of them respond to tuning.

2 · Make the state explicit

One named, enumerated state variable with one writer. This single change takes a program from unmaintainable to reviewable.

3 · Total transition function

Every (state, event) pair has a defined outcome, including the illegal ones. The ELSE branch is not optional.

4 · Draw before you code

GRAFCET specifies, SFC implements. The drawing the process engineer signs should be the thing that runs.

5 · Concurrency needs proof

Shared resources plus independent sequences equals possible deadlock. Order the acquisitions, or allocate atomically, and prove it with a P-invariant.

6 · Confirm, never assume

A step exits on evidence that the world changed. Every wait has a timeout with a destination, derived from measured data.

7 · Recoverable off-normal paths

HOLD, RESTART and ABORT are where the engineering value is. A machine with only run and abort turns every disturbance into a loss.

8 · Diagnostics are the deliverable

“Waiting for ClampClosed (LS-12)” plus a step trace costs forty lines and saves shifts. Build it before you need it.

SIX FAILURE MODES TO RECOGNISE ON SIGHT

Race condition

Two asynchronous events required in the same scan. Symptom: occasional missed cycle, sensors test good. Cure: latch the events or sequence them.

Run-through

Ascending ladder step chain with conditions already true — the whole cycle executes in one scan. Symptom: machine “skips” steps after a reset. Cure: CASE, or descending rung order.

Circular-wait deadlock

Two sequences acquiring two resources in opposite orders. Symptom: intermittent total freeze, no alarm. Cure: ordered or atomic acquisition.

Orphaned token

Simultaneous divergence closed by a selective convergence, or a branch with no completion path. Symptom: machine stops silently and needs a power cycle. Cure: matched convergence, per-branch timeouts, enclosing step.

Stored action never reset

An S qualifier or SET instruction whose R is on a path the abort never takes. Symptom: an output on when the sequence says it is idle. Cure: N qualifier, or reset on every exit path.

Assumed confirmation

A step that exits on a timer or on its own command. Symptom: the machine proceeds with a valve shut, a part missing or a heater dead. Cure: confirm from a physically different phenomenon, plus a minimum-time plausibility check.

Final check 1: Why is “the machine ran a full cycle correctly” almost worthless as evidence that a sequence is correct, and what is the minimum evidence you would accept?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because a full cycle exercises one path through the state graph, taken from one starting state, with no events out of order and no faults — typically 20 % or less of the reachable behaviour, and none of the behaviour that actually causes downtime. Every defect in the six failure modes above is invisible on a clean cycle: the race condition needs two events to coincide, the run-through needs conditions already true, the deadlock needs a particular interleaving, the orphaned token needs a branch to stall, the unreset stored action needs an abort, and the assumed confirmation needs a device to fail. None of those happen during a successful demonstration — by definition. The minimum evidence I would accept is: (1) transition coverage — a written test list with one test per arrow in the state table, each executed and signed; (2) every timeout demonstrated, by physically preventing each confirmation (block the limit switch, close a manual valve) and verifying the machine faults with the correct code and message within the expected time — this also proves the diagnostics work, which nothing else does; (3) abort and hold from every step, verifying that all stored outputs clear, all resources release and the machine can be homed and restarted afterwards; (4) illegal-event testing — press every button in every state and confirm nothing unexpected happens and the rejection is logged; (5) restart from an unknown physical state, set up by hand, verifying the homing sequence classifies it correctly or refuses safely; and (6) for anything with shared resources, a concurrency test or a formal check, because that class of defect cannot be found by testing the units one at a time. That is perhaps a day of testing for a machine-sized sequence, most of it done at the desk against simulated devices, and it is the difference between a six-week commissioning and a one-week one.
Final check 2: Give the one-sentence rule for choosing between a permissive and an interlock, and explain what goes wrong in each direction if you choose incorrectly.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The rule: ask whether the condition protects the act of starting or the state of running — if losing it while running creates a hazard or damage, it is an interlock; if losing it while running is harmless because the act it protected is already complete, it is a permissive. Choosing interlock where a permissive was correct costs availability: a momentary dip in a condition that only mattered at start — lube pressure once the oil film is established, a purge that is already complete, a tank level that only had to be adequate at the beginning — stops production for nothing, and the operators' rational response is to demand a delay, a bypass or a “maintenance mode” that quietly becomes the normal mode. Over-interlocking is therefore self-defeating: it produces the bypass culture that removes the protection you actually needed. Choosing permissive where an interlock was correct is the dangerous direction: the guard that had to be shut to start can now be opened while the spindle turns; the cooling flow that had to be present at start can be lost during the run; the extraction fan that was proved at start can trip and leave the process running. The condition is checked once, at the least informative moment, and then never again. The practical discipline: classify every condition explicitly in the cause-and-effect matrix, have the classification signed by the process or machine engineer rather than decided by the programmer, and record for each interlock what the response is (stop immediately, stop at end of cycle, controlled shutdown) and how it resets. Where a condition is genuinely a permissive but its loss during running still matters somewhat, the correct answer is usually neither — it is an alarm with an operator response, which is a third protection layer and exists precisely for the cases that are too important to ignore and too benign to trip.
Final check 3: You inherit a machine whose sequence is a pile of latched coils with no step variable. You have three days before a production restart. What do you do?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Do not rewrite it. Three days is not enough to rewrite and re-test a sequence you do not yet understand, and a half-finished rewrite is worse than the original. Instead, spend the time on observability and containment, which is cheap, low-risk and delivers most of the benefit. Day 1 — make the machine legible. Reverse-engineer the state graph by reading the latches and writing down, for each latch, what sets it and what clears it; this produces a state-transition table in a few hours and is the single most valuable artefact you can create. Then add a derived, read-only step variable: one line of code that decodes the existing latches into an integer or enum (IF S1 THEN Step := 1; ELSIF S2 THEN Step := 2; ...), plus a consistency check that alarms if the number of active latches is not exactly one (Page 3). This changes no behaviour whatsoever — it only observes — so the risk is near zero, and it immediately makes the machine trendable, alarmable and explicable. Day 2 — add diagnostics and guards. A step trace buffer, a “waiting for” string per step built from the existing transition conditions, elapsed-time logging per step, and a step timeout per step that raises an alarm (initially alarm-only, not fault-forcing, so you cannot make availability worse). Put the step number, name and elapsed time on the HMI. Now, when the machine misbehaves after restart, you will know where and why within seconds — which is exactly the capability you will need in week one. Day 3 — test the paths you can and document what you found. Run the transition-coverage tests you can safely run, exercise abort and reset from each step, note which stored outputs fail to clear (this is where the latent bugs will be), and write it all up as a prioritised defect list with an estimate. Fix only defects that are safety-relevant or that you are confident about; leave the rest documented. Then propose the rewrite properly, with evidence: the state table you produced, the defect list, the downtime data the new diagnostics will start generating, and a plan to migrate to a CASE-based state machine at the next scheduled shutdown, in a way that keeps the existing latch bits as derived outputs so the ladder mirror and any external interfaces keep working. The general lesson: under time pressure, prefer changes that add information over changes that alter behaviour. Observation is almost free and almost risk-free; modification is neither. And the state table you write on day one is the thing that makes every later decision — including whether to rewrite at all — an informed one.

WE1 — 2oo3 voting: logic, contacts and spurious trips.
Three independent pressure transmitters protect a vessel. The trip must occur when any two indicate high pressure. (a) Derive the minimal sum-of-products expression from the truth table. (b) Compare the contact count of the canonical form with the minimal form. (c) Each transmitter has a spurious (fail-to-trip-state) rate of 0.2 per year and a mean time to repair of 8 hours. Compare the spurious trip rate of 1oo1, 1oo2 and 2oo3.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) The truth table and its minimisation. With inputs A, B, C the output is 1 for every row with two or more 1s, i.e. minterms m3 (011), m5 (101), m6 (110) and m7 (111) — four minterms. On a three-variable Karnaugh map these four cells form three overlapping pairs, and each pair kills one variable: m3+m7 gives BC (A vanishes), m5+m7 gives AC (B vanishes), m6+m7 gives AB (C vanishes). Every one of these is a prime implicant, and each of m3, m5, m6 is covered by exactly one of them, so all three are essential. The minimal sum of products is therefore Y = AB + AC + BC — the majority function. Note that m7 is covered three times over; the redundancy is unavoidable and harmless. (b) Contact count. The canonical SOP has four three-input AND terms, so 4 × 3 = 12 contacts plus a four-way OR. The minimal form has three two-input AND terms, so 3 × 2 = 6 contacts plus a three-way OR — a 50 % reduction. In ladder that is three rungs in parallel rather than four longer ones. In a modern PLC the scan-time difference is negligible; the real gains are that the minimal expression is comprehensible at a glance, that it obviously encodes “any two”, and that a reviewer can verify it against the safety requirements specification without algebra. (In hardwired relay or FPGA implementations the 50 % saving is real money and real failure rate.) (c) Spurious trip rates. For 1oo1, any single spurious signal trips the plant, so the rate is simply λs = 0.2 per year — one nuisance shutdown every five years. For 1oo2, either transmitter can trip the plant, so the rates add: 2λs = 0.4 per year, one every 2.5 years. The 1oo2 arrangement halves the dangerous (fail-to-trip) probability at the cost of doubling the nuisance rate — the fundamental availability/safety trade-off. For 2oo3, two transmitters must be in the spurious state simultaneously. The first fails at rate 3λs (any of three) and remains undetected/unrepaired for the mean repair time τ = 8 h = 8/8760 = 9.132 × 10−4 yr; during that window either of the two survivors can fail, at rate 2λs. Hence the rate of spurious trips is approximately 3λs × 2λs × τ / 2 = 3λs²τ = 3 × 0.04 × 9.132 × 10−4 = 1.10 × 10−4 per year, or one spurious trip every 9 125 years. The engineering conclusion. Relative to 1oo1, the 2oo3 arrangement reduces spurious trips by a factor of about 1 800 and reduces the dangerous failure probability, which is why it is the standard compromise for high-consequence, high-availability duties. Two honest caveats, both of which dominate the arithmetic in practice: (1) this calculation assumes independent failures, whereas common-cause failures — a shared impulse line freezing, a shared power supply, a batch of transmitters with the same firmware defect, one technician mis-calibrating all three — are modelled by the β-factor and typically set the floor on achievable performance, not the random-failure arithmetic; and (2) the voting logic itself, the logic solver and the final elements all contribute, so the transmitters' contribution is only one term in the loop's PFD. See A52 for the full treatment.
WE2 — Scan order, run-through and the throughput cost of a slow task.
A 10-step ladder step chain runs in a 20 ms task. (a) After a reset, all ten transition conditions happen to be true. How long does the chain take to reach step 10 if the rungs are in ascending order, and if they are in descending order? What does the machine physically do in each case? (b) A different machine has a 40-step sequence, a nominal physical cycle time of 12.0 s and runs in a 100 ms task. Quantify the throughput cost of the scan, and of moving the sequence to a 10 ms task.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) Ascending order: one scan, 20 ms — and the machine does nothing. Rung 1 sets S1; rung 2, executing later in the same scan, reads S1 as already true, finds its condition true, sets S2 and clears S1; rung 3 does the same with S2; and so on to the bottom. All ten steps are entered and left within a single scan. Because outputs are written to the field only at the end of the scan, every intermediate step's outputs are asserted for 0 ms — the solenoids never see a command. The machine sits still while the chain reports that it has completed the cycle, which is exactly the symptom “it skipped the whole sequence after reset”. Descending order: ten scans, 200 ms. Rung for S(n) now sits above the rung for S(n−1), so it reads S(n−1) as written in the previous scan. The token can advance exactly one step per scan regardless of how many conditions are true, so each step's outputs are asserted for at least one full scan (20 ms) and the machine moves — quickly, but correctly. The difference is a factor of ten in elapsed time and the difference between a working machine and a phantom cycle, from nothing but the order of the rungs. (The proper fix remains an integer step with a CASE, where only one branch executes per scan by construction.) (b) The scan penalty. Each step costs, on average, about two task periods of pure logic latency: roughly one period for the transition condition to be seen after the physical event (the event may occur just after the input read), and one for the resulting output to be written. With 40 steps at 100 ms that is 40 × 2 × 0.100 = 8.0 s per cycle of pure logic overhead on top of 12.0 s of physical motion. Cycle time becomes 20.0 s, so throughput falls from 3600/12.0 = 300 parts/hour to 3600/20.0 = 180 parts/hour — a 40 % loss, entirely invisible on any mechanical drawing and entirely attributable to a task-configuration decision. Moving the sequence to a 10 ms task reduces the penalty to 40 × 2 × 0.010 = 0.8 s, giving a 12.8 s cycle and 281 parts/hour — recovering 101 parts/hour, or about 800 parts a shift, for a configuration change. The lessons. First, on a machine with many short steps, the scan is part of the cycle time, and its contribution scales with the number of steps, not with the amount of code. Second, the correct instinct when a fast machine underperforms is to count the steps and multiply by two task periods before blaming the mechanics. Third, there is a real trade-off: a 10 ms task costs CPU load and may not be achievable if the sequence shares a task with heavy computation, so the professional answer is to split the tasks — the sequence and its interlocks in a fast task, the recipe handling, reporting and communications in a slow one. Fourth, consolidating steps helps too: steps that exist only as programming convenience (a “wait one scan” step, a separate step per solenoid where two could fire together) each cost 2 × Ttask and should be merged.
WE3 — Petri net analysis of two units sharing two resources.

(c) Build the reachability set and identify any dead marking. (d) What is the probability that a random schedule deadlocks before either unit completes one cycle? (e) Show that reversing B's acquisition order removes the deadlock, and state the general rule.

These last two prove mutual exclusion for both resources, for every reachable marking, without simulation — and they prove every place is 1-bounded, so each maps to a single BOOL in the PLC. (c) Reachability. Exploring from M₀ = (p1,p2,a0,b0 = 1) yields six reachable markings, one of which enables no transition: Md = (a1 = 1, b1 = 1), i.e. A holds R1 and waits for R2 while B holds R2 and waits for R1. Both resource places are empty, both units are in their “holding one” place, and the net is dead. All four Coffman conditions hold simultaneously. (d) Probability of deadlock in the first cycle = exactly 0.5. From M₀ two transitions are enabled, t1 and t4, symmetric. Take t1 (A gets R1). Now two transitions are enabled: t2 (A takes R2, after which A completes and releases) and t4 (B takes R2, giving Md). Under a uniform scheduler each has probability ½. The t4 branch reaches deadlock; the t2 branch lets A finish. By symmetry the same holds if t4 fires first. So P(deadlock before either unit releases) = 0.500. Allowed to continue, the survival probability per round is ½, so P(no deadlock after n rounds) = 2−n → 0: deadlock is certain in the long run, with a median of about five transitions. This is why the defect passes acceptance testing and fails in production. (e) Ordered acquisition. Change B to take R1 then R2. The reachability set is now five markings and none is dead: whichever unit obtains R1 first is guaranteed to obtain R2 as well (nothing else can hold R2 without first holding R1), so it completes and releases both, after which the other proceeds. The circular wait cannot form because the wait-for graph can only ever contain edges in one direction. The general rule: impose a single global order on all shared resources; every requester acquires in that order and releases in the reverse order. It costs nothing at runtime, it is provable rather than testable, and it must be embodied in a shared acquire function block rather than left to the discipline of each programmer — because the defect is created by two people making locally reasonable choices on different days.
WE4 — Batch arbitration: sizing and scheduling a shared CIP skid.
Four reactors each run a 6-hour batch cycle ending with a 45-minute clean on a single shared CIP skid. (a) What is the skid's utilisation, and how many reactors could it serve in principle? (b) What is the worst-case effect on throughput if all four finish together? (c) Propose a scheduling fix and show it works. (d) What would you monitor?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) Utilisation. Demand = 4 reactors × 45 min = 180 min of skid time per 360 min cycle, so utilisation is 180/360 = 50 %. In principle one skid could serve 360/45 = 8 reactors at 100 % utilisation. On capacity alone there is no problem whatsoever — which is precisely why nobody anticipates the contention. Average utilisation is not the constraint; simultaneity is. (b) Worst case. If all four reactors finish their processing at the same instant, the skid serves them one after another. The first waits 0, the second 45 min, the third 90 min and the fourth 135 min. That last reactor's cycle becomes 360 + 135 = 495 min = 8.25 h instead of 6 h. Its output falls from 24/6 = 4.00 to 24/8.25 = 2.91 batches/day. If the clash persists (and it tends to, because the delayed reactors restart together and stay in phase — the system is self-synchronising), plant output falls from 4 × 4 = 16.0 to about 11.6 batches/day, a 27 % loss from a resource that is only half utilised. Worse, the queue is invisible: each reactor reports itself “waiting”, no alarm is raised, and the OEE system books the time against the reactor rather than against the skid contention (A22). (c) The fix: stagger the batch starts. With four reactors on a 360 min cycle, stagger the start times by 360/4 = 90 min. Since 90 min > the 45 min clean, no two cleans can overlap and the queue length is provably zero: reactor i requests the skid at t = 360k + 90i + 315, and consecutive requests are 90 min apart against a 45 min service time, leaving 45 min of slack for drift. Output returns to 16.0 batches/day, and the skid remains at 50 % utilisation with a comfortable margin. This is a scheduling solution, not a control-logic one, and it is much cheaper than the alternatives. Two supporting measures make it robust in the presence of real-world drift: (i) implement the arbitration properly anyway (atomic allocation, FIFO queue with ageing, ownership watchdog — Page 13), because a single long batch or an unplanned clean will break the stagger and you need the queue to behave sensibly when it does; and (ii) allow the batch engine to start a reactor early or late by a few minutes to re-establish the phasing after a disturbance, rather than letting the reactors drift into synchrony. (d) What to monitor. Per resource: current owner, hold time, queue depth and each waiter's wait time, all trended. The key derived metric is cumulative queue-wait hours per month, because it converts an invisible logic problem into a number in the same units as production loss: at, say, 20 queue-hours a month and a batch worth a known contribution, the cost of contention becomes directly comparable with the capital cost of a second skid. Also monitor the phase of the four cycles (a simple plot of each reactor's cycle start against clock time) so that drift towards synchrony is visible weeks before it costs anything, and alarm on any wait exceeding a threshold. The transferable lesson: a shared resource at 50 % average utilisation can still cost a quarter of your output, because queueing is driven by the coincidence of demand, not by its average. Whenever you see a shared resource, ask not “is there enough capacity?” but “what happens when everyone wants it at once, and what keeps them from wanting it at once?”
WE5 — Setting a timeout from measured data.
A clamp step is logged over three weeks. Warm-running cycles: mean 1.82 s, standard deviation 0.21 s. Cold morning cycles (about 200 per day): mean 2.90 s, standard deviation 0.38 s, maximum observed 3.10 s. The current timeout is 4.0 s and the machine faults roughly twice a week. (a) Explain the observed fault rate. (b) Derive a defensible timeout. (c) What is the cost of the new value? (d) What else would you change?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) Explaining the fault rate. Against the warm distribution the 4.0 s timeout sits at z = (4.00 − 1.82)/0.21 = 10.4 standard deviations — the probability of a warm cycle exceeding it is effectively zero, which is why nothing ever happens in the afternoon. Against the cold distribution it sits at z = (4.00 − 2.90)/0.38 = 2.90σ, giving a per-cycle exceedance probability of 1 − Φ(2.90) = 1.90 × 10−3. With about 200 cold cycles per day that is 200 × 1.90 × 10−3 = 0.38 faults per day, or 2.7 per week — which matches the reported “roughly twice a week” and confirms the diagnosis without needing any further evidence. The machine is not faulty. The timeout is wrong. (b) Deriving the timeout. Use the larger of a statistical bound and the observed worst legitimate case, then apply a margin: μ + 5σ on the warm data is 1.82 + 5(0.21) = 2.87 s; the worst legitimate observation (cold) is 3.10 s; so T = max(2.87, 3.10) × 1.3 = 4.03 s. But that calculation is done against the wrong reference: the correct practice is to bound the worst legitimate operating condition, which here is the cold distribution, giving μc + 5σc = 2.90 + 1.90 = 4.80 s, times a 1.15 margin ≈ 5.5 s. At 5.5 s the cold-cycle exceedance probability is 1 − Φ(6.84) ≈ 3.9 × 10−12 — about one nuisance stop every ten million years, i.e. never. Choose 5.5 s. Note how sharply the answer depends on which distribution you bound: bounding the warm data alone gives 4.0 s and reproduces the original defect exactly, which is almost certainly how the original value was chosen. (c) The cost. Two things get worse and both are acceptable here. First, detection delay: when the clamp genuinely fails, the machine now stands for 5.5 s rather than 4.0 s before faulting — 1.5 s of extra dwell, which for a clamp is harmless (it would matter for a heater or a pump running dry, and there the answer would be a different detection method, not a longer timeout). Second, diagnostic sensitivity: a step that is degrading — a leaking seal slowly stretching the clamp time from 1.8 s towards 3 s — will now go unremarked for longer. Recover that by adding a warning threshold at, say, 3.5 s that alarms but does not stop, and by trending the step's mean and spread weekly. That gives early warning without nuisance stops, which is exactly the separation of alarm from trip discussed on Page 11. (d) What else to change. (i) Add a minimum step time — if the clamp confirms in under, say, 0.9 s, the mechanism cannot physically have travelled, so treat it as a sensor fault rather than success. (ii) Add a cold-start mode that relaxes timeouts for the first N cycles or until oil/air conditions are proven, with a clear HMI indication, so the whole day is not compromised by the first hour. (iii) Investigate why cold cycles are 60 % slower — the likely candidates are air-header pressure at shift start and cold hydraulic oil viscosity (A47); fixing either recovers cycle time on every cold cycle, which is worth more than the timeout change. (iv) Apply the same method to every other step: this machine almost certainly has a dozen timeouts set by feel, and the same three-week dataset will re-derive all of them in an afternoon. (v) Make step-time logging permanent, because the entire analysis above was possible only because somebody had logged the elapsed time per step — without it, this is a fortnight of guessing.

Problem Set

P1. A press permissive is written Ready = GuardShut · LubeOK · AirOK · NoEstop · NoOvertemp · DoorLocked. Rewrite it in the complementary form using De Morgan, count the contacts in each, and explain the maintenance and diagnostic advantages of the second form.
Tap to reveal ↓A: By De Morgan, Ready = (GuardOpen + LubeLow + AirLow + Estop + Overtemp + DoorUnlocked)̄ — the complement of an OR of the six fault conditions. Both forms use six contacts, so there is no gate-count argument either way; the case rests entirely on maintainability and diagnostics. Advantages of the fault-OR form. (1) Extensibility: adding a seventh interlock means adding one term to an OR list, with no risk of disturbing the existing logic; in the AND-of-healths form you edit a series string that six other things depend on. (2) Free first-out list: the OR list is the list of things that can block the press, in exactly the form the HMI needs — latch the first term that goes true and you have a first-out alarm for nothing (Page 14). (3) It names faults, not healths: the terms read as the operator experiences them (“guard open”, not “guard shut is false”), so the HMI text writes itself and the tag names stop containing double negatives. (4) It matches the cause-and-effect matrix, whose rows are causes — i.e. faults — making the code traceable to the specification row by row. The one thing that must not change: the field wiring convention. Each “fault” term must still be derived from a signal that is energised in the healthy state (normally-closed contacts, read as 1 = healthy), so a broken wire produces the fault. Deriving GuardOpen directly from a normally-open guard contact would make a cut cable look like a shut guard, which is the failure the entire convention exists to prevent. Name the raw input GuardShutOK and compute GuardOpen := NOT GuardShutOK in one place.
P2. A four-state machine (IDLE, RUN, HOLD, FAULT) responds to four events (start, hold, resume, trip). Only these transitions are documented: IDLE–start→RUN, RUN–hold→HOLD, HOLD–resume→RUN, RUN–trip→FAULT. Complete the full 4×4 transition table and justify every cell you add.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Sixteen cells; four are given, so twelve must be decided — and the fact that a design review found only four documented is itself the finding. A defensible completion: IDLE: start→RUN (given); hold→IDLE (ignore — nothing to hold, but log the rejected event); resume→IDLE (ignore, log); trip→FAULT (a trip is valid from every state — the plant does not care what the sequence thinks). RUN: start→RUN (ignore — idempotent, and a second press of Start must never restart a cycle mid-flight); hold→HOLD (given); resume→RUN (ignore); trip→FAULT (given). HOLD: start→HOLD (ignore — use resume, not start; accepting start here is a classic way to skip the restart logic); hold→HOLD (ignore); resume→RUN (given); trip→FAULT. FAULT: start→FAULT (ignore — the machine must be reset deliberately, and a restart must never be a side effect of pressing Start); hold→FAULT; resume→FAULT; trip→FAULT (already there). The justifications that matter. (i) trip is accepted from every state, including FAULT itself, because a protective input must never be conditional on the sequence's opinion of the world. (ii) Every “ignore” is an explicit decision, logged, not an omission — the rejected-event log is what later reveals a bouncing pushbutton or an operator fighting the machine. (iii) FAULT has no automatic exit. The table as given contains no way out of FAULT at all, which is the biggest gap: a fifth event, reset, is required, and its transition must be FAULT–reset→IDLE conditional on the fault cause having cleared, otherwise the machine chatters in and out of FAULT. Adding it makes the table 4×5 = 20 cells. (iv) A sixth consideration: should HOLD accept trip differently from RUN? Usually not, but if HOLD is maintaining a protective condition (circulation, cooling), the trip's actions must not remove it — which is a reminder that the transition table is only half the specification; the actions in each state are the other half.
P3. Choose the correct IEC 61131-3 action qualifier for each requirement, and say what goes wrong with the obvious alternative. (a) A solenoid energised only while the step is active. (b) Increment a batch counter once when the step starts. (c) Open a vent valve, but for no more than 2 s. (d) Latch a “cleaning required” flag only if the step stays active for a full 10 s. (e) Start a 30 s purge timer that must complete even if the sequence moves on.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) N (non-stored). True while the step is active, false the instant it deactivates — the safe default and a Moore output. The obvious alternative, S, creates an obligation to reset somewhere; if the sequence aborts before that reset the solenoid stays on, which is the failure on Page 4. (b) P1 (rising pulse) — executed for one scan on step activation. Using plain N with an increment instruction would add one per scan, giving a counter that races upward; using P is acceptable on runtimes where P means “once per activation”, but P1 states the intent unambiguously. (c) L (time limited), PT := T#2s. True on activation, forced false after 2 s even if the step is still active. The alternative — N plus a separate timer and an interlock — works but puts the time limit somewhere other than the action it limits, so a later reader can remove one without the other. (d) DS (delayed and stored). The timer runs while the step is active; only if the step is still active after 10 s does the variable get set, and it then stays set. The trap is SD, which looks identical in the manual: with SD the timer continues after the step deactivates and the flag is set regardless — so a step that lasted 2 s would still latch “cleaning required” eight seconds later, from a step that is long gone. Read the pair as “Delayed-then-Stored requires continuous activity; Stored-and-Delayed does not”. (e) SD (stored and time delayed), or more commonly an SL pairing depending on what “complete” means. If the requirement is “a fuse that nothing can stop”, SD is right: the delay continues to run even after the step deactivates, and the resulting flag persists. If the requirement is instead “the purge output stays on for exactly 30 s and then stops itself, independently of the sequence”, use SL (stored, time limited). In both cases note the hazard: an action that outlives its step is an action the chart no longer models, so it needs an explicit reset on the abort and power-up paths, and the HMI should show that a purge is in progress even though no step claims it.
P4. An SFC opens with a simultaneous divergence into branches X and Y. Branch X ends in a step whose exit condition is TankEmpty; branch Y ends in a step whose exit condition is RobotHome. The two branches are closed by a selective convergence. Both branch conditions can be true at once, and branch Y contains an S action on the gripper. List every defect and its consequence.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Defect 1 — mismatched divergence and convergence (the fatal one). A simultaneous (AND) divergence creates two tokens; a selective (OR) convergence consumes only one. Whichever branch's condition is satisfied first passes its token through and the sequence continues, leaving the other token orphaned in its final step forever. Consequences: the chart is no longer safe, because the next cycle's AND-divergence will try to create a second token in an already-marked place; the orphaned branch's outputs may remain asserted (its step is still active); and the machine will typically run correctly for one or more cycles and then hang, with the failure appearing unrelated to the change that caused it. Defect 2 — both conditions can be true simultaneously at a selective convergence. By evolution rule 4 all clearable transitions clear at once, so both branch transitions fire in the same instant, both tokens are consumed and two tokens are placed in the following step. The downstream sequence then runs twice, or produces an illegal marking. This is the intermittent, unreproducible variant of the same structural error. Defect 3 — the S action on the gripper has no guaranteed R. If branch Y's token is the orphaned one, the reset action — wherever it is — is never reached, so the gripper stays commanded closed while the chart believes nothing is running. On an abort, the same. Defect 4 (implied) — no timeout on either branch. If TankEmpty never arrives (blocked outlet, failed level switch), branch X waits indefinitely and, with a correct AND convergence, would block the whole machine with no alarm. The corrected structure. (i) Close the simultaneous divergence with a simultaneous convergence, so the transition requires both final steps to be active and consumes both tokens. (ii) Give every step in both branches its own timeout with a destination, so a stalled branch faults by name rather than blocking silently. (iii) Wrap the concurrent region in an enclosing step whose deactivation kills both branches at once, providing a clean abort and hold path. (iv) Replace the gripper's S with an N action on the enclosing step, or place matching R actions on the normal, fault and abort exits. (v) If the branches genuinely need not complete together, do not use concurrency at all — model them as independent charts with a handshake, so one branch's stoppage does not become the whole machine's stoppage.
P5. A cell has three machines and one robot that loads and unloads all of them. Model it as a Petri net: place rob holds one token; each machine i has places waitₖ, loadₖ (robot held), procₖ, unloadₖ (robot held). Write the P-invariant for the robot, state what it proves, and say whether the net can deadlock.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The robot P-invariant. Take y with a 1 on rob and a 1 on every place in which the robot is held — that is load₁, load₂, load₃, unload₁, unload₂, unload₃. Every transition either takes the token from rob and puts it into one of those six places (sum unchanged), or takes it out of one of them and returns it to rob (sum unchanged), so yᵀC = 0. Since M₀ puts exactly one token on rob, we have
M(rob) + Σₖ M(loadₖ) + Σₖ M(unloadₖ) = 1 for every reachable marking.
What it proves: the robot is allocated to at most one machine at any time (mutual exclusion, proved rather than tested), the six holding places and rob are each 1-bounded so they map to single BOOLs, and — usefully for capacity planning — the robot's utilisation is bounded by the fraction of time the token is not on rob. There is a second family of invariants, one per machine: M(waitₖ) + M(loadₖ) + M(procₖ) + M(unloadₖ) = 1, proving each machine is in exactly one condition. Can it deadlock? As described, no — and the invariants tell you why. Each machine needs only one resource (the robot) at a time, and it always releases the robot before entering procₖ. There is no hold-and-wait: no process ever holds the robot while waiting for something else. Since one of Coffman's four conditions is structurally absent, deadlock is impossible; the worst outcome is contention (machines queue for the robot) and possibly starvation if the allocation policy is a fixed priority rather than FIFO. What would break it. Add a second shared resource — a shared fixture, a shared gauge, a single output conveyor slot, or a buffer of finite size — that a machine must acquire while still holding the robot, and hold-and-wait reappears immediately; if two machines can acquire the two resources in opposite orders you have the circular wait of WE3. The same happens if the robot must place a part into a full output buffer before it can release: the robot is then held pending a downstream event, which couples the resource to the rest of the system. The practical rule this illustrates: a single shared resource, always acquired alone and always released before waiting on anything else, cannot deadlock — so keep resource holds short, and never wait for a process event while holding a resource.
P6. A part passing a proximity sensor on a conveyor produces a 30 ms pulse. The sequence runs in a 20 ms cyclic task. (a) Can the logic reliably detect the part? Show the reasoning. (b) What task period would be required? (c) Give three alternatives that do not require changing the task period.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) No — and not merely “usually”. Inputs are sampled once per task period, at the start of the scan. A 30 ms pulse arriving at an arbitrary phase relative to a 20 ms sampling clock is guaranteed to be caught only if the pulse is at least two sample periods long (40 ms); at 30 ms it will normally be sampled once, but there is a window — when the pulse begins just after one sample and ends just before the next-but-one — in which it can fall entirely between samples and be invisible. Worse than losing it occasionally: the loss is phase-dependent, so it correlates with conveyor speed and can appear as “one part in two hundred is not counted”, which is exactly the kind of defect that is blamed on the sensor for months. Formally this is the Nyquist condition applied to a discrete event: to guarantee detection, sample at least twice within the shortest event, i.e. Ttask ≤ pulse/2. (b) Ttask ≤ 15 ms, and in practice 10 ms to leave margin for jitter, input filter delay and task overrun. Remember the input module's own filter (often 1–3 ms, sometimes configurable to tens of milliseconds) sits in series with this and must be checked — a 10 ms hardware filter on a 30 ms pulse leaves 20 ms, which changes the arithmetic. (c) Three alternatives that leave the task period alone. (i) Latch the event in hardware: use an input module with a pulse-catch / event-latch function, which sets a bit on the edge and holds it until the program reads and clears it. The pulse can then be arbitrarily short. This is the cleanest answer and costs nothing but configuration on most platforms. (ii) Use a high-speed counter or interrupt input. The event is handled by dedicated hardware or by an event task with microsecond latency, independent of the cyclic task. Mandatory if you also need position or count accuracy. (iii) Stretch the pulse at source: many proximity and photoelectric sensors have a configurable one-shot / pulse-stretch output (typically 20–100 ms), or an external timing relay can do it. Alternatively reposition or re-specify the sensor so the target dwell is longer — a larger target, a slower point on the mechanism, or a retro-reflective arrangement with a wider beam. What is not an alternative: adding software filtering, retry logic or a “bigger flag”. If the information never entered the process image, no code can recover it. This is the single most useful thing to know about scan-based systems: you cannot compute your way out of an unsampled event.
P7. In a ladder program, rung 5 computes a one-shot: Pulse := Trigger AND NOT TriggerLast. Rung 40 contains TriggerLast := Trigger. A colleague “tidies up” by moving the TriggerLast assignment to rung 4. What happens, and what is the general principle?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The one-shot stops working entirely — Pulse is never true again. With the assignment on rung 40 (below the user), rung 5 reads TriggerLast as written on the previous scan, which is the whole point: on the scan in which Trigger first goes true, TriggerLast is still false, so Pulse is true for exactly one scan; rung 40 then updates the history for next time. Moving the assignment to rung 4 makes it execute before rung 5, so by the time rung 5 evaluates, TriggerLast already equals Trigger in the same scan, and Trigger AND NOT TriggerLast is always false. The failure is silent: the program compiles, monitors normally, and simply never pulses. Whatever the pulse did — increment a counter, latch a step, send a message — stops happening, and the symptom appears in a completely different part of the machine. The general principle: in a scan-based language, the position of a rung is part of its semantics. A rung reads the current scan's values from everything above it and the previous scan's values from everything below it, so “tidying”, sorting alphabetically, or inserting a new rung in the middle of a block can change behaviour without changing a single instruction. The engineering responses. (1) Do not hand-roll edge detection. Use the standard R_TRIG / F_TRIG function blocks (or the platform's ONS instruction), which keep their history inside their own instance and are therefore immune to rung order. This removes the entire class of defect. (2) If you must hand-roll it, put all history updates in one clearly labelled block at the very end of the program, with a comment saying that nothing may be inserted after it, and treat that block as structurally significant. (3) Prefer ST for anything order-sensitive, where the sequence of statements is explicit and reviewable in a diff rather than implied by graphical position. (4) In review, treat any change to rung order as a functional change requiring the same testing as a logic change — because it is one.
P8. A plant has two identical 5 m³ reactors, each with a jacket, an agitator and three feed lines; a shared weigh hopper; and a shared CIP skid. A product requires: charge solvent, charge powder by weight, heat and hold, cool, discharge, clean. Map this onto the ISA-88 physical and procedural models, and decide what should be a phase, an operation and a unit procedure.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Physical model. Process cell = the whole reaction suite (two reactors, weigh hopper, CIP skid, common services). Units = Reactor 1 and Reactor 2 — each processes one batch at a time, which is the defining test. The weigh hopper is the interesting case: it is a shared resource used by both units, so it is best modelled as a separate unit or as an equipment module owned by the process cell and allocated, not as part of either reactor; the same applies to the CIP skid. Modelling a shared item as part of one unit is the commonest S88 modelling error and it makes arbitration impossible to express. Equipment modules within each reactor: the jacket temperature-control module (valves, pump, TIC), the agitator module (VFD, speed control, interlocks), each feed line module (block valve, control valve, flow measurement), the discharge module, the vent/pressure module. Control modules: individual valves, motors, transmitters and loops. Procedural model. Procedure = “Make product X” — the whole recipe. Unit procedure = the work done in one unit; here there is essentially one, “Reactor processing”, which runs on whichever reactor is allocated. (If the product required a transfer to a second vessel for finishing, that would be a second unit procedure — the boundary between unit procedures is where the batch physically moves between units.) Operations = groups of phases that accomplish a process-meaningful stage and would be a sensible place to hold: Charge, React, Cool & Discharge, Clean. Phases = the smallest independently commandable actions with their own parameters: ChargeSolvent(line, quantity, tolerance), ChargePowder(material, weight, tolerance), Agitate(speed, duration), Heat(setpoint, ramp, soak), Hold(duration, tolerance), Cool(setpoint, rate), Discharge(destination, endpoint), CIP(programme). The judgement calls, and how to make them. (i) Is “heat and hold” one phase or two? Two, if you ever want to hold at a different temperature, report the soak separately, or hold the batch during the soak; one, only if they are inseparable in every product. Prefer smaller phases — combining is easy later, splitting a phase that is already validated is not. (ii) ChargePowder must acquire the weigh hopper, and the acquisition belongs to the phase, released on every exit path including ABORT. (iii) CIP is a phase (or an operation) that acquires the CIP skid, and if it also needs a transfer pump, it must acquire the full set atomically, in the global order (WE3, WE4). (iv) Formula parameters live in the recipe, not the code: quantities, temperatures, times and tolerances are all recipe data, so a new product grade is a new master recipe rather than a software change — which is the entire point of the standard. (v) Every phase must implement HOLDING, RESTARTING and ABORTING, and for this plant the interesting ones are ChargePowder (stop dosing, record the partial weight so the restart can charge only the remainder) and Heat (hold the jacket at a safe temperature and keep agitating, so the batch does not stratify or exotherm).
P9. A cycle contains six waiting steps. Each has a measured mean duration of 3.00 s with a standard deviation of 0.45 s, and the timeout on each is 4.00 s. The machine runs 500 cycles per shift, 20 shifts per month. (a) How many nuisance stops per shift and per month? (b) What timeout gives fewer than one nuisance stop per month? (c) Comment on the design.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) Per step, z = (4.00 − 3.00)/0.45 = 2.222, so the exceedance probability is 1 − Φ(2.222) = 0.01313. With six independent waiting steps, the probability that at least one step times out in a given cycle is 1 − (1 − 0.01313)6 = 0.0763 — about one cycle in thirteen. At 500 cycles per shift that is 38.1 stops per shift, and over 20 shifts 763 stops per month. If each stop costs even five minutes of operator attention and restart, that is over sixty hours a month — the machine is effectively unusable, and the operators will already have found a way to defeat it. (b) To keep the monthly total below one, the per-step probability must satisfy p < 1/(500 × 20 × 6) = 1.67 × 10−5, which needs z = 4.15, giving T = 3.00 + 4.15(0.45) = 4.87 s — call it 5.0 s (z = 4.44, about 0.3 stops per month). Sanity-check the intermediate values: 4.50 s gives z = 3.33 and still 26 stops a month; the curve is brutally steep, and the difference between an unusable machine and a reliable one is one second of timeout. (c) Comment. Three things. First, the original 4.00 s was almost certainly chosen as “a bit more than 3” by eye; the arithmetic shows that intuition is worthless here because the tail probability changes by four orders of magnitude across a range that feels similar. Second, the number of steps matters as much as the per-step probability: six steps multiply the cycle failure rate by six, so a long sequence needs proportionally tighter per-step probabilities — design against the cycle nuisance rate, not the step one. Third, and most important, a 15 % coefficient of variation on a 3 s step is large, and the right question is why. If the spread comes from a genuine physical variable (product weight, air pressure, temperature) then a fixed timeout is a poor model and a rate-based or condition-based check would be better; if it comes from a marginal sensor or a sticking mechanism, then the correct fix is mechanical and the timeout arithmetic is treating a symptom. Reducing σ is worth far more than increasing T: halving the standard deviation to 0.225 s would make the original 4.00 s timeout a 4.4σ bound, solving the problem with no loss of detection speed at all.
P10 — Synthesis. Design the sequential control for a two-station assembly cell: station A presses a bearing into a housing (12 s), station B laser-marks it (8 s), and one six-axis robot loads and unloads both stations and the infeed and outfeed conveyors. Target 200 parts/hour. The robot's cycle time between stations is 4 s. Produce: the state model, the resource strategy, the timeout policy, the mode and recovery design, the diagnostics, and the verification plan. Identify the capacity constraint.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
1. Capacity first — before any code. 200 parts/hour is one part every 18.0 s of takt. Station A occupies 12 s and station B 8 s per part, both within takt individually and in parallel. The robot, however, must perform four transfers per part (infeed→A, A→B, B→outfeed, plus its return/positioning moves) at about 4 s each ≈ 16 s per part, which is 89 % of takt. The robot is the constraint, and at 89 % utilisation with any variability the cell will not hold 200/h reliably — queueing theory alone predicts growing waits. State this before designing anything: the sequencing cannot fix a capacity problem, and a beautifully arbitrated cell that misses takt is a failed project. Options: reduce the number of robot moves (combine A→B and infeed pick with a dual gripper — the standard answer, cutting robot time to roughly 8–10 s per part), speed up the moves, or add a second robot. Assume a dual gripper is fitted, giving about 10 s of robot time per part (56 % utilisation), and proceed. 2. State model. Four concurrent state machines, one per asset, each an instance of the same pattern — not one monolithic sequence. Station A: IDLE → LOADED → CLAMPING → PRESSING → MEASURING (force/displacement check) → UNCLAMPING → READY_TO_UNLOAD → IDLE, plus FAULT and, from every state, HELD. Station B: IDLE → LOADED → CLAMPING → MARKING → VERIFYING (vision check of the mark) → READY_TO_UNLOAD → IDLE, plus FAULT/HELD. Robot: IDLE → (JOB_SELECT) → MOVING_TO_PICK → PICKING → MOVING_TO_PLACE → PLACING → RETRACTING → IDLE, plus FAULT/HELD. Cell coordinator: a supervisory machine owning mode, part tracking and job dispatch. Each is a function block instance with an enumerated step, one writer, an ELSE branch to FAULT, and outputs assigned once as a function of state. 3. Resource strategy. The shared resources are the robot, and the space inside each station (the robot and the press must never occupy it together). Model each station as an exclusive resource with a single global acquisition order: Robot → StationSpace, always, acquired atomically by the robot's job. A job is dispatched only when the coordinator can grant the complete set {robot, source location, destination location} in one indivisible allocation — this kills hold-and-wait, and the fixed order kills circular wait, so deadlock is structurally impossible (WE3). Every station's press/laser is interlocked on RobotClear derived from a real position signal, not from the robot's own opinion, and the safety-rated version of that interlock lives in the safety controller (A35/A52), not here. Dispatch policy: FIFO by part age with a simple rule that unloading takes priority over loading — otherwise a full station blocks the cell. 4. Timeout policy. Every state has a timeout with a destination and, where physics allows, a minimum time. Derive each from measured data after commissioning (WE5); before that, use nominal × 1.5 with a warning at nominal × 1.25, and log every step's elapsed time from day one so the real values can be derived in the first fortnight. Press step gets both a maximum (stuck ram) and a minimum (a press stroke completing in 2 s when it should take 12 s means the housing was missing or the force never built — that is a quality escape, not a fast cycle). The measuring step confirms by force–displacement signature, not by a timer. 5. Mode and recovery. Modes: Auto, Semi-auto (one part), Manual/jog (hold-to-run, safety interlocks fully active), Setup (key switch, reduced speed, enabling device), Maintenance (isolated). Mode changes are requests, honoured only at a safe point — between parts, never mid-transfer. Recovery: a homing sequence that reads every position sensor, classifies the actual state, refuses to move if the state is inconsistent (both limit switches on), and otherwise retracts the robot to a safe pose first, then homes each station — in an order that cannot cause a collision. Part tracking must survive: on restart the coordinator must know what is in each station, so part IDs and station contents are retained and, on power-up, verified against presence sensors rather than assumed. Provide a defined degraded mode: if station B faults, the cell may continue pressing and buffering, or must stop — that is a business decision to be written into the specification, not left to the programmer. 6. Diagnostics. Per asset: step number and name, elapsed time, and a “waiting for” string naming the exact condition. Cell level: resource ownership view (who holds the robot, for how long, who is queued), a 100-entry step-transition trace with timestamps and exit reasons, a rejected-event log, first-out capture on trips, and step-time trending for condition monitoring — a press step creeping from 12.0 to 13.5 s over months is a hydraulic or seal problem found before it stops the line (A42). Alarm on any resource wait exceeding a threshold, naming the holder. 7. Verification plan. (i) Desk simulation from week one: every device function block has a Simulate input, so the four state machines and the arbitration run on a soft PLC before hardware exists — this is where the concurrency defects are found. (ii) A Petri net model of the robot and the two station spaces, checked for boundedness, liveness and deadlock-freedom, with the invariants written down. (iii) Transition-coverage test list — one test per arrow, executed and signed. (iv) Every timeout demonstrated by physically preventing its confirmation. (v) Abort and hold from every state on every asset, verifying that all stored outputs clear, all resources release, and homing succeeds afterwards. (vi) Illegal-event testing: every button in every state. (vii) A throughput test at rate, for a full shift, with the queue-wait metric recorded — because the capacity analysis in step 1 is a prediction, and the only honest way to close it is to measure.

Derivations & Traps

Four results to be able to reproduce from memory, and the seven mistakes that cost marks and machines.

DERIVATION 1 · EVERY MEALY MACHINE HAS AN EQUIVALENT MOORE MACHINE

The claim is that the two formalisms have equal expressive power, so choosing Moore for industrial work costs nothing but states. The proof is constructive, which is what makes it useful: it tells you exactly how to convert.

Given a Mealy machine Mₖ = (S, Σ, Λ, δ, ωₖ, s₀) with ωₖ : S × Σ → Λ (output depends on state AND input) Construction. Build a Moore machine Mₘ whose states are PAIRS (state, output-on-arrival): Sₘ = S × Λ at most |S|·|Λ| states δₘ( (s, y), a ) = ( δ(s,a), ωₖ(s,a) ) carry the output forward ωₘ( (s, y) ) = y output is now a pure state property initial state = ( s₀, y₀ ) with y₀ an arbitrary initial output Proof of equivalence. Consider any input string a₁a₂...aₙ. In Mₖ the k-th output is ωₖ(sₖ₋₁, aₖ). In Mₘ after reading aₖ the machine is in state ( sₖ, ωₖ(sₖ₋₁,aₖ) ), whose output is by definition ωₖ(sₖ₋₁,aₖ) — the same symbol. The two output sequences are identical apart from the initial output y₀, which Mₘ emits before any input is read. ∎ Cost and consequence. • State count grows by at most a factor |Λ| — in practice far less, because only the (state, output) pairs actually reachable are needed. • The Moore machine is one step "slower": its output appears in the state AFTER the input, not simultaneously with it. • In a PLC that lag is one scan, against field devices that respond in hundreds of milliseconds — so the cost is nil and the diagnostic benefit (output is a pure function of a single visible state variable) is large. That is the whole industrial argument for Moore.
DERIVATION 2 · THE P-INVARIANT THEOREM

This is the result that turns Petri nets from a drawing into a proof technique. Everything about mutual exclusion and boundedness on Pages 6 and 13 rests on it.

Setup. For a Petri net with incidence matrix C = Post − Pre, firing transition t changes the marking by the t-th column of C: M′ = M + C · eₜ where eₜ is the unit vector for t After a firing SEQUENCE with firing-count vector σ (how many times each transition fired), the changes simply add: M′ = M₀ + C · σ the state equation Theorem. If a vector y ≥ 0 satisfies yᵀ C = 0, then for every marking M reachable from M₀: yᵀ M = yᵀ M₀ Proof. yᵀ M = yᵀ (M₀ + Cσ) = yᵀM₀ + (yᵀC)σ = yᵀM₀ + 0·σ = yᵀM₀ ∎ The quantity yᵀM is therefore a conserved weighted token count — an invariant of the system, true for all time, independent of any schedule, any timing and any future extension of the rest of the net. Corollary 1 — boundedness. If yᴵ > 0 for some place p, then yᴵ M(p) ≤ yᵀM = yᵀM₀ ⇒ M(p) ≤ yᵀM₀ / yᴵ so p is bounded by a number you can compute WITHOUT enumerating any reachable markings. With yᵀM₀ = 1 and unit weights, M(p) ≤ 1: the place is a plain BOOL. Corollary 2 — mutual exclusion. If y has 1s exactly on the places {resource free, A holds it, B holds it, ...} and yᵀM₀ = 1, then M(free) + M(A holds) + M(B holds) + ... = 1 always so at most one holder exists in every reachable marking — mutual exclusion is PROVED, not tested. The one caveat that matters. The state equation is a NECESSARY but not sufficient condition for reachability: a non-negative integer solution σ may exist for a marking that no firing ORDER can actually realise, because the equation ignores whether the intermediate markings were legal. So invariants prove things are IMPOSSIBLE (safety properties) — reliably; the state equation alone cannot prove a marking IS reachable. For that you need the reachability graph or a model checker.
DERIVATION 3 · NYQUIST FOR DISCRETE EVENTS

Why a scan-based controller needs two samples inside the shortest event, and why the response budget is two task periods rather than one. Both follow from the same worst-case phase argument.

Setup. A cyclic task samples all inputs at times tₖ = kT, k = 0,1,2,... A pulse of width W starts at an arbitrary time t₀, uniformly distributed relative to the sampling clock, and is present over [t₀, t₀+W). Detection condition. The pulse is seen if and only if at least one sample instant falls inside the interval, i.e. if there exists an integer k with t₀ ≤ kT < t₀+W. Case W ≥ T. The interval [t₀, t₀+W) has length ≥ T, and consecutive sample instants are exactly T apart, so at least one sample instant must lie inside it, whatever the phase. Detection is guaranteed. Case W < T. Write t₀ = mT + φ with phase φ ∈ [0,T). A sample falls in the interval only if φ = 0 or T − φ < W, i.e. φ > T − W. The measure of that set is W, so P(detected) = W / T and P(missed) = 1 − W/T. A 30 ms pulse on a 20 ms task: seen "always"? No — W > T here, so it IS guaranteed. But add the input filter delay Tᶜ in series and the effective width becomes W − Tᶜ; with a 15 ms filter, W_eff = 15 ms < T and P(missed) = 1 − 15/20 = 25 %. This is why the filter must be counted, and why the practical rule is stated with margin: T ≤ W / 2 (two samples inside the shortest event) which tolerates a filter, jitter and one late scan without loss. The two-period response budget. Worst case, an event occurs immediately AFTER an input read, so it waits up to T to be sampled. The logic then executes and the result is written to the outputs at the END of that scan, a further T later in the worst case. Hence T_logic(worst) = 2T, and per sequence step the cycle-time penalty is 2T — the figure used on Pages 7 and 14 and in WE2. For an n-step sequence the total scan penalty is 2nT, which is why a 40-step sequence on a 100 ms task loses 8 s per cycle.
DERIVATION 4 · WHY TIMEOUT CHOICE IS VIOLENTLY NON-LINEAR
Setup. A step's duration is approximately normal, mean μ, standard deviation σ. Write the timeout as a multiple of the mean, T = kμ, and the variability as a fraction, c = σ/μ (the coefficient of variation). Per-step nuisance probability. z = (T − μ)/σ = (kμ − μ)/(cμ) = (k − 1)/c ← note μ cancels: only the RATIO k and the variability c matter pₛₜₑₚ = 1 − Φ(z) Per-cycle probability for n independent waiting steps p₊ₖ₋ = 1 − (1 − pₛₜₑₚ)ⁿ ≈ n · pₛₜₑₚ for small p Stops per shift with N cycles per shift S = N · [ 1 − (1 − pₛₜₑₚ)ⁿ ] ≈ N · n · (1 − Φ((k−1)/c)) Sensitivity — the point of the derivation. dz/dk = 1/c a 0.1 change in k moves z by 0.1/c With c = 0.10, dz/dk = 10: increasing k from 1.3 to 1.5 moves z from 3.0 to 5.0, and the tail probability falls from 1.35×10⁻³ to 2.87×10⁻⁷ — a factor of 4700 for a 0.2 change in a number that "feels" almost the same. Three engineering consequences. 1. Intuition is worthless here. Compute, or measure. 2. n multiplies the rate. Long sequences need tighter per-step probabilities: design against the CYCLE rate, not the step rate. 3. Since z = (k−1)/c, halving c is exactly as effective as doubling (k−1) — and halving the variability costs no detection speed at all, whereas raising k delays every genuine fault detection by (Δk)·μ. Reduce the spread first.
SEVEN TRAPS
  • 1“The chart will pick one branch.” It will not. GRAFCET evolution rule 4 clears every transition that can be cleared, so a selective divergence with two simultaneously true receptivities takes both branches and creates a second token. Mutual exclusivity is the designer's job: write A and NOT A, or an explicit priority.
  • 2SD versus DS. SD = Stored and Delayed: the timer runs on even after the step deactivates, and the variable is set regardless. DS = Delayed and Stored: the step must remain active for the whole delay. Choosing the wrong one produces a latch that fires from a step that ended long ago.
  • 3Rung order is semantics, not layout. A rung reads this scan's values from above it and last scan's from below. Reordering, sorting or inserting rungs changes behaviour — it is a functional change and must be tested as one. This is the source of both the run-through bug and the broken one-shot.
  • 4Command is not confirmation. A step that exits because a command was issued, or because a timer expired, will happily proceed with a valve shut, a heater dead or a part missing. Exit on evidence from a physically different phenomenon, and add a minimum time as well as a maximum.
  • 5RETAIN on the step variable. A retained step number means the machine wakes after a power failure believing it is mid-cycle, while the physical world has moved. Default to NON_RETAIN plus a homing sequence that verifies the physical state rather than assuming it.
  • 6“There's a timeout” when there is only an alarm. A timeout must have a destination state. An alarm with no state change leaves the machine hung in the same step, still waiting, with an annunciator on — which is exactly the failure the timeout was supposed to prevent.
  • 7Average utilisation is not contention. A shared resource at 50 % utilisation can still cost a quarter of your output, because queueing is driven by the coincidence of demand (WE4). Always ask what happens when everyone wants it at once — and what keeps them from wanting it at once.

Two honourable mentions. Don't-cares in safety logic: marking an “impossible” input combination as X lets the minimiser assign it whatever is convenient — and the day it occurs, the output is whatever the tool chose. Force impossible combinations to the safe value explicitly. The S with no R: every stored action creates an obligation that must be discharged on every exit path, including abort, fault and power-up; if you cannot enumerate those paths, use N instead.
