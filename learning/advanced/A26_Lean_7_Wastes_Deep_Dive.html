What Lean Really Is

Not a toolbox of tricks — a philosophy about value and the time between cash-out and cash-in.

Definition

Lean is a management system that maximises customer value while minimising waste — delivering exactly what the customer wants, when they want it, with the fewest resources, the shortest lead time, and the least effort.

The term "Lean" was coined by John Krafcik (1988) and popularised by Womack & Jones in The Machine That Changed the World (1990), describing the production system Toyota had built over decades — the Toyota Production System (TPS), architected by Taiichi Ohno and Shigeo Shingo on foundations laid by Sakichi & Kiichiro Toyoda.

The deep idea: in any process, work is either value-adding (changes the product the way the customer wants and would pay for), necessary non-value-adding (waste you can't yet remove — e.g. a required inspection), or pure waste (muda). Lean is the discipline of shrinking the second and eliminating the third — forever.

The Lean House — TPS Visualised
The TPS "house": two pillars (JIT + Jidoka) on a foundation of stability, with people-driven Kaizen at the heart. Remove a pillar and the roof falls.

Why an engineer should care: a typical part spends 95–99.5% of its factory time waiting, moving, or queuing — only a sliver is actually being machined. That means the biggest lever on lead time, cost and cash is almost never "make the machine faster" — it's deleting the waiting. Lean is how you find and delete it systematically.

The 5 Lean Principles

Womack & Jones' loop — the operating system that everything else plugs into.

The Improvement Loop
  • 1
    Specify Value — define value from the customer's view, for a specific product. Value is what they'd knowingly pay for. Internal convenience is not value.
  • 2
    Map the Value Stream — identify every step (value-add, necessary, waste) from raw material to customer. Make the waste visible (see Page 5).
  • 3
    Create Flow — make the value-adding steps flow without stops, batches or backflows. Ideal target: single-piece flow.
  • 4
    Establish Pull — let the downstream customer pull work; nothing is made until the next step signals demand (Kanban). Kills overproduction.
  • 5
    Pursue Perfection — repeat forever. Each loop exposes the next layer of waste. Kaizen never ends.

These five form a closed loop, not a checklist: step 5 sends you back to step 1 with a sharper eye. Lean organisations run thousands of these micro-loops every year.

VA

Value-Add

Transforms the product the way the customer wants. Target: maximise its share of lead time.

NNVA

Necessary Non-Value

Required today but the customer wouldn't pay for it — safety checks, regulatory tests, setup. Target: minimise.

NVA

Pure Waste (Muda)

Adds nothing — waiting, moving, defects, overproduction. Target: eliminate.

95%

The Reality

In most factories, value-add is <5% of total lead time. The opportunity is enormous.

The 3 Evils — Muda, Mura, Muri

The 8 wastes are only one of three enemies. Toyota attacks all three "3M".

They feed each other. Mura (uneven demand) forces you to either build inventory (muda) or temporarily overload the line (muri) to catch up — and an overloaded machine breaks down, creating waiting and defects (muda again). Attacking only the 8 wastes while ignoring unevenness is treating symptoms. Heijunka (production levelling, Page 6) tackles Mura at the source.

🔴 D · Defects

Any output that doesn't meet spec the first time — scrap, rework, returns, warranty claims. Cost = material + labour already spent + inspection + rework + reputation. The later a defect is caught, the more it costs (the "1-10-100" rule).

Industrial example: A weld cell runs 3% rejects from torch-offset drift between recipes — Jidoka + Poka-Yoke catches it at source instead of at final QC.
Why Overproduction Is "The Worst"

Ohno called overproduction the worst waste because it manufactures the other seven: make too much and you need somewhere to store it (Inventory), forklifts to move it (Transport), people to count it (Motion), cash tied up waiting to sell (Waiting), and any defect stays hidden in the pile far longer (Defects). Fix overproduction with pull and most of the rest shrink automatically.

Distinguishing the Confusable Pair

🚚 Transport

Movement of materials/product that the customer doesn't pay for. Fix: cellular layout, point-of-use delivery.

🚶 Motion

Movement of people/machines — reaching, bending, walking to fetch tools. Fix: 5S, shadow boards, ergonomic stations.

Memory trick: Transport moves the part; Motion moves the person. Both are waste, but the countermeasure differs.

Value Stream Mapping (VSM)

Lean's core diagnostic — draw the whole door-to-door flow and expose where time hides.

Reading a VSM
A current-state VSM: process boxes carry C/T (cycle time) and C/O (changeover); triangles are inventory; the saw-tooth timeline splits non-value (top, red) from value-add (bottom, green).
The Killer Metric — Process Cycle Efficiency
PCE = Value-Add Time ÷ Total Lead Time Example above: PCE = 186 s ÷ (8.0 d × 86 400 s/d) = 186 ÷ 691 200 ≈ 0.027 % ← 99.97 % of the time is waste

A current-state map is only half the job. You then draw a future-state map — where would flow, pull and levelling go? — and the gap between the two becomes your Kaizen project list. World-class discrete manufacturing reaches PCE of ~25%; most plants sit below 5%.

Flow, Takt & Line Balance

Once you can see the waste, you engineer the flow — paced to the customer's drumbeat.

Takt Time — The Heartbeat of Demand
Takt = Available Work Time per shift ÷ Customer Demand per shift If a line works 27 000 s/shift (7.5 h) and the customer needs 450 units/shift: Takt = 27 000 ÷ 450 = 60 s/unit → A finished unit must leave the line every 60 s — no faster (overproduction), no slower (missed demand).

Takt ≠ cycle time. Takt is set by the customer; cycle time is what your process actually achieves. Every workstation's cycle time must sit just under takt. A station above takt is a bottleneck; far below takt means idle time (waiting). Line balancing redistributes tasks so all stations hug the takt line.

Continuous Flow

One piece moves to the next step immediately — no batch, no queue. The ideal state.

Heijunka 平準化

Production levelling — mix and sequence small batches to smooth volume & variety, killing Mura.

SMED

Single-Minute Exchange of Die — slash changeover time so small batches become economical (enables flow).

Pull & the Kanban System

Stop pushing forecasts into the line. Let consumption pull replenishment.

Push vs Pull
Push makes to forecast and piles WIP between steps. Pull makes only to replace what the next process consumed — the Kanban card is the "make one more" signal travelling upstream.
Sizing a Kanban Loop
Number of Kanban = (D × LT × (1 + SF)) ÷ Container Qty D = demand rate (parts/unit time) LT = replenishment lead time SF = safety factor (buffer for variability) Example: D=60/h, LT=2 h, SF=0.2, container=20 = (60 × 2 × 1.2) ÷ 20 = 144 ÷ 20 = 7.2 → 8 cards

Kanban can be physical cards, bins (two-bin system), electronic (e-Kanban), or marked floor squares. The rule is iron: no Kanban, no production. Want less inventory? Remove a card — that forces the loop to expose and fix the problem that "needed" the buffer.

Jidoka & Poka-Yoke — Built-in Quality
Jidoka (自働化)

"Autonomation" — automation with a human touch. A machine detects its own abnormality, stops, and signals (Andon), so it never produces a pile of bad parts unattended. One operator can then run several machines.

Poka-Yoke (ポカヨケ)

Mistake-proofing. A fixture that only accepts the part one way; a sensor that won't let the cycle start until all bolts are present. Prevent the error, or detect it instantly — don't rely on attention.

Together these implement the right pillar of the TPS house: never knowingly pass a defect to the next process. This is the engineer's highest-leverage contribution — designing quality in rather than inspecting it in.

Metrics, Culture & Pitfalls

Lean lives or dies on respect-for-people and the right scoreboard.

The Lean Scoreboard
PCE

Process Cycle Efficiency

Value-add ÷ lead time. The headline Lean metric.

LT

Lead Time

Door-to-door elapsed time. Shrinking it frees cash & exposes waste.

WIP

Work-In-Process

Little's Law: WIP = Throughput × Lead Time. Cut WIP → cut LT.

FPY

First-Pass Yield

Good units first time ÷ started. Rolled across steps = RTY.

OEE

Equipment Effect.

Avail × Perf × Quality — links to TPM (A23).

$/u

Inventory Turns

COGS ÷ avg inventory. More turns = leaner.

Little's Law: WIP = Throughput × Lead Time → halve WIP at constant throughput and lead time halves. This is the maths behind "drain the inventory to go faster".
Where Lean Programmes Fail
⚠ Tool-chasing

"Doing 5S" and "running Kaizen events" as box-ticking, with no link to value or strategy. Lean is a system, not a kit.

⚠ No respect-for-people

Using Lean to cut headcount erodes the trust that powers Kaizen. People won't expose problems if it costs them their job.

⚠ Local optimisation

Speeding up a non-bottleneck just builds more WIP (Goldratt). Improve the constraint, or the whole stream.

⚠ Copy-paste TPS

Cloning Toyota's tools without the thinking. The tools are answers to Toyota's problems; find your own.

⚠ Ignoring Mura/Muri

Hunting muda while demand swings wildly — you'll just oscillate between overload and idle.

⚠ No standard work first

You can't improve what isn't stable. Standardise, then Kaizen — improvement needs a baseline.

How Lean Connects to the Rest of Your Library

Lean is the umbrella; OEE (A22) measures the equipment losses it targets, TPM (A23) keeps machines reliable enough to flow, 5S (A24) is the visual foundation, RCA (A25) drives the Kaizen problem-solving, and Six Sigma (DMAIC) brings the statistics — together "Lean Six Sigma." Lean removes waste; Six Sigma removes variation.

Quick Check · Tap to reveal

Q1. A plant speeds up its fastest machine, which is not the bottleneck. What happens to lead time and WIP?

Tap to reveal ↓ Lead time barely moves; WIP rises. The non-bottleneck now floods the constraint with more inventory it can't process (local optimisation trap). Only improving the bottleneck — or removing waiting around it — shortens the stream.

Q2. Takt time falls from 60 s to 45 s. What changed and what must you do?

Tap to reveal ↓ Customer demand rose (same work time ÷ more units = shorter takt). Every station must now cycle under 45 s — rebalance tasks, add a station, or apply Kaizen/SMED. Any station above 45 s is now a bottleneck.

Q3. Why is removing a Kanban card a deliberate improvement tactic, not just a cut?

Tap to reveal ↓ Lowering the "water level" exposes the "rocks." Less buffer means a problem (a long changeover, an unreliable machine) that the inventory was hiding now stops the line — forcing you to actually fix the root cause. Inventory hides problems; pulling it out reveals them.

Q4. Using Little's Law, a line has throughput 120 units/h and WIP of 600 units. What's the lead time?

Tap to reveal ↓ LT = WIP ÷ Throughput = 600 ÷ 120 = 5 hours. Halve WIP to 300 → lead time drops to 2.5 h at the same throughput. This is exactly why pull/Kanban (which caps WIP) compresses lead time.

Q5. Which 3M does Heijunka primarily attack, and why does that also reduce muda?

Tap to reveal ↓ Mura (unevenness). Levelling volume and mix means you no longer build inventory for the peaks (muda: inventory/overproduction) or overload machines and people to catch up (muri). Smoothing demand at the source dries up the muda it was generating.
