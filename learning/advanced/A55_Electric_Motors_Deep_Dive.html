How a Motor Actually Makes Torque

Every electric motor is one idea in three costumes: put current in a magnetic field, get force — and the geometry decides which "family" you built.

An electric motor is an electromechanical energy converter: it turns electrical power (V·I) into mechanical power (T·ω) with the magnetic field as the invisible gear between them. Roughly 45% of the world's electricity is consumed by electric motors, so a single point of efficiency is a national quantity of energy. Master the field and you master the whole zoo.

Two Ways to See the Same Torque

① Force on a conductor (BIL)

A wire of length ℓ carrying current I in a field B feels F = B·I·ℓ. Wrap N such conductors on a rotor at radius r and the torque is T = N·B·I·ℓ·r. This is the "motor-effect" picture — brushed DC motors are its clearest embodiment.

② Two fields trying to align

A stator field and a rotor field pull into alignment like two bar magnets. Torque ∝ the product of the two field strengths × sin(δ), the angle between them. Synchronous and PM machines are this picture; the "torque angle" δ is the currency.

Both are the same Maxwell-stress physics. A third, subtler mechanism — reluctance torque — appears when the rotor is magnetically shaped so the flux path wants to pull it into the low-reluctance position (SynRM, IPM, stepper VR, and the salient-pole term in any machine).

The Motor Family Tree
The One Distinction That Organises Everything

Ask of any AC machine: does the rotor turn at the same speed as the stator's rotating field, or slower?

  • 1Synchronous — rotor locks to the field (n = ns). The rotor carries its own excitation (magnets, DC winding, or salient iron). No slip, no rotor-copper loss → the efficiency champions (PMSM, SynRM). Speed is rigidly set by frequency.
  • 2Asynchronous (induction) — rotor runs slower than the field by the slip s. The relative motion induces the rotor current (Faraday), which is why it needs no brushes or magnets — the world's most-used motor precisely because it is so rugged and cheap.
  • 3Commutated (DC / universal) — a mechanical (brush) or electronic (BLDC) switch keeps rotor current always positioned to make torque, so torque control is trivial and instantaneous. The price is brushes, or the inverter that replaces them.
Quick Check

Why does an induction motor produce zero torque at exactly synchronous speed — the moment it would seem to be "keeping up" best?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Torque in an induction motor depends on rotor current, and rotor current is induced only when the rotor conductors cut lines of the rotating flux — i.e. when there is relative motion (slip). At synchronous speed the rotor moves exactly with the field, the relative speed is zero, no EMF is induced, no rotor current flows, and F = BIℓ = 0. The machine must slip to make torque; that is why it is called asynchronous. Real motors settle at a small slip (typically 1–5%) where the induced torque exactly balances the load.

Electromechanical Fundamentals

Five equations run every motor on Earth. Learn them once here and the rest of this module is bookkeeping.

The Force / EMF Duality

The same B-field does two jobs at once, and they are reciprocal:

Motor effect   F = B·I·ℓ   (force on a current-carrying conductor, N)
Generator effect   e = B·ℓ·v   (motional EMF, the back-EMF, V)

Every motor is also a generator: as it spins it produces a back-EMF that opposes the supply. The back-EMF is what limits the current at speed — stall a motor and back-EMF collapses to zero, current soars to V/R (the locked-rotor current, 6–8× rated). This single fact explains inrush, starting protection, and why V/f control exists.

Torque, Power & the Energy Balance
T = k·Φ·Ia   ·   Eb = k·Φ·ω   ·   Pmech = T·ω = Eb·Ia

Multiply torque by speed and you get the mechanical power; multiply back-EMF by armature current and you get the same number — that identity Eb·Ia = T·ω is the electromechanical energy balance, the hinge of the whole subject. Note torque needs flux × current: weaken the field and you need more current for the same torque (the field-weakening trade at high speed).

The Rotating Magnetic Field — The Heart of AC Machines

Feed three windings, spaced 120° in space, with three currents 120° apart in time, and their combined MMF is a field of constant magnitude that rotates at the synchronous speed:

This is Tesla's and Ferraris' 1880s insight and it is why the world runs on three-phase: no moving parts create the rotation — the field spins electromagnetically, the rotor merely chases it. (See A30 for the 120° phasor mathematics.)

Where Reluctance Torque Comes From

If the rotor's magnetic geometry is salient (d-axis reluctance ≠ q-axis reluctance), the flux exerts a pull that drags the rotor toward the minimum-reluctance alignment. The general torque of a doubly-fed machine has two terms:

T = k·Φf·Iq  +  ½·(Ld − Lq)·Id·Iq   (excitation torque + reluctance torque)

The first term needs a magnet or field winding; the second needs only shaped iron. IPM (interior-magnet) machines deliberately use both, which is why they dominate EV traction (see A38). A pure SynRM uses only the second term — no magnets, no rotor copper, so cheap and efficient but lower power factor.

Quick Check

A 4-pole, 50 Hz motor spins at 1470 rpm on load. What is the synchronous speed, the slip, and the frequency of the rotor currents?

Tap to reveal ↓A: ns = 120·50/4 = 1500 rpm. Slip s = (1500 − 1470)/1500 = 30/1500 = 0.02 (2%). Rotor-current frequency fr = s·f = 0.02·50 = 1 Hz. The rotor sees the flux sweeping past it at only 30 rpm relative, so its induced currents are very low frequency — which is why rotor iron loss is negligible at running speed and why the rotor can be a solid cast-aluminium cage.

DC Motors — The Teaching Machine

Obsolete in new installs, indispensable in your head: the DC motor is where torque control is transparent, and every VFD is an emulator of it.

Construction & the Job of the Commutator

A DC motor has a stationary field (poles: wound or permanent magnet) and a rotating armature carrying the working current. The genius is the commutator: a segmented copper ring with carbon brushes that mechanically reverses the armature current every half-turn, so the force on each conductor always pushes the same rotational way. The commutator is a mechanical inverter — and its brushes are the wear item that killed the DC motor for most modern drives.

The Governing Equations
V = Eb + IaRa   →   Eb = V − IaRa = k·Φ·ω
ω = (V − IaRa) / (k·Φ)   ·   T = k·Φ·Ia

Read the speed equation like a recipe: raise the voltage V → speed rises (armature-voltage control, the clean method, constant-torque region). Weaken the flux Φ → speed rises but max torque falls (field weakening, constant-power region above base speed). This is exactly what a VFD does to an induction motor by V/f below base and constant-V above — the DC motor just makes it visible.

The DC Motor Family & Their Torque–Speed Signatures

Safety trap: a series DC motor must never be belt-coupled or run unloaded — with T ∝ I², removing load lets speed climb until the armature bursts. Always positive-couple.

Armature Reaction & Commutation

The armature current makes its own cross-field that distorts and weakens the main flux (armature reaction), shifting the magnetic neutral and causing sparking at the brushes. Cures: interpoles (commutating poles carrying armature current to cancel the reactance voltage in the commutating coil) and, on large machines, a compensating winding in the pole faces. Poor commutation shows as ring-fire and rapid brush wear — the DC machine's chronic maintenance headache.

Quick Check

A 240 V DC shunt motor draws an armature current of 40 A with Ra = 0.25 Ω at 1200 rpm. If the load torque suddenly halves (flux constant), what happens to the steady-state speed, and why does it barely move?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Eb = 240 − 40·0.25 = 230 V. Halving load halves Ia to 20 A (since T = kΦIa). New Eb = 240 − 20·0.25 = 235 V. Since ω ∝ Eb (flux constant), new speed = 1200·(235/230) = 1226 rpm, a rise of just 2.2%. The speed barely changes because the IaRa drop (10 V) is tiny next to the 230 V back-EMF — the shunt motor is inherently a "stiff", near-constant-speed source. That small stiffness is exactly the shunt curve on the chart above.

The Three-Phase Induction Motor

The 90-plus-percent motor: no brushes, no magnets, no rotor connections — torque conjured out of pure induction. This is the one you will meet most.

Construction — Two Cylinders and an Air-Gap

Stator

Laminated silicon-steel core, slotted, carrying a 3-phase distributed winding. Energised, it produces the rotating field ns = 120f/P. Identical in every 3-phase machine.

Rotor

Cage: aluminium/copper bars shorted by end-rings — cast in one piece, unbreakable, no external connection. Wound: a 3-phase winding brought out via slip-rings to add external resistance.

The air-gap is deliberately tiny (0.3–2 mm) because it is the dominant reluctance in the magnetic circuit — a wider gap means more magnetising current and worse power factor. Concentricity of that gap is why bearing health (see A19) is a motor-reliability issue.

The Slip Story — Numbers That Define Operation
s = (ns − nr) / ns  ·  nr = ns(1 − s)  ·  fr = s·f  ·  E2s = s·E2
  • 1s = 1 at standstill (start): rotor frozen, full field sweeps past it — rotor EMF and frequency are maximal, so is current (locked-rotor, 6–8× rated).
  • 2s ≈ 0.01–0.05 at full load: only a whisker of slip, so fr ≈ 0.5–2.5 Hz. Full-load speed for a 4-pole 50 Hz motor is ~1440–1470 rpm, never 1500.
  • 3s = 0 is synchronous — reachable only if driven (then it generates). s > 1 is plugging (reverse braking); s < 0 is induction-generator territory (wind turbines, overhauling loads).
Cage-Rotor & the NEMA / IEC Design Classes

The rotor bar shape tunes the whole torque–speed curve, because at start (fr = 50 Hz) skin effect pushes current to the bar tops, raising effective resistance for high starting torque, then at run (fr ≈ 1 Hz) the current fills the bar for low running slip. Deep-bar and double-cage rotors exploit this.

On a VFD this all matters less — the drive can command any torque up to the current limit — but for DOL/star-delta starting the design letter decides whether the motor accelerates the load without cooking. (See A51.)

Quick Check

A wound-rotor motor is started with external rotor resistance and produces its maximum torque at standstill. As it accelerates, the operator shorts out the resistance in steps. Why does that give a smooth, high-torque, low-current start?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Maximum torque always occurs at the slip where s = R2/(X2 at that point) — the "breakdown slip" moves with rotor resistance. Adding external rotor resistance shifts the breakdown point to s = 1 (standstill), so you get peak torque exactly when you need it to break the load away, while the added resistance simultaneously limits the starting current. As the motor speeds up, cutting resistance in steps keeps operation near the torque peak throughout the run-up, then the fully-shorted rotor gives efficient low-slip running. This is the classic slip-ring crane/hoist start — high torque and modest current, the two things DOL cannot deliver together.

Induction Motor — The Equivalent Circuit

A transformer that lets its secondary spin. One per-phase circuit predicts current, torque, power factor and efficiency at every speed.

Why It Looks Like a Transformer

The stator is a primary, the rotor a short-circuited secondary coupled across the air-gap. The one twist: the rotor's effective secondary resistance is R2′/s, not R2′ — the slip appears in the denominator, and that single term carries all the mechanical power.

Power Flow — Follow the Watts
P_in → (stator Cu & iron loss) → Pag (air-gap) → (rotor Cu loss) → Pmech → (friction/windage) → Pshaft

The air-gap power splits in a fixed ratio set only by slip — this is the most examined fact in the subject:

Pag : Pcu2 : Pmech = 1 : s : (1−s)   ⇒   Pcu2 = s·Pag,   Pmech = (1−s)·Pag

Consequence: rotor copper loss is directly proportional to slip. A motor forced to run at 10% slip wastes 10% of its air-gap power as rotor heat — which is exactly why rotor-resistance speed control and high-slip operation cook rotors, and why VFDs (which change frequency, keeping slip tiny) win on efficiency.

The Torque Equation (Thevenin Form)
T = (3 / ωs) · Vth² · (R₂′/s) / [ (Rth + R₂′/s)² + (Xth + X₂′)² ]

Two limiting cases fall straight out:

Breakdown (max) torque

Occurs at smaxT = R₂′ / √(Rth² + (Xth+X₂′)²). The value of Tmax is independent of R₂′ — but the slip at which it occurs is proportional to R₂′. That is the whole wound-rotor trick.

Voltage sensitivity

T ∝ V² at every slip. A 10% voltage sag drops available torque by 19% — a classic cause of stalling on a weak feeder or during a large DOL start elsewhere on the bus.

Quick Check

A 4-pole 50 Hz motor delivers 15 kW of air-gap power at 3% slip. Estimate the rotor copper loss and the mechanical power developed.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Using Pcu2 = s·Pag = 0.03 × 15 000 = 450 W. Mechanical power Pmech = (1−s)·Pag = 0.97 × 15 000 = 14.55 kW. Note the shaft power is slightly less again after friction & windage. The elegance: you needed only the slip and the air-gap power — the 1 : s : (1−s) rule does the rest, no circuit solving required.

Torque–Speed Curve & the Four Quadrants

One curve tells you whether the motor will start the load, how it settles, and where it will stall. Then learn to run it backwards and as a brake.

Anatomy of the Curve

The motor settles where its curve crosses the load curve on the stable (right) side. If the load ever exceeds breakdown torque — a jam, a voltage sag (T∝V²), an over-tightened belt — the operating point slides left, torque falls as it slows, and the motor stalls at locked-rotor current until protection trips.

Regenerative braking (Q II/IV) is only useful if something absorbs the returned energy: a VFD needs a braking resistor or active front end (see A08), otherwise the DC bus over-volts and trips. Plugging dumps everything as rotor heat and is reserved for small or rare stops.

Quick Check

A fan motor runs happily at 1470 rpm. Someone stiffens the belt so the load torque at every speed rises 40%, pushing the new load line above the motor's breakdown torque. Predict the outcome.

Tap to reveal ↓A: If the raised load line no longer intersects the motor curve on the stable slope (or only above breakdown), there is no stable operating point. On start-up the motor may not even accelerate past the pull-up torque; if it was running, the extra load slows it, but past breakdown the developed torque falls as it slows further — a runaway deceleration into stall. It sits at near-locked-rotor current (6–8× rated), heating fast, until the overload relay (thermal class 10/20, see A51) trips. Fixes: reduce the load, raise the voltage, or select a higher-breakdown (Design C) or larger motor.

Why They Win on Efficiency

In an induction motor, rotor current — and therefore rotor copper loss s·Pag — is unavoidable; it is the price of induction. A synchronous rotor carries its excitation from magnets or a DC winding, so at steady state there is essentially no rotor copper loss. Remove that whole loss bucket and efficiency climbs a class or two — which is precisely why IE5 (Ultra-Premium) is only reachable with PMSM or SynRM technology, per IEC 60034-30-1:2025.

Torque, Torque-Angle & the Need for a Drive
T = (3P/4)·[ λm·iq + (Ld − Lq)·id·iq ]   (magnet torque + reluctance torque)

A PMSM cannot start across the line — connect it to 50 Hz and the rotor, at rest, cannot instantly lock to a field already spinning at 1500 rpm; it just vibrates. It needs an inverter that ramps frequency from zero and, via field-oriented control, keeps the stator current vector 90° (electrical) ahead of the rotor flux for maximum torque-per-amp. The rotor position must be known (encoder, resolver, or sensorless back-EMF estimation) — this is the servo drive of A09.

Advantages

Highest efficiency & power density; full torque at zero speed; precise position control; compact.

Trade-offs

Needs an inverter always; magnet cost & rare-earth supply risk; demagnetisation if over-temp; safety — a spinning PMSM is a live generator.

Quick Check

A technician disconnects a PMSM servo from its drive but the axis is still coasting. He measures voltage at the motor terminals and gets a shock. Explain — and give the safe-handling rule.

Tap to reveal ↓A: A permanent-magnet machine is a permanent generator: whenever the rotor spins, the magnets induce a back-EMF at the terminals regardless of whether the drive is connected — voltage rises with speed and can reach hundreds of volts on a large or fast axis. So a "disconnected" but coasting or gravity-driven PMSM is live. Safe rule: bring the axis to a true mechanical stop and lock/brake it before touching terminals; treat a PM motor's terminals as live any time the shaft can turn. This is also why EV and large-servo systems use an active short-circuit or crowbar to safely brake a runaway PMSM, and why over-speed can over-volt (and destroy) an inverter's DC bus.

Stepper & Special Motors

When you want position without a feedback loop, torque at standstill, or a motor that runs on the mains and screams at 20 000 rpm — the specialists.

The Stepper — Digital Motion

A stepper moves in fixed angular steps, one per drive pulse, holding position between pulses with no feedback. It is a synchronous machine run open-loop by counting pulses.

Step angle θ = 360° / (Nr · phases · 2)  — a 1.8° hybrid stepper = 200 full steps/rev
TypeRotorStep angleCharacter
Variable ReluctanceToothed soft ironLarge (7.5–15°)Reluctance torque only; no detent
Permanent MagnetMagnetisedCoarse (7.5–18°)Has detent torque (holds unpowered)

  • 1Microstepping — sinusoidally proportion the two phase currents to interpolate between full steps (÷2…÷256), for smoothness and resolution. Torque per microstep falls; accuracy is limited by detent torque, not by the electrical division.
  • 2Open-loop risk — if load torque exceeds available torque the motor skips steps silently and position is lost with no error flag. Size for ≥2× margin, or close the loop (encoder + "closed-loop stepper").
  • 3Mid-band resonance — steppers have a natural oscillation (~100–200 pps) where they can stall; ride through it, add damping, or microstep.
  • 4Torque falls with speed — winding inductance chokes current as pulse rate rises; the torque–speed (pull-out) curve droops steeply. Chopper drives with high bus voltage push current in faster to extend it.
Other Specialists

Universal motor

A series DC motor that also runs on AC (field and armature reverse together). Cheap, very high speed (10–30k rpm), high power density — power tools, vacuums, blenders. Brushes wear; noisy; poor efficiency.

Switched Reluctance (SRM)

Doubly-salient, no magnets, no rotor winding — torque purely by reluctance as stator phases energise in sequence. Rugged, cheap, fault-tolerant; needs a precise controller and can be acoustically noisy (torque ripple).

Hysteresis motor

Smooth, quiet synchronous run using a hard-magnetic ring rotor. Low torque — clocks, gyros, small precision drives.

Single-phase induction

No inherent rotating field — needs a start method: split-phase, capacitor-start, capacitor-run (PSC), shaded-pole. Domestic pumps, fans, compressors. The start/run capacitor is the usual failure.

Quick Check

A 3D-printer axis uses a 1.8° hybrid stepper at 1/16 microstepping driving a 40 mm/rev belt. What is the theoretical linear resolution — and why is the real repeatability far coarser?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Full steps/rev = 360/1.8 = 200; with 1/16 microstepping = 3200 microsteps/rev. Linear resolution = 40 mm ÷ 3200 = 0.0125 mm (12.5 µm) per microstep. But real repeatability is far coarser because microstep position accuracy is set by the ratio of drive torque to detent + load torque, not by the electronic division — the rotor sits where torques balance, which can be off by several microsteps. Add belt compliance, backlash, and missed steps under load, and true positioning is more like ±50–100 µm. Microstepping buys smoothness and quietness, not proportional accuracy — a classic exam and shop-floor trap. (See A18.)

Efficiency vs Load — and Why Over-Sizing Wastes Money

Because iron and friction losses are roughly constant while copper losses grow with load², efficiency peaks near 75% load and falls off at light load (constant losses dominate a small output) and slightly at overload. A motor run at 30% load — a common over-sizing sin — sits well down the efficiency curve and at poor power factor.

Quick Check

A plant runs a 22 kW motor at only 35% load "to be safe." Give two independent reasons this is costing money.

Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Efficiency falls off at light load — the constant iron and friction losses are now a large fraction of the small output, so η is several points below the ~75%-load peak. A correctly sized smaller motor (or a VFD matching output to demand) would run near peak efficiency and good PF. Over-sizing "for safety" is a persistent, invisible energy tax.

Insulation Thermal Classes — Life Halves Every 10°C

The class sets the maximum hot-spot temperature = ambient (40°C ref) + permitted rise + hot-spot allowance. The 10-degree rule: every 10°C above the class limit roughly halves insulation life (Montsinger/Arrhenius). Specifying Class F insulation used to a Class B rise banks a ~25°C margin — the standard reliability hedge, and why VFD-fed motors (extra harmonic heating) and hot climates like Sri Lanka's should insist on it.

IP, IC & Duty Codes

IP — ingress

IP55: 5 = dust-protected, 5 = jets of water. IP54/55 general; IP56/65 washdown/marine. First digit solids, second liquids.

IC — cooling

IC411: totally-enclosed, shaft-fan surface cooling (TEFC) — the workhorse. IC416 = forced (separate blower) for VFD low-speed. IC01 = open drip-proof.

IM — mounting

IM B3 foot, B5 flange, B35 both, V1 vertical. Determines bearing arrangement & thrust capacity.

Quick Check

A Class F motor is run continuously so its winding sits at 150°C hot-spot. It "works." Why is the maintenance engineer still unhappy, and what is the likely outcome?

Tap to reveal ↓A: 150°C is only just under the Class F limit of 155°C, so the motor has used up all its thermal margin — there is no Class-B safety hedge left. By the 10-degree rule, running ~25°C hotter than a class-B rise would allow means insulation life is cut by roughly a factor of 2³ ≈ 8×: a 20-year winding becomes a 2–3-year winding. The outcome is premature insulation breakdown — turn-to-turn shorts, then a phase-to-earth fault. The engineer wants the root cause found (overload, blocked cooling, high ambient, VFD harmonics, under-voltage raising current) and the operating temperature brought back under a class-B rise, not merely "it runs."

Insulation Health — Megger, PI & Surge
  • 1Insulation Resistance (IR / "megger") — apply 500/1000 V DC winding-to-earth. Rule of thumb: ≥ (rated kV + 1) MΩ at 40°C, corrected for temperature. Trends matter more than absolutes.
  • 2Polarization Index (PI) = IR(10 min)/IR(1 min). >2 good, 1–2 questionable, <1 wet/contaminated. It sees moisture and dirt that a spot reading misses.
  • 3Surge / hi-pot — stresses turn-to-turn insulation (IR only tests to earth). Catches the incipient turn short that IR passes. Off-line, done with care.
  • 4Motor Current / Electrical Signature Analysis (MCA/ESA) — on-line: broken rotor bars show as sidebands at (1±2s)·f around the line frequency; eccentricity and bearing faults have their own signatures.

Condition Monitoring — Predict, Don't React

Vibration

1× (unbalance), 2× (misalignment), bearing tones (BPFO/BPFI), 2×line-freq (electrical). The richest early-warning channel → A42.

Thermography

Hot bearings, hot terminals, unbalanced phases show as thermal patterns before failure → A41.

Electrical

MCA/ESA for rotor bars & eccentricity; PI trending for insulation; partial-discharge on MV machines.

Shaft currents & bearing fluting deserve a special mention on VFD-fed motors: fast dV/dt induces a shaft voltage that discharges through the bearing (EDM pitting → fluting → noise → failure). Cures: insulated (hybrid ceramic) NDE bearing, shaft grounding ring, dV/dt or sine filter (see A19 and A08).

Quick Check

A motor passes its IR (megger) test at 500 MΩ but fails within a week with a turn-to-turn short. How did the test miss it, and what test would have caught it?

Tap to reveal ↓A: An IR/megger test measures insulation resistance from the winding to earth. A turn-to-turn (or coil-to-coil) weakness is insulation within the winding, between adjacent conductors at nearly the same potential — the megger's DC voltage barely stresses it, so the reading looks healthy. A surge comparison test (or a controlled AC hi-pot) applies a fast, high-voltage pulse that develops a large volt-drop between turns, exposing the weak inter-turn insulation as a change in the ringing waveform. Lesson: IR proves the ground-wall, not the turn insulation — you need surge testing to certify the latter, which is why both belong in a rewind acceptance test.

Selection, Sizing & Recap

Match the motor to the load's torque profile and thermal duty — then protect and monitor it. The whole module in one workflow.

Load Types — the First Question

The variable-torque row is where the money is: a fan at 80% speed needs only ~51% power (0.8³), so a VFD instead of a damper is the single biggest energy save in most plants.

Thermal Sizing — the RMS-Torque Rule

For a duty cycle of varying load, the motor is sized on the heating (RMS) torque, not the peak — because heating follows I²t. The peak torque must still fit under breakdown (with margin), but continuous rating is governed by:

Trms = √( Σ Ti²·ti / Σ ti )  ≤  Trated  ·  and Tpeak ≤ 0.8·Tbreakdown

For servo/dynamic axes you also match inertia (Jload/Jmotor within ~1–10) for good response and stability — the reflected-inertia problem of A09.

The Selection Workflow
  • 1Load — torque–speed profile, duty cycle (S1…S10), inertia, breakaway, direction/quadrants.
  • 2Speed & control — fixed (DOL/star-delta) or variable (VFD)? Sets poles, and whether you need PMSM/SynRM for efficiency or servo dynamics.
  • 3Environment — ambient, altitude, dust/water (IP), hazardous area (Ex), cooling (IC), mounting (IM). Derate for Sri Lankan heat.
  • 4Efficiency & economics — meet MEPS (IE3 min), then run the life-cycle cost: energy dwarfs purchase price, so buy up a class for high-hour duties.
  • 5Protect & monitor — FLC-set overload (class 10/20), short-circuit, thermistors, phase-loss; add CM (vibration/thermal) for critical machines.
Recap — Eight Ideas to Keep

1 · Field is the gear

Every motor: current in a B-field → force. F=BIℓ and e=BLv are reciprocal.

2 · Slip makes torque

Induction motors must lag the field; s=(ns−nr)/ns, and Pcu2=s·Pag.

3 · T ∝ V²

A 10% sag cuts induction torque 19%. Voltage quality is torque.

4 · No rotor loss = IE5

PMSM/SynRM kill rotor copper loss → the efficiency ceiling.

5 · Nameplate is a contract

Insulation class, IP, IC, duty, SF bound how hard you may push.

6 · 10°C halves life

Spec Class F used to rise B; derate for heat & VFD harmonics.

7 · Size on RMS torque

Heating follows I²t; peak fits under breakdown, continuous under Trms.

8 · Protect the invisible

Single-phasing, unbalance & shaft currents kill quietly. Relay + CM.

Six Ways Motors Die in Service
Overload / over-temp
Sustained I above FLC or blocked cooling cooks insulation — the 10° rule bites. Cure: correct sizing, class 10/20 OL, clean the fins.
Single-phasing
Lose one line; the other two carry 1.7× and the winding burns in a phase pattern. Cure: phase-loss / negative-sequence relay.
Bearing failure
~50% of all motor failures. Contamination, misalignment, lubrication, or VFD shaft currents (fluting). Cure: alignment, sealing, grounding ring → A19.
Insulation breakdown
Turn-to-turn short from thermal ageing, moisture, or VFD dV/dt spikes → phase-to-earth fault. Cure: Class F+, PI trending, dV/dt filter.
Voltage unbalance
A few % unbalance drives a large negative-sequence current that heats the rotor disproportionately. Cure: balance the supply, unbalance relay.
Broken rotor bars
Repeated heavy starts crack cage bars; MCA sidebands at (1±2s)f flag it. Cure: soft-start/VFD, copper rotor, ESA monitoring.
Quick Check

A centrifugal pump is throttled by a valve to 70% flow, wasting energy across the valve. Why is fitting a VFD and slowing the motor so dramatically better — and roughly how much power might it save?

Tap to reveal ↓A: A centrifugal pump is a variable-torque load obeying the affinity laws: flow ∝ speed, head ∝ speed², power ∝ speed³. Throttling to 70% flow with a valve keeps the motor at ~full speed and just burns the surplus head as heat across the valve — power stays near 100%. Slowing the motor to deliver 70% flow instead needs only 0.7³ ≈ 0.34, i.e. ~34% of full power — a saving on the order of 60%+, minus static-head and drive-efficiency corrections. That cubic relationship (see A47 / A08) is why VFDs on fans and pumps are the highest-return energy project in most plants — usually a bigger win than upgrading the motor's IE class.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems worked end-to-end. Cover the answer, try it, then read the discussion.

WE-1 · Slip, Rotor Frequency & Power Flow

A 3-phase, 415 V, 4-pole, 50 Hz induction motor runs at 1440 rpm. Its stator input is 12 kW; stator copper + iron losses total 900 W; friction & windage = 300 W. Find slip, rotor frequency, air-gap power, rotor copper loss, mechanical power and shaft output, and efficiency.

ns = 120·50/4 = 1500 rpm · s = (1500−1440)/1500 = 0.04 (4%) · fr = s·f = 2 Hz
Pag = Pin − (stator losses) = 12 000 − 900 = 11 100 W
Pcu2 = s·Pag = 0.04 × 11 100 = 444 W · Pmech = (1−s)·Pag = 10 656 W
Pshaft = Pmech − F&W = 10 656 − 300 = 10 356 W · η = 10 356/12 000 = 86.3%

Discussion: The 1 : s : (1−s) rule did the heavy lifting — no equivalent circuit needed. Notice rotor copper loss (444 W) is locked to slip; halve the slip (better design or lower load) and you halve that loss. The 86% efficiency is typical of a small older motor; an IE3 version would trim the stator copper and stray losses to reach ~90%.

WE-2 · Breakdown Torque from the Equivalent Circuit

Per-phase (referred): V₁ = 240 V (phase), R₁ = 0.5 Ω, X₁ = X₂′ = 1.0 Ω, R₂′ = 0.4 Ω, magnetising branch neglected. The machine is 4-pole, 50 Hz. Find the slip at maximum torque and the maximum (breakdown) torque per phase basis. (Take Vth≈V₁, Rth≈R₁ with Xm large.)

smaxT = R₂′ / √(Rth² + (Xth+X₂′)²) = 0.4 / √(0.5² + 2.0²) = 0.4/2.062 = 0.194
ωs = 4πf/P = 4π·50/4 = 157.1 rad/s
Tmax = (3/ωs)·Vth² / [2·(Rth + √(Rth²+(Xth+X₂′)²))]
= (3/157.1)·240² / [2·(0.5 + 2.062)] = 0.01910·57 600 / 5.124 = 214.7 N·m

Discussion: Tmax does not contain R₂′ — proof that breakdown torque is set by the leakage reactances and voltage, while rotor resistance only relocates the slip at which it occurs (here ~19%). Double R₂′ (add external rotor resistance) and smaxT would jump toward 0.39, moving peak torque toward standstill for starting — the wound-rotor lever, at the same Tmax.

WE-3 · DC Motor Speed & Field Weakening

A 220 V DC shunt motor: Ra = 0.4 Ω, runs at 1000 rpm drawing Ia = 25 A. (a) Find Eb. (b) The field flux is then reduced to 80% (constant load torque). Find the new armature current and speed.

(a) Eb1 = V − IaRa = 220 − 25·0.4 = 210 V
T ∝ Φ·Ia constant ⇒ Ia2 = Ia1·(Φ₁/Φ₂) = 25/0.8 = 31.25 A
Eb2 = 220 − 31.25·0.4 = 207.5 V · ω ∝ Eb/Φ
N₂ = N₁·(Eb2/Eb1)·(Φ₁/Φ₂) = 1000·(207.5/210)·(1/0.8) = 1235 rpm

Discussion: Weakening the field raises speed (by ~24% here) but forces more armature current for the same torque — the constant-power trade above base speed. Push it too far and commutation suffers and Ia overheats the armature. This is exactly the field-weakening region a VFD emulates above base frequency on an induction motor.

WE-4 · Efficiency Upgrade Payback (IE2 → IE4)

A 37 kW motor runs 6 500 h/yr at 80% load. Replace an IE2 unit (η = 91.0%) with an IE4 (η = 94.3%). Tariff = 45 LKR/kWh. Price premium = 180 000 LKR. Find annual energy saved and simple payback.

Output power drawn = 37·0.80 = 29.6 kW · Input(IE2) = 29.6/0.910 = 32.53 kW · Input(IE4) = 29.6/0.943 = 31.39 kW
ΔP = 32.53 − 31.39 = 1.14 kW · Energy saved = 1.14 × 6 500 = 7 410 kWh/yr
Saving = 7 410 × 45 = 333 450 LKR/yr · Payback = 180 000 / 333 450 = 0.54 yr ≈ 6.5 months

Discussion: A 3.3-point efficiency gain on a high-hours motor pays for itself in well under a year, then saves ~333 k LKR every year of a 15–20-year life. This is why life-cycle costing — not purchase price — must drive motor buying, and why MEPS keep ratcheting the floor. Add a VFD if the load is variable-torque and the saving multiplies again.

Problem Set

Ten problems, increasing difficulty. Work each fully before revealing.

P1 · Synchronous speed

A 6-pole motor on a 60 Hz supply — synchronous speed? And at 3% slip, shaft speed?

Tap to reveal ↓A: ns = 120·60/6 = 1200 rpm. nr = 1200(1−0.03) = 1164 rpm.
P2 · Torque from power

An 11 kW motor runs at 1455 rpm. What is the shaft torque?

Tap to reveal ↓A: T = 9550·P/N = 9550·11/1455 = 72.2 N·m. (Or T = P/ω = 11000/(2π·1455/60) = 11000/152.4 = 72.2 N·m.)
P3 · Voltage-sag torque

The supply to an induction motor sags to 85% of rated during a neighbouring DOL start. By how much does available torque fall?

Tap to reveal ↓A: T ∝ V², so T falls to 0.85² = 0.7225 → a drop of ~27.8%. If load torque exceeded 72% of normal breakdown, the motor could stall. This is why large starts are staggered on weak feeders.
P4 · Rotor copper loss

A motor's air-gap power is 20 kW and it runs at 5% slip. What is the rotor copper loss, and what does raising slip to 10% do to it?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Pcu2 = s·Pag = 0.05·20 000 = 1000 W. At 10% slip it doubles to 2000 W for the same air-gap power — rotor heating is directly proportional to slip, the reason high-slip control cooks rotors.
P5 · Star-delta starting torque

A motor's DOL starting torque is 180% of full-load. What is its starting torque in star during a star-delta start?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Star gives ⅓ of DOL torque (and ⅓ of DOL current). Star start torque = 180%/3 = 60% of full-load torque. If the load needs more than 60% to break away, star-delta will stall in star — pick a higher-torque design or a soft-starter/VFD. (See A51.)
P6 · Efficiency at part load

Why can a motor's power factor drop below 0.6 at 25% load even though its full-load PF is 0.87?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The magnetising current (reactive, ~constant, set by the air-gap) stays roughly fixed while the working (real) current shrinks with load. As real current falls, the ratio of real to total current — the power factor — collapses. Hence over-sized, lightly-loaded motors both waste energy and drag the plant PF down.
P7 · Insulation life

A Class F winding designed for a class-B rise (nominal hot-spot ~130°C) is actually run at 160°C. Estimate the relative life.

Tap to reveal ↓A: ΔT above the intended 130°C is 30°C. Life halves per 10°C, so relative life ≈ (½)^(30/10) = (½)³ = 1/8 of design life — an 18-year winding becomes ~2 years. And 160°C also exceeds the 155°C Class F ceiling, so ageing is even faster than the linear rule implies.
P8 · Single-phasing current

A delta-connected motor loses one supply line while running. Roughly what happens to the current in the two remaining lines, and why is it dangerous?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The motor keeps running (the rotating field degrades to pulsating but inertia carries it) at reduced torque, but to deliver similar power on two lines the remaining line currents rise to roughly 1.7–2× normal. Because it still turns, no obvious symptom appears, yet the overheated windings burn out within minutes-to-hours in a characteristic phase pattern. A phase-loss / negative-sequence relay is essential.
P9 · PMSM vs induction efficiency

Two 15 kW motors, same frame, one induction, one PMSM, both on drives. Why is the PMSM typically 2–4 points more efficient, and where does that gap come from?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The PMSM's rotor is excited by permanent magnets, so at steady state it carries essentially no rotor current and no rotor copper loss (s·Pag = 0). In the induction motor, rotor copper loss is unavoidable — it is the price of induction — and it is largest of the load-dependent losses after stator copper. Removing that whole bucket is why PMSM/SynRM reach IE4/IE5 where induction struggles.
P10 · Stepper step count

A 0.9° hybrid stepper drives a leadscrew of 5 mm pitch at 1/8 microstepping. What is the linear resolution per microstep, and why should you not quote it as the machine's accuracy?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Full steps/rev = 360/0.9 = 400; ×8 microsteps = 3200/rev. Linear = 5 mm/3200 = 1.56 µm per microstep. But that is resolution, not accuracy: microstep position is set by the balance of drive torque against detent + load torque, so the rotor can sit several microsteps off the commanded angle; add leadscrew backlash and lost steps under load and true repeatability is far coarser. Microstepping buys smoothness, not proportional precision.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Derive the three results examiners love, keep the symbols straight, and sidestep the seven classic mistakes.

Derivation 1 · The Air-Gap Power Split (1 : s : 1−s)

In the equivalent circuit the entire rotor branch resistance is R₂′/s, carrying rotor current I₂′. The power crossing the air-gap is the power into that resistance:

Pag = 3·I₂′²·(R₂′/s)

The actual rotor copper loss is the power in the real rotor resistance R₂′:

Pcu2 = 3·I₂′²·R₂′ = s·[3·I₂′²·(R₂′/s)] = s·Pag

The remainder is mechanical, modelled by the split-out resistor R₂′(1−s)/s:

Pmech = 3·I₂′²·R₂′·(1−s)/s = (1−s)·Pag  ⇒  Pag : Pcu2 : Pmech = 1 : s : (1−s)
Derivation 2 · Slip at Maximum Torque

From the Thevenin torque T = (3/ωs)·Vth²·(R₂′/s) / [(Rth+R₂′/s)² + (Xth+X₂′)²], set dT/ds = 0. The maximum occurs where the rotor branch resistance equals the magnitude of the rest of the series impedance:

R₂′/s = √(Rth² + (Xth+X₂′)²)  ⇒  smaxT = R₂′ / √(Rth² + (Xth+X₂′)²)

Substituting back, Tmax = (3/ωs)·Vth² / [2·(Rth + √(Rth²+(Xth+X₂′)²))] — independent of R₂′. So rotor resistance sets where the peak sits, never how high it is. This is the maximum-power-transfer idea in rotating-machine clothing.

Derivation 3 · The DC Motor Speed Equation

KVL round the armature: V = Eb + IaRa, with Eb = kΦω. Solve for ω:

ω = (V − IaRa) / (kΦ)  ·  and since T = kΦIa, Ia = T/(kΦ)
ω = V/(kΦ) − RaT/(kΦ)²  — a straight line: no-load speed V/(kΦ) minus a droop ∝ torque

This is the mathematical shape of the shunt curve on Page 3, and the template for every four-quadrant drive: change V to translate the line up/down, change Φ to rotate its slope.

Seven Exam & Field Traps
  • 1Rated speed ≠ synchronous. A "1500 rpm" motor runs at ~1440–1470; 1500 is the field speed, reached only at zero load (never on an induction machine).
  • 2T ∝ V², not V. A 10% sag costs 19% torque — the most-missed factor-of-two-ish error.
  • 3Tmax is independent of R₂′. Rotor resistance moves the slip of peak torque, not its value.
  • 4Nameplate kW is output. Input = output/η. Don't size cables/protection off the shaft power.
  • 5Star-delta gives ⅓ torque and ⅓ current — not ⅓ voltage-then-linear. If the load needs >⅓ breakaway, star stalls.
  • 6Microstepping ≠ accuracy. It divides the electrical angle, not the mechanical error; detent torque limits real position.
  • 7A PM motor is always a generator. A spinning PMSM is live at its terminals with the drive off — brake before touching.
