📷Machine Vision

Machine vision is not image processing with a camera bolted on. It is a photon budget that ends in a relay contact. Every hard failure in this field happens upstream of the software, in four quantities that fight each other and cannot all be won: resolution, depth of field, light, and time. This module derives that fight in closed form — including the one law that says depth of field is fixed by your resolution alone — and then follows a single Sri Lankan bottling line from LKR 2.8 million a month of invisible loss to a twenty-day payback.

A Bottling Line That Rejected Thirty Thousand Good Bottles a Month — and Shipped the Bad Ones

COLD OPEN — BIYAGAMA, 400 mL PET BEVERAGE LINE 3 CORE

Twelve thousand bottles an hour, sixteen hours a day, twenty-six days a month — 4,992,000 bottles. The conveyor runs at 600 mm/s on a 180 mm pitch. Two vision stations guard the end of the line: one reads the continuous-inkjet best-before code on the shoulder of the bottle, one checks that the cap is present and square. Both were commissioned three years ago by a good integrator, both are “working”, and the plant's quality dashboard shows a 99.4 % pass rate that nobody has ever questioned.

The line is quietly losing about LKR 2.82 million every month. Not one rupee of it appears on the vision system's own screen, because the vision system is not broken. It is doing exactly what its optics permit it to do, which is considerably less than anyone assumed on the day the purchase order was signed.

FAULT 1 — THE CODE READER THAT CANNOT SEE THE CODE CORE

The date-code camera is a 5 megapixel unit: 2448 × 2048 pixels on 3.45 µm pixels, so an 8.45 × 7.07 mm sensor. During commissioning the field of view was opened to 320 mm, because the plant wanted the same camera to also see the neck band, the shoulder and — on a future product — a second lane. Nobody objected. That single decision, made in ten seconds, is the fault.

Spatial resolution: s_px = FOV / N_px = 320 mm / 2448 px = 0.1307 mm/px The CIJ printer lays down a 5×7 dot-matrix character: character height 3.0 mm → 3.0 / 0.1307 = 22.9 px (looks fine) stroke width 0.30 mm → 0.30 / 0.1307 = 2.29 px (fatal) OCR needs ≥ 3 px across the narrowest stroke — 4 px to be comfortable. At 2.29 px the stroke straddles two pixels with a random phase: sometimes it lands centred and reads black, sometimes it splits and reads mid-grey.

This is not a marginal miss. Sub-three-pixel strokes fail as a function of where the bottle happens to sit on the pixel grid, which is random, which is why the failure looks intermittent and therefore looks like a printer problem. Three years of maintenance tickets blame the inkjet.

The measured consequence: a 0.62 % reject rate on “code unreadable”. A one-week manual audit pulled 400 rejected bottles out of the bin and put them under a desk lamp: 94 % carried a perfectly legible, correctly printed code.

Rejected for “unreadable code” 0.0062 × 4,992,000 = 30,950 bottles/month Of which genuinely good 0.94 × 30,950 = 29,093 bottles/month Loss per bottle (filled, capped, labelled, unsaleable) LKR 55 FAULT 1 COST = LKR 1,600,136 per month
FAULT 2 — THE CAP CHECK THAT MEASURES A TILT THAT ISN'T THERE CORE

The cap station uses a colour rolling-shutter camera at 60 frames per second. A rolling shutter does not expose the whole sensor at once; it exposes row 1, then row 2, then row 3, sweeping down the frame. At 60 fps the frame period is 16.7 ms, of which the datasheet gives 16.0 ms as the readout of the 2048 rows — the rest is blanking — so each row starts 7.81 µs after the one above it. Meanwhile the bottle is moving at 600 mm/s.

Every row therefore sees the bottle a little further along than the row above. A vertical cap edge is recorded as a sheared edge — a leaning cap that is not leaning. The magnitude falls out of two numbers and nothing else:

Shear angle θ = arctan( v · t_row / s_px ) v = 600 mm/s conveyor speed t_row = 16.0 ms / 2048 = 7.8125 µs per row s_px = 0.0569 mm/px (cap station, tighter FOV) θ = arctan( 600 × 7.8125e-6 / 0.0569 ) = arctan(0.0824) θ = 4.71° — against a reject threshold of 3.0°

Read that again: the artefact is bigger than the tolerance. Every bottle arrives at the algorithm pre-tilted by 4.71° in a fixed direction. The integrator, three years ago, solved this the way everyone solves it — by subtracting a constant offset until good bottles passed. The system now measures tilt relative to a 4.71° baseline, which works perfectly until the line speed changes, and which has the side effect of cancelling out real tilt in one direction. Caps cocked the other way sail through.

Measured escape rate (traceability audit, three months) 1 in 4,300 bottles 4,992,000 / 4,300 = 1,161 leakers shipped per month Cost per leaker (distributor credit, case rework, complaint handling) LKR 900 FAULT 2 COST = LKR 1,044,837 per month

Plus one item that does not appear in any of these numbers: a supermarket chain's quality escalation, and the eleven days of management time it consumed.

FAULT 3 — THE SKYLIGHT STRETCH

Neither station is enclosed. Both use white LED bar lights and no optical filter. Directly above the line, forty metres of translucent roof sheeting admits daylight that varies by a factor of five between 07:00 and 15:00, and again with the season.

Daylight is not a small perturbation here. It adds a variable grey-level pedestal to every image — and a fixed threshold sitting on a moving pedestal is a threshold that moves. Around 13:00 on clear days the reject rate climbs, an operator is called, and the operator does the only thing available on the HMI: lowers the threshold until the rejects stop. That converts the station from over-rejecting to under-detecting, and nobody writes it down. Between 06:00 and 08:00 the next morning somebody raises it again.

Six re-tuning stoppages per month × 22 min = 132 min = 2.2 h Line contribution margin LKR 78,000 / h FAULT 3 COST = LKR 171,600 per month (and it is the mechanism that keeps Fault 2 alive)

The Chain, the Photon Budget, and the Four Quantities That Cannot All Be Won

WHAT MACHINE VISION ACTUALLY IS CORE

Machine vision is the engineering of a decision — pass, fail, position, count, measurement — produced automatically from radiant energy that has interacted with a part. The image is an intermediate product, not the goal. The camera is not the sensor; the illuminated scene is the sensor, and the camera merely digitises it.

That framing is not philosophy, it is operational. It tells you where to spend money (light and optics, not processors), where to look first when something fails (upstream, not downstream), and why a vision engineer's most-used instrument is a tape measure.

That asymmetry is the reason so many vision projects are declared “an AI problem” when they are a lighting problem.

The photon budget. Signal is a chain of multiplications; noise is a square root. That asymmetry is the whole economics of illumination.

PREDICT FIRST
Your image is too dark. You may make exactly one change. Which buys usable signal without damaging something else you need?
D is the only one that is free of a side effect. Gain (A) multiplies the signal and the noise — the SNR does not improve at all, and above unity gain you are usually throwing away dynamic range; gain is a display control masquerading as a sensitivity control. Opening the aperture (B) does add four times the light, but it divides the depth of field by two and, past about f/4 on a 3.45 µm pixel, the extra light arrives already blurred by diffraction. Longer exposure (C) adds light in exact proportion, but it also multiplies motion blur by four — on our line, 800 µs at 600 mm/s is 8.4 px of smear. Only more photons at the scene — a brighter source, a closer source, a better geometry, or a strobe — raises the signal without spending resolution, depth or time. In machine vision, light is the only currency with no exchange rate.
THE FOUR-WAY FIGHT CORE

Everything difficult in this field is one of four quantities being traded against another. It is worth learning them as a closed system, because a request to improve any one of them is automatically a request to give up one of the others:

  • 1Resolution — millimetres per pixel. Improve it by shrinking the field of view or adding pixels. Shrinking the FOV costs coverage; adding pixels costs bandwidth, processing time and, at fixed sensor size, light per pixel.
  • 2Depth of field — how far the part may wander along the optical axis. Improve it by closing the aperture. Closing the aperture costs light quadratically and, past the diffraction ceiling, costs resolution. Page 5 proves that at a given resolution there is a hard maximum depth of field you can never exceed, whatever lens you buy.
  • 3Light — photons per pixel per exposure. Improve it with a brighter or better-placed source. This is the only one of the four with no intrinsic penalty, which is why it is the answer to most problems — and why it is the one most often left at “whatever came in the box”.
  • 4Time — exposure duration, frame rate, latency. Improve motion fidelity by shortening exposure; that costs light in exact proportion. Improve throughput by raising frame rate; that costs readout time, which costs shear on a rolling shutter and processing headroom everywhere.
The whole module in one line: light → buys either resolution, or depth, or speed — pick two. and the exchange rates are not opinions. They are: light ∝ 1/N² (aperture) light ∝ t (exposure) blur ∝ v · t / s_px (motion) DOF ∝ N (aperture) Airy ∝ N (diffraction ceiling on N)
WHERE THE STANDARDS SIT STRETCH

Three standards bodies matter, and knowing which one owns which question saves a great deal of argument with vendors:

EMVA 1288

Camera measurement. Release 4.0 in effect since June 2021, with a “Linear” module and a new “General” module that can characterise non-linear or pre-processed cameras. It defines how quantum efficiency, temporal dark noise, saturation capacity, SNR and dynamic range are measured, so two datasheets are comparable.

GenICam

Camera control. A transport-independent programming interface: the camera ships an XML description of its own features, so one application can drive any compliant camera on any interface. It is why “ExposureTime” means the same thing on a GigE and a CoaXPress camera.

Transport standards

Getting pixels out. GigE Vision (~1 Gbit/s per link, 10 GigE variants above), USB3 Vision (~4.5 Gbit/s, short cable), CoaXPress CXP-12 (12.5 Gbit/s per link, up to four links). Bandwidth, cable length and trigger fidelity are the three axes.

A useful discipline when reading a camera datasheet: if a number is not EMVA 1288, treat it as marketing. “Sensitivity”, “low-light performance” and “dynamic range” quoted without the standard are not comparable between manufacturers, and occasionally not comparable between two models from the same manufacturer.

Illumination: The Component That Decides Whether the Problem Is Easy or Impossible

THE CENTRAL CLAIM CORE

A machine vision algorithm cannot recover contrast that the illumination did not create. Every threshold, every edge finder, every neural network is a function of grey levels; if the feature and its background have the same grey level, no amount of processing distinguishes them. Lighting is not a way of making the image nicer. It is the act of encoding the feature into the signal.

The practical form of this claim: a good lighting solution makes the defect the brightest or darkest thing in the frame. If you have achieved that, the algorithm is a threshold and a blob count, it runs in two milliseconds, and it will still be working in ten years. If you have not, you will be tuning parameters forever and eventually someone will suggest deep learning — which, as page 11 argues, is frequently a very expensive way of paying for the light you did not buy.

An interview question that separates people who have done this from people who have read about it: “How would you find a clear scratch on a clear plastic lid?” The answer is not a filter or an algorithm — it is dark-field illumination. A scratch is a slope discontinuity; at grazing incidence it scatters light into the lens while the flat surface reflects everything away. The scratch turns white on a black background and the problem collapses to a blob count.

THE SIX GEOMETRIES CORE

The six geometries. Note that four of them differ only in where the light is — the camera does not move. Lighting design is geometry design.

A rule that will serve you for a career: work through this list in order of contrast, not in order of convenience. Backlight gives the highest contrast of any technique in machine vision — typically 200:1 or better, because the background is a light source and the part is opaque — so if the measurement can be made from a silhouette, make it from a silhouette. Only when the feature is genuinely on the surface do you move to diffuse, dark-field or coaxial.

WAVELENGTH, FILTERS AND THE DEFEAT OF AMBIENT LIGHT CORE

This is the part of illumination that plants get wrong most consistently, and it is exactly Fault 3 of our line. The reasoning runs in three steps.

Step 1 — choose a narrow-band source. Monochromatic LED illumination (typically 450 nm blue, 525 nm green, 625–660 nm red, 850–940 nm near-IR) concentrates all of your photons into a 20–30 nm band instead of spreading them across 300 nm of visible spectrum.

Step 2 — put the matching bandpass filter on the lens. The filter passes your band and rejects everything else. Your signal loses only the filter's insertion loss, roughly 10–20 %. Broadband ambient light loses the ratio of the filter bandwidth to its own spectral extent.

Ambient rejection ratio ≈ filter FWHM / ambient spectral width Bandpass 660 nm, FWHM 20 nm; daylight spread over ≈400 nm of visible fraction of ambient surviving = 20 / 400 = 5 % → a 20× suppression Line 3 pedestal: 35 grey levels of daylight swing → 1.75 grey levels Signal contrast (print vs PET): ≈40 grey levels, unchanged apart from the filter's ~15 % insertion loss → 34 grey levels Signal-to-ambient-drift ratio: 40/35 = 1.14 → 34/1.75 = 19.4

Step 3 — strobe, and let the duty cycle do the rest. A strobed LED can be driven far above its continuous rating for a short pulse. Firing a 660 nm strobe for 95 µs once per bottle means the scene is dominated by your light during the only 95 µs that matters, while the ambient contribution is scaled down by the exposure ratio as well as by the filter.

Ambient contribution scales with exposure time; strobed signal does not. Old station: 2 ms exposure, continuous white LED, no filter New station: 95 µs exposure, 660 nm strobe, 20 nm bandpass ambient suppression = (95 µs / 2 ms) × (20 nm / 400 nm) = 0.0475 × 0.05 = 0.0024 → a 420× reduction The skylight problem does not get better. It stops existing.
🏭

In Sri Lankan factories this matters more than in the European plants most textbooks were written in. Translucent roof sheeting is common, ambient level swings hard between monsoon overcast and clear-sky noon, and few production halls are light-controlled. Enclosure plus monochromatic source plus matched bandpass filter should be the default specification on every vision station you commission here, not an upgrade option. It typically costs LKR 40,000–70,000 per station and removes an entire category of failure permanently.

STROBING, PULSE ENERGY AND THE LED YOU WILL DESTROY STRETCH

Overdriving LEDs is legitimate and standard, but it is bounded by junction temperature, not by courage. A useful mental model: the die has a thermal time constant of order milliseconds, so a pulse much shorter than that is absorbed by the die's own heat capacity, and what matters is the average power plus the peak current limit of the die.

Duty cycle D = t_pulse × f_trigger Line 3: D = 95 µs × 3.333 /s = 3.17e-4 = 0.032 % A controller permitting 10× overdrive at ≤1 % duty is comfortable here by a factor of 30 on duty — the binding limit is the LED's absolute peak forward current, not the thermal average. Radiant energy delivered per image: E = P_peak × t_pulse Doubling P_peak and halving t is energy-neutral and blur-positive — which is the whole reason strobes exist.
  • 1Always drive a strobe from a dedicated strobe controller, triggered by the camera's own strobe output, not by the PLC. The camera knows when its shutter is open to the microsecond; the PLC knows it to the scan.
  • 2Check the LED's peak forward current against the controller setting on commissioning day and write it in the file. The commonest cause of a vision station going dim over eighteen months is an overdriven emitter that nobody has recorded as overdriven.
  • 3Beware the eye-safety question with high-power strobes and any laser line — IEC 60825-1 classification applies to structured-light heads, and a Class 2M line laser at 660 nm still deserves an enclosure and a label.
  • 4Mount the light so it can be cleaned. A dusty diffuser is a slow, invisible attenuation that changes your grey levels by tens of percent over a year, and it will be blamed on the camera.

Optics and the Sizing Chain: Six Numbers That Decide the Project

THE CHAIN, IN THE ORDER YOU MUST WALK IT CORE

Sizing a vision station is a deterministic calculation, not a matter of taste. It starts at the smallest feature that matters and ends at a lens part number, and every intermediate quantity is forced. If you begin anywhere else — at a camera you have in the cupboard, at a field of view somebody sketched, at a budget — you will produce the Line 3 date-code station.

  • 1Smallest feature d — the narrowest dimension of the thing you must detect or measure. Not the part. Not the defect's length. Its narrowest dimension. On Line 3 that is the 0.30 mm CIJ stroke, not the 3 mm character.
  • 2Pixels required across it k — from the table below. This is where engineering judgement enters, and it is the only place it does.
  • 3Spatial resolution s_px = d / k mm per pixel. Forced.
  • 4Field of view FOV = N_px × s_px, with a margin for part position tolerance. Forced, given the sensor.
  • 5Magnification m = h_sensor / FOV, and equivalently m = p / s_px — two forms of the same number that will save you repeatedly.
  • 6Focal length from the working distance you are allowed. Forced.
HOW MANY PIXELS DOES A FEATURE NEED? CORE

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem says that to represent a feature of size d you need samples closer than d/2 — two pixels across the feature. That is a theoretical floor for a known, phase-aligned, noiseless signal, and it is the source of an enormous amount of under-specified machine vision. On a real line the feature's position on the pixel grid is random, the edges are not step functions, and the signal has noise. The practical requirement is higher, and it depends on what you are asking:

THE LENS EQUATION, AND WHY THE FAMOUS SHORTCUT IS WRONG BY 6 % CORE

Almost every vendor page gives the sizing formula as f = WD × h_sensor / FOV. It is an approximation, valid when magnification is small, and it is worth carrying the exact form because at the magnifications used in code reading and small-part inspection the error is enough to make you order the wrong lens.

Thin lens: 1/a + 1/b = 1/f m = b/a with a = object distance (working distance to the principal plane) b = image distance, m = magnification = h_sensor / FOV b = f(1+m) a = f(1+m)/m f = a · m/(1+m) = WD · h_sensor / (FOV + h_sensor) <- exact f ≈ WD · h_sensor / FOV <- the shortcut Line 3 date-code station, target FOV 140 mm, WD 350 mm, h_s = 8.446 mm: exact f = 350 × 8.446 / (140 + 8.446) = 19.91 mm shortcut f = 350 × 8.446 / 140 = 21.11 mm (+6.0 %) Fit the nearest standard C-mount lens, f = 20 mm, and the geometry inverts to give what you will actually see: m = f/(WD - f) = 20/330 = 0.06061 FOV = h_s/m = 8.446/0.06061 = 139.35 mm (× 116.6 mm vertical) s_px = FOV/N_px = 139.35/2448 = 0.05693 mm/px = 56.9 µm/px CIJ stroke 0.30 mm → 0.30/0.05693 = 5.27 px ✓ comfortable Character 3.0 mm → 52.7 px tall ✓ far above the 20 px floor

Two habits fall out of this. First, always invert: choose the standard focal length, then recompute the field of view you will really get, because that is the number the mechanical team must design the guarding around. Second, note the identity m = p / s_px — magnification is nothing but the ratio of pixel pitch to spatial resolution. Telecentricity in one line: An entocentric lens accepts a cone of rays from each object point, so the image size depends on distance: Δsize/size ≈ Δz / WD A telecentric lens accepts only rays parallel to the axis, so within its telecentric range the image size does not depend on distance at all. That single calculation is the whole telecentric argument, and it is the one to run whenever a customer asks for a measurement in millimetres rather than a pass/fail. If the part can move along the optical axis by more than about 1/300 of the working distance, an ordinary lens will fail the measurement on geometry alone, before noise, calibration or algorithm are even considered.

PREDICT FIRST
You must resolve a 0.10 mm feature across a 250 mm-wide product. Your 5 MP camera (2448 px wide) is already installed. What is the honest answer?
C. The requirement is s_px ≤ d/k = 0.10/3 = 0.033 mm/px, which needs 250/0.033 = 7,500 px across the width. A 5 MP sensor gives 2448. You are short by a factor of 3.06 — so it is a 20 MP+ camera, three 5 MP cameras tiled with overlap, or a line-scan camera at 8k. Option A is the Line 3 mistake stated as arithmetic: one pixel per feature is below the Nyquist floor, never mind the practical floor. No lens (B) creates spatial samples that the sensor does not have; sharpness sets how well you use the pixels you own, not how many you own. And light (D) improves contrast, not sampling. Resolution failures are the one class of vision problem that money can only solve with more pixels or a smaller field of view.
RESOLVING POWER IS NOT PIXEL COUNT: MTF STRETCH

Pixels tell you how finely the image is sampled. The lens decides how much contrast survives to be sampled, and the standard description of that is the modulation transfer function: the ratio of image contrast to object contrast as a function of spatial frequency, in line pairs per millimetre.

Contrast (modulation) M = (I_max - I_min) / (I_max + I_min) MTF(ν) = M_image(ν) / M_object(ν) ν in lp/mm Sensor Nyquist frequency, at the sensor: ν_Ny = 1/(2p) p = 3.45 µm → ν_Ny = 145 lp/mm Referred to the object, divide by magnification: ν_object = ν_Ny × m = 145 × 0.0606 = 8.8 lp/mm at the part The system MTF is the product of the parts: MTF_sys = MTF_lens × MTF_sensor × MTF_motion × MTF_defocus Anything below MTF ≈ 0.2 is contrast you cannot reliably threshold.

Two consequences follow that are worth internalising. First, MTF is multiplicative: a 0.6 lens on a 0.7 sensor with a 0.8 motion term gives 0.34, and adding a little defocus at 0.6 takes it to 0.20 — the edge of usability. Small degradations compound quickly and invisibly. Second, a lens must be specified for the pixel it will sit on. A lens whose MTF has collapsed by 145 lp/mm cannot exploit a 3.45 µm pixel, and mounting a 5 MP-rated lens on a 12 MP camera buys you file size, not information. Ask the vendor for the MTF curve at your f-number and your wavelength; if they cannot supply it, that is itself information.

The Optical Squeeze: Why Depth of Field Is Decided by Your Resolution, Not Your Lens

THE PROBLEM, STATED HONESTLY CORE

Every engineer meets this fight in the first week: the part wobbles, so you need more depth of field, so you close the aperture. The image gets dark, so you add light or lengthen the exposure. And somewhere past f/8 the image stops getting sharper as you stop down — it starts getting softer. This is not a bad lens. It is diffraction, it is unavoidable, and it caps the depth of field you can ever have at a given resolution.

The result derived on this page is, in my view, the single most useful piece of optics an automation engineer can carry: at a chosen spatial resolution and wavelength, the maximum usable depth of field is fixed — it does not depend on the sensor, the pixel size, the focal length or the price of the lens.

STEP 1 — DEPTH OF FIELD CORE

Depth of field is a tolerance, not a property: it is the range of object distance over which the blur circle of a point stays below an agreed circle of confusion c. In machine vision, unlike photography, c has an obvious natural unit — the pixel. One pixel is aggressive; two pixels is the usual working choice and is what we will use.

Total depth of field, for a lens at magnification m and f-number N: DOF ≈ 2 · N · c · (1 + m) / m² Line 3 date-code station: m = 0.06061, p = 3.45 µm, take c = 2p = 6.9 µm N = f/4 DOF = 2(4)(0.0069)(1.0606)/0.003674 = 15.9 mm N = f/5.6 DOF = 22.3 mm N = f/8 DOF = 31.9 mm N = f/11 DOF = 43.8 mm Note DOF ∝ N: stopping down two stops doubles the depth. That is the free lunch everybody reaches for. Step 2 is the bill.
STEP 2 — THE DIFFRACTION CEILING CORE

A lens of finite aperture cannot form a point image. A point source becomes an Airy pattern, whose central disc diameter grows in direct proportion to the f-number:

Airy disc diameter at the sensor: d_Airy = 2.44 · λ · N · (1 + m) At λ = 660 nm (red LED), m = 0.0606, pixel p = 3.45 µm: f/2.8 d = 4.78 µm = 1.39 px f/8 d = 13.66 µm = 3.96 px f/4 d = 6.83 µm = 1.98 px f/11 d = 18.79 µm = 5.45 px f/5.6 d = 9.56 µm = 2.77 px f/16 d = 27.33 µm = 7.92 px The optics are wasting the sensor as soon as d_Airy exceeds about 2 px (two samples across the blur = the sampling limit). Solve for that N: 2p = 2.44 · λ · N · (1+m) N_max = 2p / (2.44 · λ · (1+m)) = 0.0069/(2.44×0.00066×1.0606) N_max = f/4.04

So on this station f/4 is not a preference, it is a ceiling. At f/8 the image is being blurred across four pixels by physics before it reaches the sensor: the camera is a 5 megapixel camera delivering about 1.3 megapixels of real information. This is the most commonly missed fact in industrial imaging, and it gets worse every time pixels shrink — a 2.74 µm pixel pushes the ceiling to about f/3.2.

STEP 3 — THE RESULT WORTH MEMORISING CORE

Now substitute the ceiling into the depth-of-field expression. Two cancellations happen, and they are the interesting part.

Read what has just happened. The pixel size disappeared. The focal length disappeared. The sensor format disappeared. The maximum depth of field over which you can hold a stated spatial resolution depends on that resolution and the wavelength, and on nothing else you can buy. Doubling your megapixels at the same field of view halves s_px and therefore quarters the depth of field available to you.

That table is the answer to a whole family of arguments. Sensors, Shutters and Time: The Two Ways Motion Destroys an Image

MOTION CAUSES TWO DIFFERENT DEFECTS, AND THEY HAVE DIFFERENT CURES CORE

A moving part damages the image in two independent ways. Blur comes from the shutter being open while the part moves, and its cure is a shorter exposure. Shear comes from different rows of the sensor being exposed at different instants, and a shorter exposure does not cure it at all. Confusing the two is Fault 2 of Line 3, and it is one of the most expensive misunderstandings in the field.

DEFECT 2 — ROLLING SHUTTER SHEAR CORE

A global-shutter sensor transfers the charge of every pixel to shielded storage at the same instant; the whole frame represents one moment. A rolling-shutter sensor resets and reads out row by row, so the frame is a diagonal slice through space-time. For a stationary scene this is invisible. For a moving part it is a systematic geometric distortion.

Row-to-row delay: t_row = t_readout / N_rows Line 3 cap camera, 60 fps (16.67 ms frame period). The datasheet gives the row readout of the 2048 rows as 16.0 ms; the balance is blanking. Take t_readout from the datasheet, never from 1/frame-rate. t_row = 16.0 ms / 2048 = 7.8125 µs Between one row and the next, the part moves v · t_row in object units, which is v · t_row / s_px pixels sideways per pixel downwards. So a vertical edge is recorded leaning by: θ_shear = arctan( v · t_row / s_px ) = arctan( 600 × 7.8125e-6 / 0.05693 ) = arctan( 0.08234 ) = 4.71°

Now the part that makes this a law worth remembering, and the reason it defeated three years of troubleshooting on Line 3: the shear angle contains no reference to the size of the object, the height of the region of interest, or the number of rows used. Cropping the ROI to speed the camera up does not reduce it. Zooming in does not reduce it — halving s_px actually doubles it. Shortening the exposure does not reduce it, because exposure and readout are independent. The only levers are slower part, faster readout, or a global shutter.

Noise, Signal-to-Noise Ratio, and the Smallest Contrast You Can Ever Detect

WHY THIS PAGE EXISTS CORE

Every pass/fail decision is ultimately a comparison of grey levels, and grey levels are random variables. A defect is detectable when the difference it makes exceeds the noise by enough margin to be reliable — and both halves of that sentence are computable in advance. The engineer who can compute the minimum detectable contrast before quoting a job avoids the two career-defining mistakes: promising detection that physics forbids, and buying a camera three times better than the problem required.

The practical target: expose the feature of interest to 50–70 % of full well. Not the brightest thing in the image — the feature. Highlights may clip; that is usually fine and often desirable. THE RESULT: MINIMUM DETECTABLE CONTRAST CORE

A defect changes the signal by ΔS. It is reliably detectable when ΔS exceeds the noise by a confidence factor k — the Rose criterion takes k = 5 for reliable visual detection, and 3 to 5 is the industrial range. Two refinements make the result usable: the noise on a difference of two regions, and the fact that a defect usually covers many pixels which can be averaged.

Detection condition: ΔS ≥ k · σ_total / √n where n = number of pixels the defect covers (independent samples) Divide through by S to get contrast, C = ΔS/S: C_min = k · √(S + σ_r²) / ( S · √n ) and in the shot-noise limit, the memorable form: C_min ≈ k / √(n · S) Worked, at 60 % full well (S = 6,300 e⁻, σ_r = 2.5, k = 5): n = 1 px C_min = 6.30 % a single-pixel speck must be very dark n = 64 px C_min = 0.788 % an 8×8 defect: sub-1 % contrast is fine n = 400 px C_min = 0.315 % And at 10 % full well (S = 1,050 e⁻), same 64 px defect: n = 64 px C_min = 1.935 % — 2.5× worse, purely from light

Three consequences worth carrying. First, bigger defects are easier in proportion to the square root of their area, which is why the hardest inspection tasks are always the small ones and why the specification must state the smallest defect, not the typical one. Second, contrast requirements and light requirements are the same requirement: an under-lit station does not fail loudly, it fails by quietly raising the size of the smallest defect it can find. Third, gain does not appear anywhere in this equation — multiplying signal and noise by four leaves C_min exactly where it was.

For anything shiny, budget the light for it and fit polarisers from day one.

ZHANG'S METHOD, AND HOW TO DO IT PROPERLY CORE

The standard planar calibration takes many views of a known planar target — a checkerboard, a dot grid, or a coded target — and recovers intrinsics, distortion and the pose of each view simultaneously by non-linear optimisation. It is built into every vision library. Doing it well is a discipline, and the following list is the difference between a 0.05 px residual and a 0.4 px residual:

  • 1The target must be genuinely flat and its pitch genuinely known. A checkerboard printed on an office printer and taped to cardboard will give you a beautifully converged calibration of the wrong camera. Buy a glass or ceramic target with a certified pitch, or accept that your scale is uncertain by whatever your printer is.
  • 2Fill the frame, including the corners. Distortion coefficients are estimated almost entirely from the periphery; a set of views clustered in the centre leaves k₁ essentially unconstrained, and the software will still report success.
  • 3Tilt the target substantially — 30° to 45° — in several directions. Views that are all fronto-parallel cannot separate focal length from distance. This is the single commonest cause of a calibration that looks perfect and measures badly.
  • 4Ten to twenty views, and inspect the per-view reprojection error. Delete the outlier views and re-solve; a blurred or clipped view poisons the whole estimate.
  • 5Calibrate at the working aperture, working distance and working wavelength. Focal length changes slightly with focus, distortion changes with aperture, and refractive index changes with wavelength. A calibration done at f/8 in white light does not describe a station running at f/4 under 660 nm.
  • 6Record the RMS reprojection error in the file. Below 0.1 px is good; above 0.3 px, something in this list was skipped.
  • 7Re-calibrate after any mechanical or optical touch — refocusing, changing the aperture, loosening the lens, bumping the bracket. Fit lock screws and, on a machine that vibrates, put a witness mark across the lens/mount joint so a shift is visible.
FLAT-FIELD CORRECTION: THE OTHER CALIBRATION CORE

Geometric calibration fixes where. Radiometric — flat-field — calibration fixes how bright, and it is skipped far more often than it should be. Illumination is never uniform, lenses vignette (the classical cos⁴ falloff plus mechanical vignetting), and pixels differ in gain and offset.

Two-point correction, per pixel: I_corrected(x,y) = ( I_raw(x,y) - Dark(x,y) ) × G(x,y) Dark(x,y) = average of many frames with the lens capped (offset, DSNU) G(x,y) = mean(Bright - Dark) / (Bright(x,y) - Dark(x,y)) (gain, PRNU plus vignetting plus illumination non-uniformity, all at once) Why it matters for thresholding: a station with 25 % corner falloff has a grey level at the edge of the frame that is 25 % below the centre. Any fixed global threshold is therefore a different threshold at every position in the image — and the defect that passes in the middle fails at the corner, intermittently, forever.

Flat-field correction takes ten minutes at commissioning, needs a uniform white or grey reference in the object plane, and removes an entire class of position-dependent instability. It also gives you a maintenance metric for free: re-capture the bright reference every quarter and compare the mean — a falling mean is a dirty diffuser or an ageing LED, quantified, months before it causes a failure.

📏

A useful acceptance test to write into a vision specification: “place the certified artefact at nine positions across the field of view and at three heights spanning the depth of field; report the measured value at all twenty-seven positions.” A system that has been calibrated properly shows scatter. A system that has been calibrated at one point shows a trend — and the trend is exactly the perspective and distortion error you are about to inherit.

THE FOUR-STAGE STRUCTURE THAT NEVER CHANGES CORE

STAGE 3 — MEASURE: EDGES ARE THE ONLY PRIMITIVE THAT MATTERS CORE

Nearly every industrial measurement reduces to locating edges and fitting geometry to them. The classical caliper tool projects the image along a search line, differentiates, and finds the peak of the gradient; the sub-pixel refinement that follows is the subject of page 10.

  • 1Project before you differentiate. Averaging the grey levels along the width of the caliper before taking the derivative is what buys the noise immunity — a caliper 40 px wide has a sixth of the noise of one 1 px wide.
  • 2Fit to many points, not two. A diameter from two opposed edge points is at the mercy of two pixels; a circle least-squares-fitted to 200 edge points along the rim is about ten times more repeatable (√(200/2)) and tells you the residual, which is itself a defect detector.
  • 3Always look at the fit residual. A circle fit with an RMS residual of 0.3 px is a circle; one with 2.1 px is a dent, and the residual found it without any extra tool.
  • 4Use robust fitting (RANSAC or a re-weighted least squares) whenever a stray edge point is possible. One outlier at the end of a line drags an ordinary least-squares fit by an amount nobody notices.
  • 5Measure the thing the drawing specifies. Maximum inscribed, minimum circumscribed and least-squares diameters are three different numbers on the same rim — and GD&T says which one applies (A21).

Subpixel Metrology and the Question Nobody Asks: Is the Gauge Capable?

A VISION MEASUREMENT IS A GAUGE, AND GAUGES HAVE RULES CORE

The moment a vision system reports a number in millimetres rather than a verdict, it has become a measuring instrument, and it inherits every obligation of one: a stated uncertainty, a demonstrated repeatability and reproducibility, a traceable reference, and a documented relationship to the tolerance it is policing. Very few industrial vision installations can produce any of these, which is why so many of them are quietly distrusted by the quality department.

SUBPIXEL EDGE LOCATION: HOW IT IS POSSIBLE AT ALL CORE

A pixel is not a point sample of a point; it is the integral of the scene over a finite area. When an edge crosses a pixel, that pixel's grey level is a weighted average of the two sides — and the weight is the fraction of the pixel the bright side covers. That fraction is a continuous quantity, so the edge position is encoded in the grey level, not merely in which pixel it fell into.

Three standard estimators, in ascending order of quality: 1. Linear interpolation of the crossing. Between the two pixels straddling the mid-grey level, interpolate: x_sub = x₁ + (T - I₁) / (I₂ - I₁) 2. Centre of mass of the gradient. Differentiate along the caliper, then take the intensity-weighted centroid of the gradient peak: x_sub = Σ xᵢ · gᵢ / Σ gᵢ 3. Parabola fit to the gradient peak — three points around the maximum: x_sub = x₀ + ½ · (g₋₁ - g₊₁) / (g₋₁ - 2g₀ + g₊₁) All three cost microseconds. All three are worth about a factor of 10 to 50 in resolution over integer-pixel edge detection.

Subpixel estimation. The grey level of the transition pixel is a linear function of where the edge sits inside it — which is why a hundredth of a pixel is achievable, and why blur that is not symmetric biases it.

THE HONEST NUMBER: NOISE IS NOT THE LIMIT CORE

It is tempting to derive the achievable repeatability from the SNR of page 7 and stop there. Do the arithmetic and you get a startling answer — and then you must be honest about why it is wrong.

Random-noise floor for an edge fitted over n profiles: σ_edge ≈ w / ( SNR · √n ) w = edge transition width, in px w = 2 px, SNR = 72, n = 50 profiles along the caliper: σ_edge ≈ 2 / (72 × 7.07) = 0.0039 px Measured repeatability of a real, well-built station, same optics: 0.02 – 0.10 px The gap is a factor of 5 to 25, and all of it is systematic:

Residual distortion

What the calibration did not model — typically 0.02–0.1 px after a good Zhang solve, more at the field edge

Thermal drift

An aluminium bracket 200 mm long moves 4.6 µm per °C; the lens focal length drifts too. A 10 °C day-night swing is real

Part presentation

Tilt, height and rotation variation, each converting into apparent size through perspective

Illumination asymmetry

A light stronger on one side biases the gradient peak — a fixed offset that looks like a real dimension

This is the central practical lesson of vision metrology. Beyond a very modest SNR, more light and better cameras stop improving the measurement, because the measurement is no longer noise-limited. What improves it after that point is mechanical stability, thermal control, calibration quality and presentation repeatability — none of which appear in a camera catalogue. Engineers who do not know this buy a 20 megapixel camera to fix a bracket problem.

THE CAPABILITY CRITERION, DERIVED PROPERLY CORE

Now the question that decides whether the system may be used at all. Measurement systems analysis (see A27) requires the gauge's variation to be small against the tolerance it polices. The standard criterion, in the %GRR form:

%GRR = 6σ_gauge / T × 100 T = total tolerance band ≤ 10 % capable 10 – 30 % conditionally acceptable, with justification > 30 % not capable — do not use for acceptance For a two-edge dimension, the two edge errors add in quadrature: σ_mm = √2 · σ_px · s_px Substituting and solving for the coarsest resolution that still passes: 6 · √2 · σ_px · s_px ≤ 0.10 · T s_px ≤ T / ( 60 √2 · σ_px ) Worked, for a ±0.25 mm tolerance (T = 0.50 mm) and a realistic, systematically-limited σ_px = 0.05 px: s_px ≤ 0.50 / (60 × 1.4142 × 0.05) = 0.118 mm/px Compare the folklore rule — “one pixel = one tenth of tolerance”: s_px ≤ 0.050 mm/px — 2.4× more pixels than the physics requires

This result has real money in it. On a 300 mm-wide product, the folklore rule demands 6,000 px across the field and a 12 MP+ camera; the derived criterion is satisfied by 2,542 px and a 5 MP camera. But the derived criterion is only valid if you can demonstrate σ_px = 0.05 — which means a real gauge study on the real station, not a claim. That is the trade: folklore is safe and expensive; the derivation is cheap and obliges you to measure.

Even a capable gauge makes mistakes at the limit, because the measured value is the true value plus an error. Deep Learning Vision: What It Is Genuinely For, and the Statistics That Catch People Out

THE HONEST BOUNDARY CORE

THE FOUR ARCHITECTURES YOU WILL ACTUALLY MEET CORE

Classification

One label per image or per ROI: good / chipped / stained. Needs balanced examples of every class. Fastest to train, least informative — it tells you what, never where.

Object detection

Boxes plus labels. Counting, presence of multiple items, locating components. Annotation cost is moderate; class imbalance still bites.

Segmentation

Per-pixel labelling. The right choice when the size or area of the defect decides the verdict, because it produces a measurable region you can threshold on mm².

Anomaly detection

Trained on good parts only; flags whatever does not fit. The most industrially useful family, because collecting a thousand good parts is easy and collecting a thousand of each defect is usually impossible.

Anomaly detection deserves the emphasis. The fundamental problem with supervised defect classification on a real line is that a good line does not produce defects — that is what makes it a good line. You will wait months for forty examples of a rare failure, and forty examples will not train a classifier. Approaches that model the distribution of normal appearance and score deviation from it invert this: they are trained on the abundant class, they detect defect types nobody has seen yet, and they degrade gracefully. The price is that the score is a continuous novelty measure with no natural units, so the threshold becomes an engineering decision that must be set from data and re-validated.

The two distributions overlap; the threshold slides the errors from one type to the other. Accuracy is meaningless here — only the two error rates, priced, mean anything.

Model architecture is a weekend. Data is the project, and it is where deep-learning vision installations succeed or quietly fail. Five realities worth budgeting for:

  • 1Label noise sets the ceiling. If two experienced inspectors disagree on 8 % of borderline parts — and they will — no model trained on their labels can be more consistent than that.   • 2Split by production batch, never randomly. A random split puts images of the same physical part in train and test, and reports a validation score that has nothing to do with next week. Split by shift, batch, roll or mould cavity.
  • 3Freeze the optics before you collect a single image. Every image is a sample from a distribution defined by the lighting, lens, aperture, exposure and working distance. Change any of them afterwards and the training set describes a camera that no longer exists.
  • 4Augment only in ways the physics allows.   • 5Plan for drift from day one. A new supplier's substrate, a re-tooled mould, an ageing LED, a seasonal change in the material — all shift the input distribution. Height-to-image sensitivity, for a triangulation angle α between the laser and the camera axis: Δx_image = m · Δz · sinα so the height resolution follows directly from the lateral resolution: σ_z = σ_x_object / sinα = (σ_px · s_px) / sinα Worked, for a seal-bead profiler: s_px = 0.040 mm/px, subpixel line-centre σ_px = 0.05 px, α = 30° σ_z = (0.05 × 0.040) / 0.5 = 0.0040 mm = 4.0 µm Raise α to 45°: σ_z = 0.002/0.7071 = 2.83 µm (41 % better) Raise α to 60°: σ_z = 0.002/0.8660 = 2.31 µm The trade: sensitivity rises with sinα, but so does occlusion — at 60° a 3 mm step casts a 3·tan(30°) = 1.73 mm shadow in which no data exists at all. Steep angles measure beautifully and see nothing.

Laser line finding is itself a subpixel problem: the line is a Gaussian intensity profile several pixels wide, and its centre is estimated by centre-of-gravity or Gaussian fitting along each column, exactly as on page 10. This is why triangulation achieves micrometres from a camera with 40 µm pixels — the accuracy comes from the subpixel estimate divided by sinα, not from the pixel size.

Triangulation. Sensitivity and occlusion are the same parameter viewed from two sides — which is why profilers are specified by both.

STEREO, AND WHY DEPTH ACCURACY DEGRADES WITH THE SQUARE OF DISTANCE CORE
Disparity for a baseline b, focal length f (in pixels), at range Z: d = b · f / Z → Z = b · f / d Differentiate to get the depth uncertainty from disparity uncertainty: σ_Z = Z² · σ_d / (b · f) Worked, for a bin-picking head: b = 120 mm, f = 1,600 px, σ_d = 0.2 px Z = 600 mm σ_Z = 360000 × 0.2 / 192000 = 0.375 mm Z = 1200 mm σ_Z = 1440000 × 0.2 / 192000 = 1.50 mm Z = 2000 mm σ_Z = 4000000 × 0.2 / 192000 = 4.17 mm Doubling the distance quadruples the depth error. Doubling the baseline halves it — at the cost of more occlusion and a bigger head.

The other structural weakness of stereo is that disparity requires correspondence, and correspondence requires texture. A blank white moulding, a machined face, a clean sheet of steel: all give a stereo system nothing to match, and the point cloud develops holes exactly where the surface is most uniform. The standard fix is active stereo — project a random dot pattern with an IR projector and let the algorithm match the projected texture instead of the absent surface texture. It is cheap, effective, and turns stereo from a laboratory technique into an industrial one.

STRUCTURED LIGHT AND TIME OF FLIGHT, BRIEFLY STRETCH

Structured light projects a sequence of patterns — Gray codes for unambiguous coarse position, then sinusoidal fringes shifted in phase for fine interpolation — and decodes each pixel independently.

Phase-shifting, N steps: Iᵢ = A + B·cos(φ + 2πi/N) φ = arctan( Σ Iᵢ sin(2πi/N) / Σ Iᵢ cos(2πi/N) ) Phase resolves to a small fraction of a fringe, so a 2 mm fringe pitch with 1/100 phase resolution gives 20 µm of height — but N patterns must be captured, so the part must not move by more than a fraction of a fringe during the whole sequence. At 4 patterns and 5 ms each, a part moving 600 mm/s travels 12 mm — the method is disqualified. Structured light is for stopped parts, indexed stations, or robot-mounted scanning where the head stops to scan.

Time of flight measures the round trip of modulated light. Indirect ToF recovers the phase of an amplitude-modulated signal, which sets both the resolution and the ambiguity range:

Z = c · Δφ / (4π f_mod) ambiguity range Z_amb = c / (2 f_mod) f_mod = 20 MHz → Z_amb = 3e8 / 4e7 = 7.5 m f_mod = 100 MHz → Z_amb = 1.5 m, better resolution, shorter range Beyond the ambiguity range the reading wraps — a 9 m object reads as 1.5 m. Dual-frequency modulation resolves the wrap; if a datasheet does not mention it, ask.

ToF's characteristic industrial artefact is multipath: light that reaches the sensor after bouncing off two surfaces arrives late and biases the reading, so inside corners read further away than they are, and shiny concave shapes are systematically wrong. It is excellent for presence, volume estimation, pallet dimensioning and safety-volume monitoring, and it is the wrong tool for a dimension on a drawing.

CHOOSING, IN ONE PASS CORE
  • 1Is the part moving? If yes, laser triangulation (line rate slaved to an encoder) or stereo. Structured light needs it still.
  • 2What z accuracy does the drawing demand? Micrometres → triangulation. Tenths of a millimetre → structured light or short-range stereo. Millimetres → stereo or ToF.
  • 3Is the surface shiny, dark or translucent? All three defeat triangulation and structured light differently: specular returns saturate or vanish, translucent surfaces let the laser sink in and shift the apparent height by hundreds of micrometres. Test on the real material before quoting — never on a white 3D-printed mock-up.
  • 4Are there deep features? Compute the occlusion shadow, depth × tan(90°−α), before choosing the angle, and consider a second head from the opposite side.
  • 5Do you need 3D at all? A great many height problems are solved more cheaply by a shadow, a backlight from the side, a laser distance sensor (A11) or a mechanical gauge. 3D is powerful and it is never the cheap option.

Triggering, Latency and the Reject: A Correct Verdict on the Wrong Bottle

THE LAST BLOCK IN THE CHAIN, AND THE ONE MOST OFTEN LEFT TO CHANCE CORE

A vision system does not produce images; it produces an action applied to a specific physical object. Between the photon and the air blast lie a trigger sensor, an exposure, a readout, a processing time, a communications link, a PLC scan and a valve — and every one of them contributes both a delay and a variation in that delay. Delay you can compensate. Variation you cannot, and it is what puts the reject on the neighbouring bottle.

This is worth stating plainly because it inverts the intuition: the mechanical parts of the chain are the well-behaved ones. A valve's pull-in time varies by a few percent; a vision program's execution time can double when a second blob appears in the image, and a PLC scan contributes a full scan period of pure phase uncertainty simply because the result arrived at an arbitrary moment within it.

Processing jitter

Data-dependent execution. Bound it: fix the number of tools, cap iterative searches, and time out to a defined verdict rather than running long.

Scan phase

Up to one full scan of uncertainty. Cured by an interrupt/event input or by timestamping, not by a faster PLC.

Network

A cyclic fieldbus with a bounded update time is predictable; a TCP socket is not. See A02 and A37.

TIME-BASED VERSUS POSITION-BASED REJECT: THE CHOICE THAT DECIDES ROBUSTNESS CORE

Having measured the lag, you must deliver the verdict to the right place. There are two architectures, and one of them fails the first time anyone changes a setting on the conveyor drive.

TIME-BASED (a delay timer): t_fire = d / v_assumed Line 3: d = 1,800 mm from camera to rejector, v = 600 mm/s t_fire = 3.000 s — set once, at commissioning A changeover trims the conveyor to 520 mm/s. The timer is not updated. In 3.000 s the bottle now travels 520 × 3.0 = 1,560 mm. position error = 1,800 - 1,560 = 240 mm in pitches = 240 / 180 = 1.33 bottles The blast now hits the bottle one or two places ahead of the defective one. A good bottle is destroyed and the bad one ships — the worst possible pair of outcomes, produced by a 13 % speed change. POSITION-BASED (encoder shift register): Encoder on the conveyor: 1,024 ppr on a Ø100 mm wheel resolution = π × 100 / 1024 = 0.3068 mm per count d = 1,800 mm → 5,867 counts. Note what the encoder architecture does: it converts a timing problem into a counting problem, and counting is immune to speed. This is exactly the same idea as encoder-locked line-scan triggering on page 6, and it is the standard solution everywhere in converting, printing and packaging. Any reject that must land on a specific object should be position-based. The extra cost is one encoder and one high-speed counter input.

Recap, Six Failure Modes, and the Quiz

EIGHT IDEAS THAT CARRY THE MODULE CORE
  • 1The illuminated scene is the sensor. Contrast that lighting did not create cannot be recovered by any algorithm, classical or learned. Work the six geometries in order of contrast, not convenience.
  • 2The sizing chain is deterministic. Smallest feature → pixels across it → s_px → FOV → m = p/s_px → focal length. Start anywhere else and you build the Line 3 date-code station.
  • 3Depth of field is bought with resolution: DOF_max ≈ 3.28 s_px²/λ. Pixel size, sensor format and lens price all cancel. If the number is smaller than the part's presentation variation, it is a mechanical problem.
  • 4Motion damages images two different ways. Blur = v·t_exp/s_px, cured by a shorter exposure. Shear = arctan(v·t_row/s_px), not cured by exposure, independent of ROI, worse when you zoom in.
  • 5Signal multiplies, noise takes a square root. SNR ≈ √S, and the smallest defect you can find is C_min ≈ k/√(nS). Gain appears in neither — gain is a display control.
  • 6Beyond a modest SNR, measurement is systematic-limited, not noise-limited. The noise floor is 0.003 px; real stations achieve 0.02–0.10 px, and the gap is brackets, temperature, calibration and presentation.
  • 7A number in millimetres is a gauge. s_px ≤ T/(60√2 σ_px) for a capable two-edge width — and you must have demonstrated σ_px with a real study before using it.
  • 8The verdict must land on the right object. Delay is compensable, jitter is not, and speed-independence comes from encoder counts, never from a timer.
SIX FAILURE MODES, AND HOW EACH ANNOUNCES ITSELF CORE
⚠ Under-resolution

Symptom: intermittent failures with no time pattern; blamed on the printer, the supplier or the operator. Mechanism: fewer than ~3 px across the feature, so success depends on the random phase of the part on the pixel grid. Find it: measure the feature in pixels — one screenshot and a ruler tool. Fix: smaller FOV, more pixels, or more cameras. Nothing else.

⚠ Motion blur from a “brightness” fix

Symptom: image looks brighter and healthier after someone raised the exposure; edge measurements get worse and codes stop reading. Mechanism: b_px = v·t/s_px grew past the feature size. Find it: compute it; or strobe once and compare. Fix: strobe, open the aperture to the diffraction ceiling, add light.

⚠ Rolling-shutter shear

Symptom: a constant geometric offset that someone once “calibrated out”; asymmetric detection — defects caught in one direction only. Mechanism: row-sequential exposure on a moving part. Find it: stop the line and re-measure — the offset vanishes. Fix: global shutter. Not a shorter exposure.

⚠ Ambient light drift

Symptom: a reject rate with a daily and seasonal rhythm; an HMI threshold that has been edited eleven times. Mechanism: a fixed threshold on a moving pedestal. Find it: one frame with the illuminator switched off. Fix: shroud + monochromatic strobe + matched bandpass. Permanent.

⚠ Stopped down past the diffraction ceiling

Symptom: more depth of field was requested; small features quietly became unreadable; gain was raised to compensate and noise arrived too. Mechanism: Airy disc > 2 px. Find it: compute 2.44λN(1+m)/p. Fix: hold the part better; do not buy depth with resolution.

⚠ Right verdict, wrong part

Symptom: good parts in the reject bin and bad parts in the box, in roughly equal numbers, starting after a changeover. Mechanism: a time-based reject delay and a conveyor speed that changed. Find it: compare trigger, verdict and reject counters. Fix: encoder-clocked tracking.

QUIZ 1 — THE SIZING CHAIN
A 5 MP camera (2448 px wide) images a 306 mm field. You must detect a 0.4 mm scratch. Does it work?
Tap to reveal Marginally, and not for measurement. s_px = 306/2448 = 0.125 mm/px, so the scratch is 3.2 px wide — just over the practical floor of 3 px for detection. It will be found, unreliably at its ends — but its width cannot be measured at all, because at 3.2 px the two edge profiles of the scratch overlap and subpixel estimation has no clean transition to fit. If the specification says “detect scratches ≥ 0.4 mm”, halve the field of view and stop arguing; if it says “measure scratch width”, this is a different project. Also note the scratch's length helps you: a long thin blob integrates along its length, so a 20 mm-long scratch at 3.2 px wide is far more reliably found than a 0.4 mm round pit at the same width.
QUIZ 2 — THE SQUEEZE
A customer specifies 25 µm/px and tells you the parts sit in a vibrating vee-block with about 2 mm of height variation. What do you say, and what do you say next?
Tap to reveal “At 25 µm/px and 660 nm, the maximum depth of field is 3.28 × 0.025²/0.00066 = 3.1 mm, and that is with an aggressive two-pixel circle of confusion. Your 2 mm eats two-thirds of it before any other error.” Then the second sentence, which is the valuable one: “so this is a fixturing project with a camera attached, not a camera project. QUIZ 3 — SHEAR VERSUS BLUR
A cap-inspection station on a rolling-shutter camera reports a consistent 3.8° tilt on every bottle, including bottles you have verified as perfectly square. The integrator proposes reducing the exposure from 1 ms to 200 µs. Will it help?
Tap to reveal No — unless the sensor supports global reset release and the enclosure is genuinely dark. Shear comes from the row-to-row readout delay t_row, which is set by the frame readout time and the row count, not by the exposure. Shortening the exposure reduces blur (which is a separate, additive defect) and leaves the shear exactly where it was. The diagnostic that settles it in two minutes: stop the conveyor and re-measure. If the 3.8° disappears with the line stopped, it is shear, not a mechanical tilt and not a calibration error. The correct fixes are a global-shutter camera, a faster readout (smaller ROI does reduce total readout time and therefore t_row, if the sensor supports row-windowing), a slower line, or global reset release with a strobe in a dark enclosure.
QUIZ 4 — DETECTABILITY
You must find a stain of 1.5 % contrast covering 400 px, at k = 5. Your sensor has a 10,500 e⁻ full well. What exposure level do you need, and what happens if the auto-exposure drops it to 15 %?
Tap to reveal You need S ≥ k²/(n·C²) = 25/(400 × 0.000225) = 278 e⁻ — only 2.6 % of full well, because 400 pixels of averaging is worth a factor of 20 in noise. This defect is comfortably detectable even in a dim image, which is the useful half of the lesson. At 15 % of full well (S = 1,575 e⁻) the achievable C_min is 5 × √(1575+6.25)/(1575 × 20) = 0.63 %, so you have 2.4× margin — fine. Now change one thing: make the stain 16 px instead of 400. C_min becomes 3.15 %, the 1.5 % stain is undetectable, and no threshold tuning will retrieve it. The specification must state the smallest defect, and area matters as much as contrast.
QUIZ 5 — CAPABILITY
A customer wants a ±0.15 mm width measured over a 240 mm field with a 5 MP camera, and your demonstrated edge repeatability is 0.06 px. Capable?
Tap to reveal No — 16.6 %, and the trap is that it feels capable. s_px = 240/2448 = 0.09804 mm/px. For a two-edge width, σ_mm = √2 × 0.06 × 0.09804 = 0.008319 mm. %GRR = 6 × 0.008319/0.30 = 16.6 % — conditionally acceptable under MSA, but not capable, and not something to accept without saying so in writing. QUIZ 6 — THE SYSTEM QUESTION
A vision station has been running for two years. Yield reports show a slow rise in false rejects, about 0.02 % per month, with no step changes. Name the three most likely causes and the one measurement that distinguishes them.
Tap to reveal Causes: (1) LED output decay — illuminators lose output steadily and a strobed, overdriven one loses it faster; (2) contamination — dust or aerosol film on the diffuser, filter or window, which is optically identical to LED decay; (3) genuine process drift — the product really is changing, and the vision system is correctly reporting it. The distinguishing measurement is the flat-field bright reference from page 8, captured against a fixed white artefact. If its mean has fallen, the loss is optical (1 or 2) — clean first, and if cleaning does not restore it, the LED has aged. This one measurement, trended quarterly, converts a two-year mystery into a five-minute answer, and it is why page 7's commissioning baseline is worth insisting on in the purchase order.

WE3 — A LIGHTING CHOICE THAT SAVED A PROJECT CORE

Situation. A Ja-Ela plant moulds black ABS housings and must detect short-shot and flash defects at the parting line, plus surface scratches. The first attempt used a white LED dome and a 5 MP colour camera. Measured feature contrast: 4 grey levels on black-on-black, at about 22 % of full well. A deep-learning proposal followed, at LKR 3.1 million.

First, is 4 grey levels even detectable? Sensor 10,500 e⁻ full well, σ_r = 2.5, defect area ≈ 250 px, k = 5, signal at 22 % of full well: S = 2,310 e⁻ C_available = 4/255 = 1.57 % C_min = 5√(2310+6.25)/(2310√250) = 5 × 48.13/(2310 × 15.81) = 240.7/36,524 = 0.659 % Margin = 1.57/0.659 = 2.4× — detectable in principle, but on a contrast that any drift, any gloss variation and any mould-release film will destroy. This is why the classical attempt was “unstable”. Now change the physics instead of the algorithm: (a) flash and short shot at the parting line are silhouette features → backlight from behind the parting plane. Contrast rises from 4 grey levels to >200, a factor of 50. (b) scratches on black gloss are slope discontinuities → dark-field ring at grazing incidence, blue 450 nm. Contrast measured after the change: 78 grey levels, a factor of 20. New C_available/C_min at 60 % full well, 250 px: (a) 78/255 = 30.6 % vs C_min 0.399 % → margin 77× (b) same order

Outcome. Two lighting changes — a backlight and a blue dark-field ring, together about LKR 180,000 — converted a 2.4×-margin problem needing a learned model into a 77×-margin problem solved by a threshold and a blob filter. The learned model was not wrong; it was an expensive way of extracting information that the illumination had thrown away, and it would have needed re-training every time the mould-release regime changed.

The honest caveat, because this example is easy to over-read: cosmetic grading of the same housings — “is this swirl mark acceptable to the customer?” — is genuinely tacit and remains a legitimate deep-learning task. The lesson is not “never use AI”; it is “never use AI to compensate for illumination you did not design, and always test the lighting alternative first, because it is cheaper and it settles the question in a day”.

The resolution. The two stations with the heavy tails — 2 and 5, with maxima of 96 and 121 ms against means of 28 and 44 — are not Gaussian. Those retries are not independent; both are triggered by the same upstream cause (a poorly printed or mis-positioned part), so they arrive together.   • 2Bound the tails rather than averaging them. Cap the retry count and time out to a defined verdict — a bounded 60 ms answer beats an unbounded 121 ms correct one on a 300 ms takt.
  • 3Look for common causes before assuming independence. Quadrature addition of variances is only valid for independent terms, and in an inspection cell the terms are usually driven by the same bad part.
  • 4Fix the input, not the timing. Both slow paths here are triggered by print quality — step 7 of page 14 again.
WE5 — THE FORENSIC: A MEASUREMENT THAT DRIFTS WITH THE WEATHER STRETCH

Situation. A vision gauge measuring a 62.00 mm aluminium extrusion width, tolerance ±0.15 mm, has been in service eight months. SPC charts show the measured mean rising by about 0.05 mm between 06:00 and 14:00 every day, then falling overnight. The parts, checked on a CMM, are stable. Station: 5 MP camera, FOV 96 mm, s_px = 0.03922 mm/px, aluminium bracket 220 mm from mount to lens axis, hall temperature swinging 24 °C to 34 °C.

Candidate 1 — the PART is expanding. Aluminium α = 23.1e-6 /°C. ΔL = 62.00 × 23.1e-6 × 10 = 0.0143 mm Real, but only 29 % of the observed 0.05 mm. Not the whole story. (And note: the CMM sees the part cold, so this term is real and the drawing should state a reference temperature — ISO 1 says 20 °C.) Candidate 2 — the BRACKET is expanding, changing the working distance. ΔWD = 220 × 23.1e-6 × 10 = 0.0508 mm over 10 °C Scale error from a working-distance change: Δs/s = ΔWD/WD = 0.0508 / 350 = 1.45e-4 Effect on a 62 mm measurement: 62 × 1.45e-4 = 0.0090 mm Real, and smaller still. Candidate 3 — the LENS. Focal length drifts with temperature; a typical fixed lens has df/f ≈ 2 to 6e-5 per °C. Take 4e-5: Δm/m = -Δf/f (at fixed WD, to first order) = 4e-4 over 10 °C Effect on 62 mm: 62 × 4e-4 = 0.0248 mm The largest single term — and the one nobody suspects. Sum of the three: 0.0143 + 0.0090 + 0.0248 = 0.0481 mm Observed: 0.050 mm. ✓ The mechanism is thermal, in three parts.

What to do about it, in order of cost. First, a reference artefact in the field of view: a hard-anodised gauge of known length imaged in the same frame as the part. Every measurement is then reported as a ratio to the artefact, and every thermal term above — lens, bracket, working distance — cancels to first order. This costs almost nothing and is the single most powerful trick in vision metrology. Second, state the reference temperature on the drawing and correct the part's own expansion (that term does not cancel, because the artefact and the part may differ in material). Third, if the residual still matters, control the temperature or use an invar bracket.

Transferable lesson. The observed drift was 0.05 mm against a 0.30 mm tolerance — a sixth of the tolerance consumed by a mechanism that never appears in a camera datasheet. When a vision measurement drifts on a daily cycle, it is thermal until proven otherwise, and the fix is a reference in the frame, not a better camera.

Station B — task (iii), the proud flap. A 3 mm height threshold does not need a profiler. Marks are lost for: one camera for all three tasks; no filter or shroud with skylights present; no strobe; a time-based reject; and quoting the tax-band tolerance without checking that ±2 mm at s_px 0.0518 is 38.6 px — comfortably measurable, but only if the carton is located first, since the tolerance is relative to the carton, not to the image.
P10 — THREE REQUESTS, AT LEAST ONE REFUSAL [8 marks]

You are the engineer on site. Respond to each, with reasoning.

(a) Production asks you to raise the gain from 1× to 6× because the night shift says the image is dark. (b) The quality manager asks you to use the vision gauge's readings as the official record for a ±0.04 mm dimension; your demonstrated %GRR on that feature is 34 %. (c) Maintenance asks you to move the reject nozzle 600 mm downstream to make room for a new conveyor motor, keeping the existing time-based delay and “just changing the number”.

Tap for the solution (a) Refuse, and diagnose instead. Gain multiplies signal and noise equally: SNR and C_min are unchanged, so nothing detectable becomes detectable. Above unity gain you also compress dynamic range and may clip. The night shift's observation is real and valuable — it means the illumination has changed between shifts, which points at ambient contribution, an LED that is dimming when the hall is cooler, or a cleaning regime that differs by shift. Capture one frame with the illuminator off on both shifts and compare; that is the answer, and it takes five minutes. (b) Refuse, in writing, with a number and a path. 34 % GRR means the gauge's own variation consumes a third of the tolerance; using it for acceptance would generate both false rejects and false accepts at rates the customer has not agreed to. Offer: (i) the measurement remains available for process monitoring and trending, where it is genuinely useful; (ii) here is what it costs to reach ≤ 10 % — halve the field of view, fit geometry rather than two edges, add a reference artefact in frame for thermal cancellation; (iii) meanwhile the official record stays with the existing method. Never let a non-capable gauge become the record because it is convenient. (c) Conditionally accept, and use the opportunity. Moving the nozzle is fine; keeping the timer is not. Point out that the delay must now be 600 mm longer at the commissioned speed only, so the existing speed-sensitivity gets proportionally worse (a fixed percentage speed error now produces a larger absolute position error, because the tracked distance grew). This is the cheapest moment in the machine's life to fit the encoder — the wiring is open, the guard is off, and the change is already being validated. Quote it as part of the same job.

Four Derivations

WHY DERIVE ANYTHING CORE

A formula you can only quote is a formula you cannot adapt. Each of these four is derived from its physical starting point, with the assumptions made visible, so that when a real problem violates one of them you know which term to change rather than which vendor to call. Two of the four produce results that are not in the usual textbooks — D2's cancellation and D4's independence — and both settle arguments that otherwise run for weeks.

D1 — THE SIZING CHAIN, EXACTLY CORE
Start: Gaussian thin-lens equation and the definition of magnification. 1/a + 1/b = 1/f m = b/a (a object, b image distance) Step 1 — express a and b in terms of f and m. b = m·a, substitute: 1/a + 1/(m a) = 1/f (m + 1)/(m a) = 1/f a = f(1+m)/m b = f(1+m) Step 2 — magnification from the sensor and the field. The sensor width h_s images the field FOV, so m = h_s / FOV Step 3 — focal length from the allowed working distance. Working distance WD is a (to the object-side principal plane), so f = a·m/(1+m) = WD · (h_s/FOV) / (1 + h_s/FOV) f = WD · h_s / (FOV + h_s) (exact) For FOV ≫ h_s this reduces to the familiar f ≈ WD·h_s/FOV, with a fractional error of exactly h_s/FOV = m. So the shortcut's error IS the magnification — 6 % at m = 0.06, 33 % at m = 0.33. Step 4 — the second face of m. The sensor has N_px pixels of pitch p across, so h_s = N_px · p, and s_px = FOV/N_px. Divide: m = h_s/FOV = (N_px p)/(N_px s_px) m = p / s_px This is the identity that makes D2 collapse. It says magnification is nothing but the ratio of pixel pitch to the resolution it delivers, and it lets you move between optical and pixel language in one step. Step 5 — the feature criterion, and the field it permits. Require k pixels across the smallest feature d: s_px ≤ d/k and FOV = N_px · s_px FOV_max = N_px · d / k Line 3: N_px = 2448, d = 0.30 mm, k = 3 → FOV_max = 244.8 mm The station was built at 320 mm. It was 31 % outside a bound that takes one multiplication to compute, and no one computed it. Assumptions made visible: thin lens (no principal-plane separation — real lenses have one, so WD from the front element differs from a by the lens's hiatus, given in the datasheet); paraxial; no distortion; square pixels; the feature is aligned with a pixel axis (a diagonal feature is sampled √2 more coarsely in the worst case, which is a real 41 % penalty worth adding to k for diagonal strokes).
D2 — THE OPTICAL SQUEEZE: DOF_max = 3.28 s_px²/λ CORE
Start: two independent pieces of physics that both scale with N. Part A — depth of field. A point at defocus Δz images as a blur circle. For a lens of aperture diameter D = f/N at magnification m, geometry gives the standard result for the total depth over which the blur stays under c: DOF = 2 N c (1+m) / m² (the (1+m) is the pupil-magnification correction; it is negligible at small m and matters above m ≈ 0.2, so we carry it.) Part B — diffraction. A circular aperture forms an Airy pattern whose first zero is at angular radius 1.22λ/D. Referred to the image plane and expressed as a diameter, with the (1+m) correction for finite conjugates: d_Airy = 2.44 λ N (1+m) Step 1 — the ceiling. Sampling theory says a blur spot smaller than two pixels cannot be represented anyway; a blur spot larger than two pixels is destroying information the sensor could have carried. Set d_Airy = 2p: 2p = 2.44 λ N_max (1+m) N_max = 2p / (2.44 λ (1+m)) Step 2 — substitute the ceiling into the depth of field. DOF_max = 2 c (1+m)/m² × 2p/(2.44 λ (1+m)) the (1+m) cancels — the finite-conjugate correction appears in both and leaves the product alone. DOF_max = 4 p c / (2.44 λ m²) Step 3 — eliminate m using D1's identity m = p/s_px. DOF_max = 4 p c s_px² / (2.44 λ p²) = 4 (c/p) s_px² / (2.44 λ) the pixel pitch cancels — it appears once in the numerator from the sampling limit and once in the denominator from the magnification, and they annihilate. Writing the circle of confusion as c = k_c · p pixels: DOF_max = (4 k_c / 2.44) · s_px² / λ = 1.639 k_c · s_px² / λ with the working choice k_c = 2: DOF_max ≈ 3.28 · s_px² / λ Verification against the independent route. Line 3: m = 0.06061, p = 3.45 µm, λ = 660 nm, c = 6.9 µm. N_max = 0.0069/(2.44×0.00066×1.06061) = 4.038 DOF = 2(4.038)(0.0069)(1.06061)/0.0036735 = 16.09 mm Closed form: 3.2787 × 0.05693²/0.00066 = 16.10 mm ✓ What the result actually says. Three things you can buy — a larger sensor, smaller pixels, a more expensive lens — have all vanished from the answer. The only two levers are the resolution you demand and the wavelength you illuminate with. Because the dependence on s_px is quadratic, halving the resolution requirement quarters the depth: s_px → s_px/2 ⇒ DOF_max → DOF_max/4 That single line explains why precision vision is dominated by fixturing. Assumptions made visible: incoherent, quasi-monochromatic illumination; a diffraction-limited lens (a real lens with aberrations reaches its best performance around f/4–f/5.6 and is worse than this at wide apertures, so the practical optimum is usually at or slightly beyond N_max, not below it); c stated in pixels; and the two-pixel sampling criterion, which is a convention — using d_Airy = p halves every figure, and some metrology houses do exactly that.
D3 — THE PHOTON CHAIN: FROM RADIANCE TO MINIMUM DETECTABLE CONTRAST CORE
Start: the camera equation. For a Lambertian scene of radiance L (W·m⁻²·sr⁻&sup9;), a lens of f-number N, transmission τ, at magnification m, the irradiance at the image plane is E_img = π τ L / ( 4 N² (1+m)² ) Step 1 — electrons collected in one pixel in one exposure. Pixel area p², exposure t, quantum efficiency η(λ), photon energy hc/λ: S = E_img · p² · t · η · λ / (h c) Collecting the levers: S ∝ L · t · p² · η / ( N² (1+m)² ) Every design decision in this module appears here exactly once, which is why it is worth writing out. Note p²: a 5.86 µm pixel collects 2.9× the photons of a 3.45 µm pixel at the same illumination — the real reason large-pixel cameras are specified for dim or fast applications. Step 2 — the noise. Photon arrivals are Poisson, so their variance equals their mean. Adding independent read and dark terms in quadrature: σ² = S + σ_r² + D t σ = √(S + σ_r² + D t) SNR = S / √(S + σ_r² + D t) → √S when S ≫ σ_r² Step 3 — where the two regimes divide. Shot noise dominates when S > σ_r². For σ_r = 2.5 e⁻ that is S > 6.25 e⁻ — about 0.06 % of full well. Any usable industrial image is shot-noise limited, which is why read noise almost never decides an industrial camera choice, and why the marketing emphasis on it is misplaced for this application. Step 4 — detection of a contrast difference. A defect covering n pixels changes the mean by ΔS. The uncertainty in the mean of n pixels is σ/√n. Requiring a confidence factor k (Rose criterion, k ≈ 5): ΔS ≥ k σ/√n C = ΔS/S ⇒ C_min = k √(S + σ_r²) / (S √n) shot-noise limit: C_min ≈ k / √(n S) Step 5 — the three consequences, each with a number. (i) Light buys contrast as a square root. To halve C_min you need 4× the photons. Going from 15 % to 60 % of full well improves C_min by exactly 2.00×; going from 60 % to 100 % improves it by only 1.29×. The useful range of “add more light” ends around 60–70 % of full well. (ii) Area buys contrast as a square root too, and area is free. Resolving the defect over 4× the pixels (halving s_px) improves C_min by 2× — but halving s_px also quarters DOF_max by D2, and divides the light per pixel by 4 if the sensor is the same size. These are the same trade seen from three directions. (iii) Gain cancels. Let every electron count be multiplied by g: C_min = k√(gS)/(gS√n)... only if the noise is also amplified, which it is. C_min is invariant under gain. The only thing gain changes is where the numbers sit in the ADC range. Assumptions made visible: Lambertian scene; uniform illumination across the ROI; independent pixels (true for shot noise, false for fixed-pattern noise, which is why flat-field correction must precede any averaging argument); a defect whose n pixels are all fully covered (a partially covered boundary pixel contributes less contrast and more variance).
D4 — THE TIME CHAIN: BLUR, SHEAR AND THE INDEPENDENCE RESULT CORE
Part A — blur. During an exposure t, a point on the part traverses v·t in object units. Its image is the convolution of the true image with a rectangular kernel of that length: b_mm = v t b_px = v t / s_px Effect on measurement, not just appearance: convolution with a symmetric rectangle does not shift a symmetric edge, so pure translation blur is unbiased for an isolated straight edge. It becomes biased as soon as the feature is asymmetric or two edges are closer than b — which is why the blur allowance for gauging (0.2 px) is far tighter than for detection (1 px). Blur also reduces gradient amplitude by roughly 1/b, degrading the subpixel estimator's SNR in exact proportion. Part B — rolling-shutter shear. Row j begins its exposure at t_j = j · t_row t_row = t_readout / N_rows Between rows the part advances v·t_row in object units, i.e. δ = v t_row / s_px pixels of horizontal displacement per row A feature that is truly vertical is therefore recorded along the line x(j) = x₀ + δ · j whose angle from vertical is θ = arctan(δ) = arctan( v · t_row / s_px ) The independence result. Observe what is not in this expression: • the number of rows in the region of interest — j cancelled when we took the slope. Cropping the ROI changes the displacement (Δx = δ·N_ROI) but not the angle. • the exposure time — t and t_row are independent parameters. Strobing does not help unless the sensor offers global reset release. • the size of the object. • the focal length, except through s_px. And what is in it, with the wrong sign for intuition: • s_px in the denominator — zooming in makes shear worse. Halving s_px to resolve a feature better doubles δ and therefore roughly doubles θ (exactly doubles tanθ). Line 3: v = 600 mm/s, t_row = 7.8125 µs, s_px = 0.05693 mm/px δ = 600 × 7.8125e-6 / 0.05693 = 0.08234 px/row θ = 4.707°, against a 3.0° reject limit. Part C — the delivery chain. The verdict must reach the part. Write the chain as a nominal lag plus a bounded jitter: T = Σ tᵢ ± Σ Δtᵢ position error = v · (T - T_compensated) ± v · ΣΔtᵢ Time-based tracking compensates T at one assumed speed v₀. At an actual speed v the fired position is x_fired = v · (d / v₀) = d · (v/v₀) error = d · (1 - v/v₀) — proportional to the tracked distance and to the fractional speed error. In pitches: error/pitch = (d/pitch) · (1 - v/v₀) Line 3: d/pitch = 1800/180 = 10, speed error 1 - 520/600 = 0.1333 error = 10 × 0.1333 = 1.33 pitches Note the structure: the error in pitches is the number of parts between camera and rejector, multiplied by the fractional speed error. Moving the rejector closer is therefore as effective as improving the speed accuracy, and is often cheaper. Encoder-based tracking replaces d/v₀ with a count, so the speed term vanishes identically and only the jitter remains: error = 1 count + v · ΣΔt_downstream The general lesson, which outlives machine vision: whenever an action must land on a moving object, express the delay in units of the motion itself (counts, pitches, encoder increments) rather than in seconds. Any system that converts time to position using an assumed speed inherits every future speed change as a position error. The same argument governs line-scan triggering, flying-shear cutting, labeller placement, printing registration and web inspection — and it is the reason encoders are on those machines at all.
