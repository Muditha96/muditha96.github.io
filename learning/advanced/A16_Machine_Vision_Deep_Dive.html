Page 1 · The Imaging Chain

A decision is only as good as the photons that made it

Quick Recap → Going Deeper

The micro card said: Vision = Light + Lens + Logic. At MSc level we treat machine vision as an information chain — every stage either preserves or destroys the signal, and you cannot recover what an earlier stage threw away.

A vision inspection is a sequence of irreversible transformations. A scene radiance field is sampled by optics, projected onto a sensor, quantised into pixels, then reduced to a feature, then to a single bit (PASS/FAIL). Garbage entering early cannot be rescued by a clever algorithm later — which is why roughly 80% of vision failures are lighting and optics problems, not software.

The six-stage imaging chain. Contrast created by lighting at stage 1 is free; contrast you try to invent in software later is expensive and fragile.
MSc framing: Think in terms of contrast budget. The job of optics + lighting is to put the feature you care about into a high-contrast, repeatable region of the sensor's dynamic range. The algorithm's job is then trivial. Most "AI will fix it" projects fail because they invert this order.
Page 2 · Optics

Lens, field of view, resolution & depth of field

Sizing the lens from the part

Optics selection is deterministic geometry, not guesswork. Given a part size and the required smallest detectable feature, the working distance, focal length and sensor all fall out of a few equations.

Spatial resolution R = FoV / sensor_pixels (mm per pixel) Smallest feature needs ≈ 3 px across (Nyquist + margin) Magnification m = sensor_size / FoV Lens (thin-lens) f ≈ WD · m / (1 + m) Field of view FoV = sensor_size · WD / f

FoV

Field of view — the real-world area the camera sees. Always add 10–20% margin for part jitter.

DoF

Depth of field — the in-focus depth range. Grows with f-number (N), shrinks with magnification.

WD

Working distance — lens-to-part. Set by the mechanical envelope of the machine.

Depth of field DoF ≈ 2 · N · c · (m+1) / m² N = f-number (f/#), c = circle of confusion (≈ pixel size), m = magnification Diffraction limit spot ≈ 2.44 · λ · N → too-small apertures blur, not sharpen
The aperture trade-off: closing the aperture (higher f/#) increases depth of field but, past the diffraction limit, destroys resolution. There is an optimum f/# for every job — usually f/4 to f/8 for industrial lenses. Telecentric lenses remove perspective error entirely for precision gauging, at the cost of an objective as large as the part.
Page 3 · The Sensor

CMOS, shutter, the Bayer filter & noise

How light becomes a number

A modern industrial sensor is a CMOS array. Each pixel is a photodiode that converts photons to electrons (the photoelectric effect — see Micro 29 for the device physics), accumulates them in a well during exposure, then reads out a voltage that an ADC quantises to a digital count.

Three sensor decisions that make or break a system: colour vs mono, dynamic range, and shutter type.
Practical rule: for measurement and most inspection use a monochrome global-shutter sensor — it is sharper, ~2× more sensitive, and free of motion skew. Use colour only when colour is the feature (e.g. wire-harness verification). Quantify noise as SNR = signal_electrons / √(shot + read² + dark); below ~10:1 your edge-finding repeatability collapses.
Page 4 · Lighting

The technique that wins 80% of jobs

Geometry & spectrum

Lighting is engineered, not chosen. The goal is to make the defect bright and the background dark (or vice-versa) and to do it repeatably regardless of ambient light. Geometry controls where light goes; spectrum controls what contrasts.

Bright-field (direct)

Light reflects into the lens. Flat shiny parts look bright; scratches go dark. The default.

Dark-field (low angle)

Grazing light; only edges & defects scatter into the lens. Surface engraving, cracks.

Backlight

Light behind the part → crisp silhouette. The king of dimensional gauging & presence/absence.

Dome / coaxial

Diffuse "cloudy-day" light kills glare on curved or specular parts. Reading codes on cans/foil.

Spectrum tricks: red/IR penetrates and reduces ambient sensitivity; blue/UV reveals fine surface texture; matched filters + monochromatic light reject factory lighting. Strobing the light synchronised to a global shutter freezes motion and lets you over-drive LEDs safely for huge instantaneous brightness.
Why it matters: a good lighting setup turns a "needs deep learning" problem into a "needs a threshold" problem. It is the single highest-leverage decision in the entire chain — and the cheapest to get right.
Page 5 · Image Formation & Calibration

The pinhole model and why you must calibrate

From world metres to image pixels

The mapping from 3D world points to 2D pixels is the pinhole camera model, refined with lens distortion. Calibration recovers these parameters so that pixel measurements become real-world millimetres.

Projection (homogeneous): s · [u v 1]ᵀ = K · [R | t] · [X Y Z 1]ᵀ Intrinsics K = | fx 0 cx | fx,fy = focal length in px | 0 fy cy | cx,cy = principal point | 0 0 1 | Radial distortion: x' = x(1 + k₁r² + k₂r⁴ + k₃r⁶) Tangential: + 2p₁xy + p₂(r²+2x²) …

Intrinsics (K, distortion) describe the camera+lens; extrinsics (R, t) describe where it sits in the world. A checkerboard or dot grid of known geometry, viewed from several poses, lets a solver (Zhang's method) recover all of them by minimising reprojection error.

Hand-eye calibration: when the camera guides a robot (A15), you additionally solve AX = XB to find the rigid transform between the camera frame and the robot flange. Skip this and your "pick here" coordinate is wrong by exactly the mounting offset — every time.
Page 6 · Classical Pipeline

Filter → segment → measure → decide

Deterministic, fast, explainable

Rule-based vision still runs the majority of high-speed inspection because it is deterministic, sub-millisecond, and auditable. The classic pipeline:

Acquire & pre-process. Flat-field correction, denoise (Gaussian/median), normalise illumination.
Enhance. Convolution kernels — Sobel/Scharr for gradients, Laplacian for edges, morphology (erode/dilate/open/close) to clean binary masks.
Segment. Thresholding (Otsu, adaptive), connected components / blob analysis, or edge tracing to isolate the region of interest.
Measure. Sub-pixel edge finding (interpolate the gradient peak to ~1/20 px), caliper tools, geometric fitting (least-squares line/circle), pattern matching (NCC — normalised cross-correlation).
Decide. Compare measurement to tolerance (links straight to GD&T, Micro 21) → emit PASS/FAIL + the reject signal.
2D convolution: g(x,y) = Σ Σ f(x−i, y−j) · h(i,j) Sobel-x kernel: [ −1 0 +1 ; −2 0 +2 ; −1 0 +1 ] → vertical edges NCC score ∈ [−1,1]: 1.0 = perfect template match (lighting-invariant)
Sub-pixel is the superpower: a 5 MP sensor over a 50 mm field gives ~25 µm/pixel, but sub-pixel edge interpolation pushes repeatable gauging to a few microns — rivalling contact gauges, with zero wear and at line speed.
Page 7 · Deep Learning Vision

When the rules can't be written down

CNNs, detection, segmentation & anomaly

Cosmetic defects — scratches on brushed metal, stains, weave flaws — defy explicit rules. Deep learning learns the rule from examples. The workhorse is the convolutional neural network (CNN): stacked learnable convolutions + pooling that build features from edges → textures → parts → objects.

A CNN learns the feature hierarchy that a classical engineer would otherwise hand-craft.

Classification

Whole image → one label. "Good / scratched / stained."

Detection

Bounding boxes + class (YOLO, Faster R-CNN). Locates each defect.

Segmentation

Per-pixel mask (U-Net). Defect area & shape for grading.

Anomaly detection

Train on good only (autoencoder/PatchCore); flag anything unfamiliar. Solves rare-defect scarcity.

The data reality: classical needs tuning; deep learning needs labelled data. Defects are rare, so anomaly detection on good samples only is the dominant industrial pattern. Validate with a confusion matrix — in inspection, a missed defect (false-negative escape) usually costs far more than a false reject, so tune the operating point accordingly.
Edge inference: models run on smart cameras or GPU/NPU edge boxes (TensorRT, OpenVINO) to hit line-rate latency. This is the vision face of Industry 4.0 edge computing (A31).
Page 8 · 3D Vision

Adding the third dimension

Four ways to recover depth

2D vision sees brightness; 3D vision sees shape — essential for bin-picking, volume, flatness and weld inspection.

MethodPrincipleStrengthLimit
StereoTwo cameras; disparity ∝ 1/depthPassive, scalableNeeds texture
Laser triangulationLaser line + angled camera, scannedVery precise (µm)Needs motion
Structured lightProject patterns, decode shiftDense, fast, accurateSensitive to ambient/shine
Time-of-FlightMeasure light round-trip timeWhole-frame, fast, long rangeLower resolution
Stereo depth: Z = f · B / d f = focal length (px), B = baseline between cameras, d = disparity (px) → bigger baseline B = better far-depth accuracy; finer disparity = less depth noise
Output: a point cloud (X,Y,Z per pixel). Robot bin-picking then fits known CAD models into the cloud to compute a 6-DoF grasp pose, which feeds the robot exactly as in A15 inverse kinematics.
Page 9 · Interfaces & Standards

Getting pixels out and decisions into the line

Camera buses, software standards, system architecture

GigE Vision

Gigabit Ethernet, up to 100 m, multi-camera. The workhorse. 10/25 GigE for high res/speed.

USB3 Vision

~5 m, cheap, high bandwidth, single camera. Bench & compact cells.

Camera Link / CXP

CoaXPress for the highest data rates — line-scan & very high speed.

GenICam standard

Vendor-neutral feature model — one API across brands.

On the line, the vision system is just another node in the Purdue/ISA-95 stack. It triggers off an encoder or sensor, runs its decision, and signals the PLC (A07) over digital I/O, PROFINET/EtherNet-IP, or publishes results via OPC-UA / MQTT-Sparkplug (A06) to SCADA & the historian.

Page 10 · Specifying a System

Worked example + how to spec it right

Worked example — verify a 10 mm dimension to ±50 µm
Tolerance band ±50 µm → want ≥ 5 px across the band → R ≤ 20 µm/px Field of view part 30 mm + 20% margin → FoV ≈ 36 mm Pixels needed 36 mm / 0.020 mm = 1800 px → use a 5 MP (2448×2048) mono cam Lighting collimated backlight → silhouette → high-contrast edges Measurement sub-pixel caliper → repeatability ≈ 1/20 px ≈ 1 µm ✓ Shutter global (part is moving) + strobe synchronised to trigger
Result: a non-contact gauge that beats the ±50 µm spec by an order of magnitude, runs at line speed, never wears, and logs every measurement for SPC — feeding directly into Six Sigma / SPC (Micro 27).
The specification checklist
Define the decision first. What feature, what tolerance, what failure modes, what false-reject vs escape cost?
Set resolution & FoV from the tolerance (≥3–5 px per smallest feature).
Engineer the lighting before choosing software — aim for high, repeatable, ambient-immune contrast.
Choose sensor & lens (mono/global shutter default; f/# at the diffraction sweet-spot).
Pick the algorithm — classical if a rule exists; deep/anomaly if the defect is cosmetic and rare.
Calibrate & integrate (intrinsics + hand-eye), wire to PLC/SCADA, validate with a confusion matrix & gauge R&R.
Quick Check — MSc level

Q1: You need to gauge a 0.2 mm feature to ±10 µm at line speed on a shiny moving part. Walk through the four highest-leverage decisions.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A1: (1) Lighting: collimated backlight for a clean silhouette, or a dome to kill specular glare. (2) Resolution: need ≥5 px across ±10 µm → R ≤ ~4 µm/px → high-MP sensor + telecentric lens to remove perspective error. (3) Sensor/shutter: mono global shutter + synchronised strobe to freeze motion. (4) Algorithm: sub-pixel caliper edge-finding, validated with gauge R&R — not deep learning, because the rule is well-defined geometry.

Q2: Why is anomaly detection (train on good samples only) the dominant deep-learning pattern in industrial inspection?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A2: Real defects are rare and varied, so you can never collect a balanced, exhaustive labelled set of every failure mode. Training a model on plentiful good parts to learn "normal," then flagging anything that deviates, sidesteps the class-imbalance and unseen-defect problem — and catches novel defects a supervised classifier was never shown.
