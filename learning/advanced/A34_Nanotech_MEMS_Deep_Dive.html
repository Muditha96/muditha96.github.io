The Nanoworld: Where the Rules Change

Nanotechnology is not just "making things smaller" — it is engineering in the size range where new physics switches on.

Definition (ISO/TS 80004)

Nanotechnology is the understanding and control of matter at dimensions of roughly 1–100 nm, where size-dependent properties emerge that neither single atoms nor bulk material possess.

One nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m — about 4 silicon atoms side by side. A human hair is ~80,000 nm wide. The "nano zone" matters because two effects take over there: surface atoms start to outnumber bulk atoms (chemistry changes) and electrons feel the size of their box (quantum confinement changes optics and electronics). MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) are the micro-scale cousins: silicon chips with moving parts — springs, masses, mirrors, diaphragms — batch-fabricated like ICs, sitting between the nano and macro worlds.

The scale ladder — where nano lives

Six orders of magnitude separate a MEMS accelerometer's proof mass (~100 µm) from the quantum dots in a display (~5 nm).

Surface Dominance: The 6/d Law

Shrink a particle and its surface refuses to shrink as fast as its volume — until the surface runs the show.

The core geometry

Surface area scales with L², volume with L³ — so the surface-to-volume ratio grows as 1/L when you shrink. For a sphere or cube of size d:

SA/V = 6/d (cube of side d; sphere: 6/d as well, using diameter) Specific surface area of a powder: S = 6 / (ρ·d) → 10 nm particles, ρ = 5 g/cm³: S = 120 m²/g — a football pitch in half a spoonful

This is why nanoparticles are super-catalysts (reactions happen at surfaces), why nano-powders can be explosive (flour-silo effect, amplified), why gold melts ~300 °C lower at 5 nm than in bulk (surface atoms are loosely bound), and why nano-fillers strengthen composites at tiny loadings — enormous interface area per gram.

Quantum Confinement: Colour by Size

Squeeze an electron into a box comparable to its own wavelength and its energy levels spread apart — size becomes a design parameter.

Particle in a box → the Brus equation

In a semiconductor nanocrystal smaller than the exciton Bohr radius (~5.6 nm in CdSe), the band gap widens as the crystal shrinks — so the same material emits different colours at different sizes.

Particle-in-a-box levels: Eₙ = n²h² / 8mL² → E ∝ 1/L² Brus (quantum dot) approximation, R in nm, CdSe: E_gap ≈ 1.74 + 3.73/R² − 0.26/R (eV) λ = 1240 / E_gap (nm) bulk gap confinement Coulomb

This is the physics behind QLED displays (one material, three colours by size), quantum-dot lasers, and fluorescent bio-labels. The same confinement makes nanowires and 2-D sheets behave electronically unlike their bulk parents — graphene's massless carriers are the extreme case.

🎛 Interactive — tune a CdSe quantum dot
Dot radius R2.0 nm

Smaller dot → bigger gap → bluer emission. Bigger dot → smaller gap → redder. Same crystal, different colour.

Other size effects

Superparamagnetism (<20 nm magnetite loses hysteresis), ballistic conduction (electrons cross without scattering), plasmon resonance (nano-gold is ruby red, not golden).

Design rule

Below ~10 nm assume bulk data sheets are wrong: melting point, band gap, magnetism, colour, diffusivity and strength all become functions of size.

Nanofabrication: Top-Down vs Bottom-Up

Two philosophies: carve small from big (lithography) or grow big from small (self-assembly, deposition).

⬇ Top-down

Sculpt bulk material with light, plasma and beams: photolithography, EUV, e-beam, DRIE etching, nanoimprint. Precise placement, wafer-scale, expensive tools. This is the IC/MEMS route.

⬆ Bottom-up

Let chemistry build the structure: CVD (graphene, CNTs), ALD (atomic-layer films), sol-gel, colloidal synthesis (QDs), self-assembly. Atomic perfection, cheap per gram, harder to place exactly where you want.

MEMS & the Scaling Laws

Shrink a machine 1000× and the physics rulebook is rewritten: gravity becomes irrelevant, surfaces become sticky, and electrostatics becomes a muscle.

Definition

A MEMS device is a chip-scale system that combines moving mechanical elements — springs, proof masses, diaphragms, mirrors — with sensing/actuating electronics, batch-fabricated on silicon using the same planar process as ICs.

Typical feature sizes are 1–100 µm. Because thousands of devices share one wafer, a precision accelerometer that would cost $1,000 as a machined instrument costs under a dollar as MEMS — which is why your phone contains about ten of them.

The cube-square law — who wins when L shrinks
Weight, inertia ∝ L³ (volume) Electrostatic force ∝ L² (area) Surface tension, van der Waals ∝ L¹ → divide L by 1000: weight falls 10⁹×, surface forces only 10³× Resonance: f₀ = (1/2π)·√(k/m) — µg masses → kHz–MHz, survives >100,000 g shock

At the micro-scale gravity is a rounding error and surface forces dominate — a released beam that touches the substrate can weld itself there permanently (stiction, the #1 MEMS yield killer). The same law is why electrostatics — feeble at human scale — becomes a perfectly good motor when gaps are 1 µm.

The universal MEMS building block: a spring-suspended proof mass whose position is read (sensor) or forced (actuator) through comb-finger capacitors.

MEMS Sensors: Accel, Gyro, Pressure

Three archetypes cover 90 % of industrial MEMS sensing — a capacitive proof mass, a Coriolis vibrator and a piezoresistive diaphragm.

1 · Capacitive accelerometer
Spring balance: x = m·a / k Parallel plate: C = ε₀A / g Differential read-out: ΔC/C ≈ x/g → ratiometric, temperature-stable Noise floor set by Brownian motion: aₙ ∝ √(4k_BT·b / m) — bigger mass = quieter

The ASIC measures the C₁–C₂ imbalance with a charge amplifier at ~1 MHz carrier. Industrial versions monitor machine vibration (ISO 10816) for predictive maintenance — the same physics as the airbag trigger that fires when ΔC says −50 g.

2 · Vibratory gyroscope

The proof mass is driven into resonance along x. Rotate the chip at Ω and the Coriolis force F = 2mΩ×v pushes it along y — the sense combs read that tiny orthogonal wobble. Two modes, one mass: drive and sense. Used in AGV/robot IMUs, camera stabilisation, vehicle ESC.

3 · Piezoresistive pressure sensor

A thin Si diaphragm flexes under ΔP; four implanted piezoresistors at the stress maxima form a Wheatstone bridge. Silicon's gauge factor is ~50–100 (metal foil: ~2) thanks to the piezoresistive effect. This is the TPMS in every tyre and the barometer in your phone.

MEMS Actuators & the Pull-In Limit

Four ways to make silicon move — and the famous electrostatic instability every MEMS designer must respect.

Electrostatic actuation — force & pull-in
Parallel plate: F = ε₀·A·V² / 2g² — grows as the gap closes → runaway! Stable travel only to x = g/3; beyond it the plate SNAPS shut (pull-in) Pull-in voltage: V_PI = √( 8·k·g³ / 27·ε₀·A ) Comb drive: F = n·ε₀·t·V²/g — constant with x → long stable travel, less force

Pull-in is used deliberately in RF-MEMS switches (snap = ON) and feared everywhere else: it is why analogue micromirrors and tunable capacitors only use the first third of their gap. Beyond g/3 the electrostatic force rises faster than the spring can answer — a mechanical bifurcation you can compute below.

⚠️ Nano-EHS — the safety file

Free nanoparticles behave like gases with mass: they stay airborne for hours, deposit deep in alveoli, and some (rigid, biopersistent fibres like certain long CNTs) raise asbestos-like concerns. The rulebook: ISO/TR 12885 (workplace nano-safety), control banding when toxicity data is missing, and REACH nano-annexes in the EU.

  • 1Hierarchy of controls: work wet or in matrix > enclosed glovebox > local exhaust with HEPA > PPE (FFP3/P100 last, not first).
  • 2Embedded ≠ free: a CNT locked in cured epoxy is inert — the risk windows are powder handling, machining/sanding composites, and filter changes.
  • 3Measure exposure with particle counters (CPC) at the task, not the room average — nano plumes are local and brief.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

The whole module in one screen — then prove you own it.

The 6 ideas to keep
  • 1Nano = 1–100 nm, where size-dependent properties emerge (ISO/TS 80004).
  • 2SA/V = 6/d — surfaces take over: catalysis, reactivity, melting-point drop.
  • 3Quantum confinement: E ∝ 1/L² — the same crystal changes colour with size (Brus equation).
  • 40D/1D/2D/3D taxonomy: dots, tubes/wires, sheets, nanostructured bulk — properties are ceilings, not guarantees.
  • 5MEMS = spring + mass + capacitor, batch-made; cube-square law makes electrostatics strong and stiction real.
  • 6Pull-in at g/3, Coriolis for gyros, piezoresistive bridges for pressure — three equations run the industry.
⚠️ Classic failure modes
🔗 Stiction

Released structure contacts substrate → capillary/vdW forces weld it. Root cause of low MEMS yield; fixed by vapour-phase release, CPD, SAM coatings, dimples.

⚡ Dielectric charging

RF-MEMS switch actuated for months traps charge in its dielectric → shifts V_PI until stuck ON/OFF. Mitigate with bipolar drive waveforms.

📦 Packaging stress

Moulding & solder reflow strain the die → offset drift in accels/gyros. Stress-isolation frames and post-solder recalibration are mandatory.

💥 Shock fracture & particle jam

Ultra-high g snaps springs or a loose particle blocks a 2 µm gap; hermetic caps and travel stops are the guards.

🧲 Nanoparticle agglomeration

High surface energy clumps nano-fillers into µm lumps → composite gets WEAKER than neat resin. Needs surfactants, sonication, in-line dispersion QC.

🌫 Fugitive nano-dust

Dry powder transfer without LEV/HEPA → chronic inhalation exposure. Control banding + wet handling; audit filter-change procedures.

🧠 Q1 — Why does a 5 nm gold particle melt ~300 °C below bulk gold?

Tap to revealAt 5 nm a large fraction of atoms sit on the surface, where they are under-coordinated and loosely bound (SA/V = 6/d). Less energy is needed to disorder the lattice — the Gibbs–Thomson size-dependent melting depression.

🧠 Q2 — Two CdSe quantum dots: one emits red, one blue. Which is bigger?

Tap to revealThe RED one. Confinement energy scales as ~1/R², so a larger dot has a smaller band gap → longer wavelength (red). Shrink the dot and the gap widens → blue.

🧠 Q3 — Why is electrostatic actuation weak at human scale but excellent in MEMS?

Tap to revealElectrostatic force scales with area (L²) while weight/inertia scale with volume (L³). Shrinking L by 1000× cuts weight 10⁹× but the electrostatic force only 10⁶–10³× — and F ∝ V²/g², so µm gaps give strong fields at modest voltages.

🧠 Q4 — What is pull-in, and at what fraction of the gap does it strike?

Tap to revealBeyond one-third of the gap (x = g/3) the electrostatic force (∝ 1/(g−x)²) rises faster than the linear spring force — the equilibrium becomes unstable and the plate snaps shut. V_PI = √(8kg³/27ε₀A).

🧠 Q5 — A MEMS gyroscope has no rotating wheel. How does it sense rotation?

Tap to revealA proof mass is driven to vibrate along x. Under rotation Ω the Coriolis force F = 2mΩ×v deflects it along the orthogonal y axis; sense combs read that deflection, which is proportional to the rotation rate.

🧠 Q6 — A composite with 3 wt % CNTs tests WEAKER than the neat polymer. Most likely root cause?

Tap to revealAgglomeration: high surface energy clumped the CNTs into micro-lumps that act as stress concentrators / defects. Nano-properties only transfer with good dispersion, interface bonding and alignment.
