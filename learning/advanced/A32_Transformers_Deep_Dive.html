You Paid for 2050 kVA. You Can Use 1633.

A GARMENT PLANT NEAR BIYAGAMA, SIX MONTHS AFTER AN EXPANSION

The plant grew. A second transformer was added beside the original one, both feeding the same LV busbar through a bus coupler that is normally closed. Nameplates: 1250 kVA, 6.0 % impedance and 800 kVA, 4.0 % impedance. Combined, 2050 kVA. Measured peak demand, 1750 kVA — a comfortable 85 % of the pair.

And yet the smaller transformer runs hot. Its winding-temperature alarm comes in most afternoons. Twice it has tripped on overload and dropped half the sewing floor, and each time the maintenance report says the same thing: “transformer overload — but total load was only 85 % of capacity, cause unknown.” The oil has been tested. It is fine. The fans have been added. It still runs hot.

Nothing is faulty. Two transformers in parallel do not share load in proportion to their nameplates — they share it in proportion to their kVA divided by their percentage impedance. The 800 kVA unit, being the “stiffer” of the two at 4 %, insists on taking almost exactly the same current as its much larger neighbour:

S₁/S₂ = (1250/6.0) : (800/4.0) = 208.3 : 200.0 At a total bus load of 1750 kVA: T1 (1250 kVA) carries 1750 × 208.3/408.3 = 893 kVA → 71 % loaded T2 ( 800 kVA) carries 1750 × 200.0/408.3 = 857 kVA → 107 % loaded The pair reaches its true limit when T2 hits 100 %: Stotal,max = 800 × 408.3/200.0 = 1633 kVA, not 2050 kVA.

Roughly 420 kVA of purchased capacity — a fifth of the installation — can never be used, and no amount of oil testing, fan-fitting or relay adjustment will release it. The number that caused this is printed on both nameplates and was never compared. The fix, and the three other things that must match before two transformers may be paralleled at all, are on page 11.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Before reading on — which single change would let the plant use the most of its installed capacity, at the lowest cost?

C — and it costs almost nothing. With the coupler open, each transformer feeds its own section of the board, so you decide the split instead of the impedances deciding it. Move feeders until each unit sits near the same percentage of its own rating and virtually all 2050 kVA becomes usable. You lose the N−1 comfort of a common bus, which is why the split must be planned rather than improvised.

D is the textbook answer and the correct permanent one — matched percentage impedance is the condition for proportional sharing — but it means buying a transformer to solve an arithmetic problem, so it is rarely first.

A treats the symptom. Fans raise the thermal limit of the unit that is already doing more than its share, and the sharing stays just as wrong; the overload protection setting, not the temperature, is what should stop you.

B makes it worse. A tap change on one of two paralleled transformers creates a voltage difference between them and drives a circulating current that flows even at no load, adding heat to both (page 11). Taps are for volts, not for load sharing.

What a Transformer Actually Is

A static machine that trades volts for amps through a shared magnetic field — and, almost incidentally, the reason a national grid is possible at all.

primary (source side) secondary (load side) laminated steel core mutual flux path
THE DEFINITION, SAID PROPERLY CORE

A transformer is a static electromagnetic device that transfers alternating-current energy between two or more circuits by mutual magnetic induction, changing the voltage and current in inverse proportion while leaving the power and the frequency essentially unchanged.

Every word earns its place. Static — no rotating part, which is why a well-made transformer outlives the engineer who specified it. Alternating — a steady flux induces nothing, so the machine is useless on DC and destroyed by it. Mutual induction — the two circuits are joined by flux, not by copper, so the transformer also provides galvanic isolation. Inverse proportion — voltage up means current down, in the same ratio, because the machine cannot create energy. Frequency unchanged — a transformer cannot change 50 Hz to 60 Hz; only a converter can, and that is the boundary between this module and A28.

It changes

V, I

in inverse ratio, plus the impedance seen through it (ratio squared).

It preserves

P, f

power out equals power in minus losses; frequency is untouched.

It cannot

DC

no dΦ/dt, no EMF — and the winding becomes a short circuit.

WHY THE GRID EXISTS BECAUSE OF THIS DEVICE CORE

Transmission loss in a line is I²R. It depends on current, not on voltage. Deliver a fixed power P at ten times the voltage and you need one tenth of the current, so the loss falls by a factor of one hundred. That single sentence is why every generator in the country sits behind a step-up transformer and every consumer behind a step-down one.

Deliver P = 10 MW over a line of total resistance R = 2 Ω (three-phase, unity PF): at 11 kV: I = 10×10⁶/(√3 × 11×10³) = 525 A → Ploss = 3I²R = 1.65 MW (16.5 %) at 132 kV: I = 10×10⁶/(√3 × 132×10³) = 43.7 A → Ploss = 3I²R = 11.5 kW (0.11 %) A voltage ratio of 12 gives a loss ratio of 12² = 144.

The Ceylon Electricity Board’s network is a chain of these ratios: 220 kV and 132 kV transmission, 33 kV and 11 kV distribution, 400/230 V to the consumer. Your factory transformer is simply the last link, and the same physics governs it as governs the 220 kV grid transformer at Kotugoda.

Faraday’s Law & the EMF Equation

One law, one integration, one number — 4.44 — and from it the volts-per-turn that sizes every transformer ever wound.

core flux Φ(t) induced EMF e(t) the 90° relationship
FROM FARADAY TO 4.44 CORE

Faraday’s law of induction states that the EMF induced in a coil equals the negative rate of change of the flux linkage. Lenz’s minus sign says the induced EMF opposes the change that produced it — which is why a transformer draws only magnetising current at no load rather than exploding.

e = −N dΦ/dt (Faraday + Lenz) Let the flux be sinusoidal: Φ(t) = Φm sin ωt, ω = 2πf e = −N Φm ω cos ωt → peak EMF Em = N ω Φm = 2πf N Φm For a sine wave the RMS value is Em/√2: E = 2πf N Φm / √2 = (2π/√2) f N Φm E = 4.44 f N Φm and Φm = Bm · Acore E = 4.44 f N Bm A — the transformer EMF equation

The constant is not magic: 2π/√2 = 4.4429. It carries a hidden assumption — that the waveform is a sine. Feed a transformer a square wave and the constant becomes 4.0; feed it a distorted mains and the peak flux is set by the area under the voltage wave, not by its RMS value, which is why a flat-topped waveform (page 5, and A56) actually runs a transformer cooler magnetically than a peaky one of the same RMS.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A transformer rated 415 V, 50 Hz is energised at 415 V, 60 Hz. What happens to the peak core flux?

B. Rearranging the EMF equation, Bm = V / (4.44 f N A) — the peak flux density is proportional to V/f, not to V. Raise the frequency at constant voltage and the flux falls: 50/60 = 0.833, so Bm drops to 83 % of design. The transformer is magnetically under-worked, runs cooler in the core, and is perfectly safe — it simply has spare capability it is not using.

The dangerous direction is the other one: a 60 Hz transformer energised at the same voltage on a 50 Hz supply sees Bm ×1.2, which for a core already designed near 1.6 T pushes it towards 1.9 T, deep into saturation — screaming magnetising current, overheating and nuisance tripping. This is the single most common import mistake in Sri Lanka: American 60 Hz equipment on a 50 Hz supply.

The same ratio explains V/f control in a VFD (A08) — a motor is also a magnetic circuit, and holding V/f constant is exactly holding flux constant.
VOLTS PER TURN — THE DESIGNER’S FIRST NUMBER CORE

Because both windings share the same flux, the EMF equation applies to each with the same f, Bm and A. Divide one by the other and everything cancels except the turns. That gives the single most useful design quantity: volts per turn.

Et = E/N = 4.44 f Bm A (volts per turn — the same for EVERY winding on that core) WORKED — an 11 kV / 400 V, 50 Hz distribution unit: core area A = 0.05 m² (net iron, after stacking factor) peak density Bm = 1.6 T (typical for CRGO grain-oriented steel) Et = 4.44 × 50 × 1.6 × 0.05 = 17.76 V per turn HV turns: N₁ = 11000 / 17.76 = 619 turns (delta-connected, full line voltage) LV turns: phase voltage = 400/√3 = 231 V (star) N₂ = 231 / 17.76 = 13.0 turns → 13 turns Turns must be INTEGERS. Rounding 13.0 → 13 fixes the true ratio; the designer then trims Bm or A slightly so the output lands on 400 V. This is why real ratios are never quite the round numbers on the label.

Notice the asymmetry that surprises everyone the first time: the high-voltage winding has hundreds of turns of thin wire, the low-voltage winding a dozen turns of massive copper strip. Ampere-turns must balance, so the low-voltage side carries the large current and needs the large cross-section. On an 11 kV/400 V, 1000 kVA unit the LV winding carries about 1443 A — it is a bar, not a wire.

THE CONSEQUENCES OF Bm = V / (4.44 f N A) STRETCH

Over-excitation

V/f ↑

Over-voltage or under-frequency drives the core towards saturation. Generator step-up transformers therefore carry a V/Hz (24) relay, not just an over-voltage relay.

Higher f, smaller core

A ∝ 1/f

At 400 Hz (aircraft) or 100 kHz (SMPS) the same power needs a far smaller core. This is why a laptop charger is the size of a matchbox and a 50 Hz doorbell transformer is not.

DC is fatal

f = 0

With f = 0 the required Bm is infinite — the core saturates instantly, the back-EMF collapses, and the winding is left as its own DC resistance across the supply.

Even a little DC

GIC

Geomagnetically induced currents and half-wave rectifier loads push a DC bias into the winding, offsetting the B–H excursion into saturation on one half-cycle: hum, harmonics and heat at rated voltage.

The nameplate frequency is not decoration. A transformer is a flux machine wearing a voltage label; the label is only true at the stated frequency. Any time you see an equipment rating written as “415 V 50 Hz”, read it as “8.3 volts per hertz” and you will never make the import mistake.

💼

A question that appears in nearly every machines viva and many interviews: “Why can a transformer not work on DC?” The weak answer is “because there is no changing flux”. The strong answer adds the second half: “…and because with no back-EMF the primary presents only its winding resistance — a few ohms — across the supply, so it destroys itself in seconds.” Say both halves.

Ratio, Current & the Impedance Transformation

Volts divide by a, amps multiply by a, and impedance — the one people forget — scales by a squared.

THE THREE RATIOS CORE

Both windings link the same flux, so each develops the same volts per turn. That gives the voltage ratio directly. Conservation of energy — and, more fundamentally, the requirement that the net magnetomotive force stay almost zero — gives the current ratio.

Define the turns ratio a = N₁/N₂ VOLTAGE V₁/V₂ = N₁/N₂ = a (same volts per turn) CURRENT I₁/I₂ = N₂/N₁ = 1/a (ampere-turn balance: N₁I₁ ≈ N₂I₂) POWER V₁I₁ = V₂I₂ (ideal — kVA in = kVA out) IMPEDANCE Z₂' = (V₁/I₁) = (aV₂)/(I₂/a) = a² · Z₂ a > 1 → step-down (fewer turns, lower volts, higher amps on the secondary) a < 1 → step-up

The ampere-turn balance is worth pausing on, because it is the mechanism by which a transformer knows that you have switched on a load. Adding secondary current N₂I₂ tries to change the core flux; the flux tries to change the primary back-EMF; the primary instantly draws whatever extra current N₁I₁ is needed to cancel the disturbance and hold the flux constant. The flux in a transformer at rated voltage is essentially the same at no load and at full load — a fact that surprises many students and immediately explains why iron loss is constant while copper loss varies with load squared (page 8).

IMPEDANCE REFERRAL — THE SQUARED TERM CORE

Because voltage scales by a and current by 1/a, any impedance viewed through the transformer scales by a². This lets you collapse a two-circuit problem into a single circuit — the trick that makes the equivalent circuit on page 6 usable.

Referring the SECONDARY to the PRIMARY (multiply by a²): R₂' = a²R₂ X₂' = a²X₂ Zload' = a²Zload V₂' = aV₂ I₂' = I₂/a Referring the PRIMARY to the SECONDARY: divide by a². CHECK — power must be invariant: I₂'² R₂' = (I₂/a)² · a²R₂ = I₂²R₂ ✓ the same watts, either side. EXAMPLE: a 4:1 step-down transformer feeds an 8 Ω load. The source sees Z' = 4² × 8 = 128 Ω.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A 230 V / 12 V bell transformer is accidentally connected the wrong way round — 230 V applied to the 12 V winding. What happens?

C. A transformer is electrically reversible — but only within its flux rating. The 12 V winding has perhaps 30 turns; applying 230 V to it demands a peak flux density about 19 times the design value. The core saturates within the first quarter-cycle, the magnetising inductance collapses, and the winding — thin wire, designed for a small magnetising current at 12 V — is left as a few ohms across the mains.

A is the seductive wrong answer: the ratio would give 4400 V if the core could carry the flux, which it cannot. The turns ratio tells you what a transformer does; the EMF equation tells you whether it survives doing it.

The legitimate version of this is an isolation transformer used in reverse, where both windings are rated for the same voltage — there, reversal is genuinely harmless.

The Core: B–H, Iron Loss, Saturation & Inrush

Everything that makes a transformer imperfect lives in the steel — and so does the reason a healthy new transformer trips its own incomer on switch-on.

B–H loop saturation / distorted Iµ linear ideal coercivity Hc
READING THE B–H LOOP CORE

Drive a ferromagnetic core around one full cycle of magnetising force H and the flux density B does not retrace its path — it traces a loop. The loop exists because magnetic domains do not rotate reversibly; some of the energy spent aligning them is dissipated as heat. Three features on the loop have names you must be able to use:

Remanence Br

0.8–1.4 T

Flux left in the steel when H returns to zero. This is what makes inrush current a lottery decided by the instant of switching.

Coercivity Hc

low = good

The reverse H needed to wipe B back to zero. Narrow loop = soft magnetic material = low hysteresis loss. Transformer steel is deliberately “soft”.

Loop area

J/m³/cycle

Energy dissipated per cycle per unit volume. Multiply by f and by core volume and you have the hysteresis loss in watts.

The lamination is the single most important manufacturing decision in the machine. A solid core would behave as a one-turn short-circuited secondary; slicing it into insulated sheets parallel to the flux forces the eddy currents into many small, high-resistance loops. Because Pe ∝ t², going from 0.35 mm to 0.23 mm steel cuts the eddy component by 57 %. Amorphous ribbon at 25 µm takes it almost to nothing — at the price of a brittle, awkward, more expensive core and a slightly larger unit.

SATURATION AND THE UGLY MAGNETISING CURRENT STRETCH

Above the knee, a large increase in H buys almost no increase in B: the domains are all aligned and the steel behaves like air. To force a sinusoidal flux through a non-linear core, the magnetising current must itself be non-sinusoidal — peaky, symmetric about zero, and therefore rich in odd harmonics, dominated by the third.

  • 1
    Where the third harmonic goes matters. In a delta winding it circulates happily inside the closed loop, keeping the flux sinusoidal. In a three-wire star it has nowhere to go, so the flux distorts instead and a third-harmonic voltage appears on the phase-to-neutral voltages — the reason a tertiary delta is fitted to large star-star transformers (page 10).
  • 2
    Design margin. CRGO saturates around 1.9–2.0 T. Designers choose 1.5–1.7 T at rated volts, leaving headroom for the +10 % over-voltage that IEC 60076-1 requires the unit to withstand continuously. Push the design flux up to save iron and you buy a transformer that hums, runs hot on a high-voltage day, and trips on inrush.
  • 3
    Magnetostriction — the hum. Laminations physically change length as they magnetise, twice per cycle: the familiar 100 Hz hum (at 50 Hz supply) plus harmonics. Louder hum after years of service usually means loose clamping, not a magnetic problem.
INRUSH CURRENT — WHY A HEALTHY TRANSFORMER TRIPS ITS OWN BREAKER CORE

Flux is the integral of applied voltage. If the supply is closed at the instant the voltage passes through zero, the flux must swing from its starting value through a full excursion in one direction — and if remanent flux from the last switch-off happens to point the same way, the demanded peak can reach twice normal plus the remanence. The core saturates hard, magnetising inductance collapses, and the primary draws a huge, offset, decaying current limited only by winding resistance and source impedance.

Worst case (switching at voltage zero, with remanence Br): Bpeak,demanded = 2Bm + Br — far above saturation Resulting first-peak inrush: Iinrush ≈ 8–12 × Irated (distribution units; up to 20× on some designs, lower on large HV units) Decay time constant τ = L/R — a few cycles for small units, several SECONDS for large power transformers. Best case: close at voltage PEAK with no remanence → almost no inrush.
  • 1
    It is not a fault, so protection must not see it as one. Differential relays use second-harmonic restraint — inrush current is asymmetric and typically 15–20 % second harmonic, whereas a genuine internal fault current is not (page 14).
  • 2
    Curve selection. The LV/HV protective device must be chosen so its instantaneous element sits above the inrush envelope — typically a C or D curve MCB, or a suitably delayed magnetic setting on an MCCB/ACB. Fitting a B-curve device upstream of a transformer guarantees a nuisance trip on energisation.
  • 3
    Cures exist. Point-on-wave controlled switching, pre-insertion resistors, or NTC inrush limiters on small units. In the field, the usual cure is simply to energise from the HV side and accept the transient.
  • 4
    Sympathetic inrush. Energising one transformer can drive an already-energised neighbour on the same bus into partial saturation, so the second unit draws inrush too. On a bus with several transformers, switching sequence matters.

WHERE LEAKAGE REACTANCE COMES FROM STRETCH

Leakage reactance is not a defect to be minimised to zero — it is a design variable. The leakage flux is the flux that crosses the gap between the LV and HV windings rather than travelling the iron path. Its magnitude therefore depends on winding geometry: the radial gap between windings, the winding height, and the number of ampere-turns.

Roughly, for concentric cylindrical windings: Xeq ∝ (N² · f · mean turn length · equivalent gap width) / (winding height) Consequences the designer trades off: LOW %Z (3–4 %) → good voltage regulation, BUT high fault current → more expensive switchgear, higher short-circuit forces HIGH %Z (8–12 %) → low fault level, gentle on switchgear, BUT poor regulation and worse load sharing behaviour in parallel Typical distribution practice: 4 % (≤630 kVA), 5–6 % (800–2500 kVA), 8–12 % or more for large power and furnace transformers.

So when the nameplate says %Z = 6, someone chose that number, and they chose it by moving copper. That is the connection between a mechanical drawing and a switchgear specification — and it is why you cannot simply “ask for the same transformer with a different impedance” without a redesign.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Two identical 1000 kVA transformers are offered: one with %Z = 4, one with %Z = 6. Your switchboard is rated 25 kA. Which do you buy?

B. Run the arithmetic before the preference. At 1000 kVA, 400 V: Irated = 1 000 000/(√3×400) = 1443 A. Fault level ≈ Irated ÷ (%Z/100):

4 % → 36.1 kA — above a 25 kA board. 6 % → 24.1 kA — just inside it (and you would still check the utility’s contribution and the X/R ratio for peak asymmetrical withstand).

A is not wrong about regulation — the 4 % unit really does hold voltage better — but regulation is a comfort issue and short-circuit withstand is a safety one. Comfort never outranks withstand.

D is a legitimate engineering answer that is almost always rejected on cost and losses: you would be paying for a reactor, and its permanent I²R loss, to undo an impedance you could have bought correctly in the first place.

WHY THE TESTS SEPARATE CLEANLY CORE

The elegance of the classical tests is that each one makes one loss negligible. At rated voltage with an open secondary the current is 1–5 % of rated, so copper loss (which goes as I²) is 0.01–0.25 % of full-load copper loss — nothing. With the secondary shorted, rated current flows at only 3–8 % of rated voltage, so the flux — and therefore iron loss — is a few per cent of normal, and its square is negligible. The wattmeter in each test reads essentially one loss alone.

OPEN-CIRCUIT (energise LV at rated V, HV open, measure V0, I0, P0): P0 = IRON LOSS Pi cosφ0 = P0/(V0I0) Iw = I0cosφ0 (core-loss component) Iµ = I0sinφ0 (magnetising) Rc = V0/Iw = V0²/P0 Xm = V0/Iµ SHORT-CIRCUIT (short LV, apply reduced V to HV until rated I flows): Psc = FULL-LOAD COPPER LOSS Pcu,fl Zeq = Vsc/Isc Req = Psc/Isc² Xeq = √(Zeq² − Req²) %Z = (Vsc/Vrated) × 100 — the nameplate impedance, by definition

THE PER-UNIT SYSTEM — WHY EVERY POWER ENGINEER USES IT STRETCH

Per-unit expresses every quantity as a fraction of a chosen base. Its magic property: the per-unit impedance of a transformer is the same seen from either side, because the a² referral factor is exactly cancelled by the (Vbase)² in the base impedance. Networks with a dozen voltage levels collapse into one arithmetic problem.

Choose Sbase (VA) and Vbase (V) at one point. Then: Ibase = Sbase/(√3 Vbase) Zbase = Vbase²/Sbase Zpu = Zactual/Zbase and %Z = 100 × Zpu BASE CHANGE — the formula you will use constantly: Zpu,new = Zpu,old × (Snew/Sold) × (Vold/Vnew)² EXAMPLE: a 6 % impedance on a 800 kVA base, expressed on a 1000 kVA base: Z = 0.06 × (1000/800) = 0.075 pu = 7.5 % — the SAME transformer; only the yardstick changed.

This is the trap in the cold open on page 1. Percentage impedances printed on two different nameplates are quoted on two different bases — each on its own rating. They cannot be compared directly, and they certainly cannot be added. Convert both to a common base first, and the load-sharing arithmetic becomes obvious.

Losses, Efficiency & All-Day Efficiency

One loss is constant and burns all year; the other varies with the square of load. Where they cross is where the machine is at its best — and it is almost never at full load.

THE TWO-BUCKET MODEL CORE

Designers choose the ratio Pi : Pcu,fl to place that peak where the machine actually lives. A distribution transformer, energised 24 hours but lightly loaded much of the day, is designed with low iron loss so its peak sits near 40–60 % load. A power transformer that runs near full load continuously is designed with the peak nearer 80–100 %.

A is the everyday intuition and it is wrong: beyond xopt the copper loss grows faster than the output does. B confuses “half load” with “half loss” — copper loss at half load is a quarter, not a half.

D deserves a careful mark. The optimum load is independent of PF (it cancels in the derivative), but the efficiency value at every load depends on PF strongly, because a poorer PF means less real output for the same losses. Both halves of that sentence are needed for full marks.
ALL-DAY (ENERGY) EFFICIENCY — THE NUMBER THAT PAYS THE BILL CORE

A distribution transformer is energised 8760 hours a year and heavily loaded for perhaps a few hundred of them. Judging it on full-load efficiency flatters it. The honest metric compares kWh out to kWh in over a full daily cycle.

ZERO SEQUENCE — THE PART EVERYONE SKIPS AND SHOULD NOT STRETCH

An earth fault or an unbalanced single-phase load produces zero-sequence current — three phase currents that are equal and in phase, summing in the neutral rather than cancelling (see A30 for the symmetrical-component derivation). Whether that current can flow depends entirely on the winding connection.

  • 1
    A delta winding blocks zero-sequence current from passing through, but lets it circulate inside the delta loop. It therefore acts as a sink: the LV earth fault does not appear as zero-sequence current on the HV line. This is why a Dyn11 gives clean, selective LV earth-fault protection and why HV earth-fault relays do not see LV earth faults.
  • 2
    A star winding without an earthed neutral has no zero-sequence path at all. On a Yyn0 with an unearthed HV star, unbalanced LV load causes the LV neutral point to shift, distorting the phase voltages — one phase rises, another collapses. The classic failure of a “cheap” star-star transformer feeding lighting loads.
  • 3
    The tertiary (stabilising) delta. Large YNyn transformers carry a third, unloaded delta winding whose only job is to provide a path for zero-sequence and third-harmonic currents. It is usually rated about one third of the main rating — and it must never be left with an open corner.
  • 4
    Core construction matters too. A 3-limb core gives zero-sequence flux no iron path (it must return through oil and tank), which itself limits zero-sequence current and heats the tank. A 5-limb core or a bank of three single-phase units provides a full path and behaves quite differently under earth fault.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A factory needs a 400 V, four-wire supply with heavy single-phase lighting and welding loads. Which vector group, and what is the decisive reason?

B — Dyn11. Two independent requirements point to it. The LV must be star with the neutral brought out, or there is no 230 V and no neutral for single-phase loads. The HV should be delta, so that third-harmonic magnetising current and the zero-sequence current produced by unbalanced single-phase load can circulate rather than distorting the phase voltages.

A is the classic false economy: with no delta on either side, a Yyn0 under heavy single-phase load suffers neutral-point shift and third-harmonic voltage distortion — the “one phase reads 260 V and another 195 V” complaint.

C fails on the first requirement — a delta secondary has no neutral, so there is no 230 V supply at all.

D is a sound transmission group but here it is backwards: it puts the delta on the LV side, again leaving no neutral for the loads.

THE AUTOTRANSFORMER — ONE WINDING, TWO JOBS STRETCH

An autotransformer has a single winding with a tapping point; part of it is common to both circuits. Power therefore reaches the output by two paths: transformed inductively through the series section, and conducted directly through the common section. Only the inductive part needs to be built, so the machine is smaller, cheaper and more efficient — and it gives up isolation to get there.

For a ratio a = V₁/V₂ (step-down, a > 1): Swinding = Sthrough × (1 − 1/a) “economy factor” Conducted (untransformed) share = 1/a of the throughput. EXAMPLE — a 33 kV / 22 kV interconnecting autotransformer, 20 MVA through: a = 1.5, 1/a = 0.667 Swinding = 20 × (1 − 0.667) = 6.67 MVA of actual transformer → a third of the material, a third of the losses, for 20 MVA of transfer. The closer the two voltages, the greater the saving — and the more useless the device becomes for isolation.

Where it wins

V₁ ≈ V₂

Grid interconnection (400/220 kV, 33/22 kV), motor starting autotransformers, laboratory variacs, 230/110 V travel converters.

Where it is banned

no isolation

A break in the common winding puts full input voltage on the output. Never use an autotransformer where galvanic separation is a safety function — medical, marine, or any IT earthing system.

Second hazard, often missed: because the impedance of an autotransformer is low, its fault level is correspondingly high. Replacing a two-winding unit with an autotransformer of the same rating can push the downstream fault current above the switchgear rating — check before you save the money.

    Short the CT before you disconnect anything. Test blocks and shorting links exist for this. It is the single most important habit in switchgear work, and the reason CT terminals in a panel carry a warning label.
  • 3
    Polarity dots are not decoration. A reversed CT gives a differential relay a false differential current and a wattmeter a negative reading. Verify with a primary injection or a polarity test at commissioning.

INSULATION, HOT SPOT AND THE 6-DEGREE RULE CORE

The life-limiting material is not the copper or the steel — it is the cellulose paper wrapped around the conductors. Paper degrades by hydrolysis, oxidation and pyrolysis; its chain length (degree of polymerisation, DP) falls from about 1000 when new to around 200, at which point it is brittle and a routine through-fault will tear it. Temperature drives that reaction at an Arrhenius rate, which IEC 60076-7 simplifies to a rule any engineer can use.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A transformer runs at a hot spot of 110 °C instead of the design 98 °C. Roughly what happens to its insulation life?

C — about a quarter. The excess is 12 °C, which is two six-degree doublings: V = 212/6 = 4, so the paper ages four times as fast and a 20-year design life becomes roughly five years. Twelve degrees is a nothing-looking number on a temperature gauge; it is a catastrophic number in an economic model.

A is linear thinking applied to an exponential process — the single most common error in thermal engineering.

D misreads what a thermal class means. The class temperature is a reference for rating, not a cliff edge; degradation is continuous, and it was already happening below 98 °C, just slowly.

WHY DIFFERENTIAL PROTECTION IS HARDER ON A TRANSFORMER STRETCH

A differential relay compares current in with current out. On a busbar that is trivial. On a transformer, four things conspire to make the comparison unequal even when the machine is perfectly healthy — and each has an engineered answer.

  • 1
    The currents differ in magnitude (that is the transformer’s job) and in phase (30° for a Dy group). Answer: ratio matching and vector-group compensation — historically by connecting the CTs in the opposite configuration to the winding (delta CTs on the star side), now done numerically in software by specifying the vector group in the relay.
  • 2
    Magnetising inrush looks exactly like an internal fault — large current in, none out (page 5). Answer: second-harmonic restraint. Inrush is rich in second harmonic (typically 15–20 %); genuine fault current is not. Modern relays block or restrain the trip when the second-harmonic ratio exceeds the setting.
  • 3
    Over-excitation also produces false differential current from magnetising current. Answer: fifth-harmonic restraint, since over-fluxing is rich in the fifth.
  • 4
    CT errors and tap changes produce a small differential current that grows with through current. Answer: a percentage bias (restraint) characteristic — the operating threshold rises with the through current, so the relay stays sensitive at low current and stable during heavy external faults when CTs may saturate unequally.

THE EIGHT IDEAS CORE
  • 1
    Flux is the currency. E = 4.44 fNΦm, so Bm follows V/f, not V. Every saturation, inrush and import problem descends from that ratio.
  • 2
    Volts divide by a, amps multiply by a, impedance by a². The squared term is the one that gets forgotten — and the one that makes fault studies work.
  • 3
    Iron loss is constant and load-independent; copper loss goes as load squared. Efficiency therefore peaks at x = √(Pi/Pcu,fl), which is rarely full load.
  • 4
    %Z is one number with two meanings: volt-drop under load, and fault current under short circuit. Choosing it is choosing your switchgear.
  • 5
    Two transformers share load as (kVA/%Z), not as kVA. Mismatched impedance strands capacity you have already paid for.
  • 6
    The vector group is not a formality. It decides paralleling, earth-fault behaviour, harmonic circulation and how the differential relay must be configured.
  • 7
    Transformers die of heat, on an exponential clock. +6 °C of hot spot halves the remaining life; a 30 °C ambient triples the ageing rate at full load.
  • 8
    The oil is the medical record. DGA finds faults months before any relay does — and trend beats absolute value every time.
SIX WAYS REAL TRANSFORMERS FAIL
1 · Thermal ageing to brittleness
Years of hot-spot excess embrittle the paper (DP → 200). Nothing happens until a routine through fault mechanically shakes the winding and the insulation tears. Fingerprint: rising CO/CO₂ and 2-FAL for years, then a sudden earth fault. Prevention: ambient correction at purchase, load management, clean radiators, working fans.
2 · Moisture ingress through a dead breather
Silica gel saturates, the transformer breathes damp air on every cooling cycle, water enters the oil and migrates into the paper. Fingerprint: falling BDV, rising water ppm, falling PI. Prevention: the cheapest maintenance item there is — check the breather colour monthly and change the gel.
3 · Through-fault mechanical damage
A close-in short circuit produces forces proportional to I², i.e. hundreds of times normal, trying to blow the windings apart radially and compress them axially. Clamping loosens; the next fault finishes the job. Fingerprint: SFRA deviation from the baseline. Prevention: fast, well-coordinated downstream protection — fault duration is the one variable you control.
4 · Bushing failure
The most common cause of catastrophic, sometimes explosive, transformer failure. Capacitive layers degrade, tan δ climbs, then a flashover. Fingerprint: rising tan δ, thermographic hot spot at the bushing (see A41). Prevention: tan δ trending and infrared survey on a schedule.
5 · Harmonic overheating
Non-linear load fills the winding with harmonic current; eddy losses rise with h² and triplen currents pile up in the neutral. The transformer runs hot at “70 % load” on an ordinary ammeter. Fingerprint: high measured THDi, neutral current comparable with phase current. Prevention: K-rated or derated unit; see A56.
6 · Wrong-group paralleling
Two units with different clock numbers (or reversed phase sequence) are closed onto a common bus. A 30–60° voltage difference across roughly 10 % impedance is a bolted fault in all but name. Fingerprint: immediate, violent, and usually terminal for both machines. Prevention: read both nameplates; measure across the open coupler before closing it.
✓ QUICK CHECK — SIX QUESTIONS

Q1: A 60 Hz transformer is imported and energised at its rated voltage on a 50 Hz supply. What happens, and why?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Bm = V/(4.44 f N A), so at constant voltage a lower frequency means higher flux: 60/50 = 1.2, a 20 % increase. A core designed near 1.6 T is pushed towards 1.9 T, into saturation — a large, distorted magnetising current, audible hum, core and winding heating, and probable nuisance tripping. The unit must be operated at 50/60 × rated voltage (i.e. about 83 %) to preserve the V/f ratio, which usually makes it useless for the intended duty.

Q2: A 1000 kVA, 400 V transformer has %Z = 4. Your switchboard is rated 25 kA. Is that acceptable?

Tap to reveal ↓A: No. Irated = 1 000 000/(√3×400) = 1443 A; Ifault ≈ 1443 × 100/4 = 36.1 kA, well above the 25 kA rating — and that is before adding the utility source contribution and motor contribution, and before considering the asymmetrical first peak (up to about 2.8× the RMS value for high X/R). Either specify a higher %Z (6 % gives 24.1 kA), or buy a board rated for the real fault level.

Q3: Why is iron loss measured with the secondary open, and copper loss with it shorted?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Each test makes the other loss negligible. Open-circuit at rated voltage: full flux (so full iron loss) but only 1–5 % of rated current, whose I²R contribution is 0.01–0.25 % of full-load copper loss. Short-circuit at rated current: full copper loss but only 3–8 % of rated voltage, so the flux — and the iron loss that depends on it — is a few per cent of normal. Each wattmeter therefore reads essentially one loss alone.

Q4: Two transformers, 1000 kVA at 5 % and 500 kVA at 5 %, are paralleled. Total load is 1200 kVA. How does it divide, and is either overloaded?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Equal %Z means sharing in proportion to nameplate: ratios 1000/5 = 200 and 500/5 = 100, sum 300. So T1 takes 1200×200/300 = 800 kVA (80 %) and T2 takes 1200×100/300 = 400 kVA (80 %). Neither is overloaded, both sit at the same utilisation, and the full 1500 kVA of installed capacity is usable. This is what matched impedance buys you — contrast with page 1, where 2050 kVA of installed plant delivered only 1633.

Q5: A transformer differential relay operates every time the transformer is energised, but the machine tests perfectly. What is happening and what is the fix?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Magnetising inrush. On energisation the primary draws 8–12× rated current while the secondary draws none — which is precisely the signature of an internal fault as far as a plain differential comparison is concerned. The fix is second-harmonic restraint: inrush current typically contains 15–20 % second harmonic while genuine fault current does not, so the relay blocks or restrains when that ratio is exceeded. Check that the restraint is enabled and its threshold is sensible (commonly 15–20 %) before suspecting the transformer. A related possibility on a lightly damped system is sympathetic inrush from a neighbouring unit.

Q6: A distribution transformer serving a plant full of VFDs and LED drivers runs at its temperature alarm although the ammeter shows only 70 % load. Explain, and give two actions.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Harmonic current. Eddy-current loss in the windings rises roughly with h², so a current that is 70 % of rating in RMS terms but rich in the 5th, 7th and 11th harmonics produces far more heating than a clean 70 % would. In addition, triplen (3rd, 9th) currents are zero-sequence and add arithmetically in the neutral, which can exceed phase current in a four-wire system with heavy single-phase electronic load.

Actions: (1) measure — a true-RMS clamp plus a power-quality logger on the phases and the neutral, and compute THDi and the K-factor; (2) derate or replace with a K-rated unit, and/or fit harmonic mitigation (detuned capacitor stages, line reactors on the drives, or an active filter). Do the measurement first: derating a transformer that is simply overloaded, or replacing one whose real problem is a blocked ventilation louvre, both waste money. See A56.

P5  [10 marks] · CIRCULATING CURRENT FROM A TAP MISMATCH

Two identical 1000 kVA, 400 V transformers with %Z = 5.5 are paralleled, but one has been left on a tap 2.5 % different from the other. Find the circulating current in per-unit and in amperes, and state two consequences.

Tap to reveal ↓ΔE = 0.025 pu; loop impedance = Z₁ + Z₂ = 0.055 + 0.055 = 0.110 pu.
Icirc = 0.025/0.110 = 0.227 pu
Irated = 1 000 000/(√3 × 400) = 1443 A → Icirc = 328 A, flowing at NO LOAD.

Consequence 1 — heat for nothing. Extra copper loss in each unit ≈ (0.227)² = 5.2 % of full-load copper loss, present continuously and doing no work.
Consequence 2 — lost capacity. The circulating current is almost purely reactive, so it adds to the load current roughly in quadrature: at full load a unit carries √(1443² + 328²) = 1480 A, about 2.5 % over rating, and reaches its thermal and protection limits early.

Third mark-earning point: the current is limited only by the transformer impedances, so the lower the %Z, the worse a given tap mismatch becomes — and a 5 % ratio error (two tap steps) on these units would give 0.45 pu, i.e. 650 A of pure circulation.

P7  [8 marks] · A VECTOR-GROUP REFUSAL, WITH NUMBERS

A plant has a 1000 kVA Dyn11 transformer, %Z = 5. A supplier offers a surplus 1000 kVA Dyn1 unit, %Z = 5, at a large discount, “identical apart from the label”. Justify your refusal quantitatively.

Tap to reveal ↓Clock 11 means the LV leads the HV by 30°; clock 1 means it lags by 30°. The two secondaries are therefore 60° apart.

Voltage difference across the paralleled loop:
ΔV = 2V sin(60°/2) = 2V(0.5) = 1.0 pu — a full rated voltage
Loop impedance = 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.10 pu
Icirc = 1.0/0.10 = 10 pu = ten times rated current, continuously, with no load connected.

That is the magnitude of a bolted three-phase fault. The units would trip immediately if the protection is correct, and be destroyed if it is not. There is no adjustment, tap, reactor or relay setting that makes this acceptable — the phase displacement is built into the winding connections.

What can be done: use the Dyn1 unit on a separate, permanently split section of the board where it never parallels with the Dyn11; or have the manufacturer re-connect and re-label it (possible on some designs, and it is then a factory job with new test certificates, not a site job). P8  [10 marks] · READING A DGA REPORT

Latest oil sample from a 10 MVA, 33/11 kV transformer (ppm), with last year’s figures in brackets: H₂ 180 (60), CH₄ 90 (45), C₂H₆ 40 (30), C₂H₄ 220 (70), C₂H₂ 35 (2), CO 620 (400), CO₂ 5400 (4100). Interpret and give an action plan in priority order.

Tap to reveal ↓Interpretation. Two signals dominate. Acetylene has gone from 2 to 35 ppm — C₂H₂ is produced only at arcing temperatures, so this indicates a high-energy discharge (Duval zone D1/D2). Simultaneously ethylene has more than tripled, confirming high-temperature involvement, and the rising CO with a falling CO₂/CO ratio shows cellulose is being degraded, not just oil. Hydrogen tripling is consistent with both discharge and partial discharge activity.

The combination — arcing plus paper involvement plus a steep rate of change — is the serious end of the spectrum. Rate of increase is the alarming feature, more than any single absolute value.

Action plan, in order:
1. Re-sample immediately, ideally two samples by different people, to exclude a sampling or laboratory error. Confirm which compartment was sampled.
2. Check whether the unit has an OLTC and whether the sample could have come from — or been contaminated by — the diverter compartment, where acetylene is normal. A leaking barrier between compartments explains many false alarms.
3. Reduce load and increase monitoring frequency (weekly, or continuous online DGA if available). Review recent through-fault history and any recent switching.
4. Plan an outage for electrical tests: turns ratio on all taps, winding resistance, excitation current, tan δ, and SFRA against the fingerprint to detect winding movement.
5. Prepare contingency — spare or rental unit, load transfer plan — before you need it, because the outage may become unplanned.
6. Involve the manufacturer and a specialist; consider IEEE C57.104 condition classification and IEC 60599 ratio methods for a documented second opinion.

What NOT to do: do not degas or reprocess the oil first. It removes the evidence and treats the symptom, and the fault will simply regenerate the gases.
P9  [10 marks] · TRIPLENS AND THE NEUTRAL

A four-wire LV board carries 300 A of fundamental per phase, with a 3rd harmonic at 35 %, a 5th at 15 % and a 7th at 9 % of fundamental, balanced across the three phases. Find the neutral current and the phase rms current, estimate the K-factor, and say what the transformer sees.

Tap to reveal ↓Neutral. Triplen harmonics are zero-sequence — they are in phase in all three conductors and therefore add arithmetically in the neutral:
I₃ per phase = 0.35 × 300 = 105 A → IN = 3 × 105 = 315 A

Combined with eddy losses rising as h², the unit runs hotter than the LV ammeter suggests.

Actions: uprate or double the neutral conductor (and check the neutral link and busbar rating in the board), verify the transformer against its K-rating, and attack the source — single-phase electronic loads — with a zig-zag filter or by rebalancing across phases. See A56.
P10  [12 marks] · SYNTHESIS — A SUBSTATION IN TROUBLE

A 1600/2000 kVA ONAN/ONAF transformer serves a factory. Findings: winding temperature alarm most afternoons; measured demand 1750 kVA; the fan contactor was isolated last year because it chattered; the transformer room louvres are partly blocked by stored pallets; the LV neutral current measures 60 % of phase current; the silica-gel breather is entirely pink; the last DGA was three years ago and was normal. Diagnose in priority order, state what you would do this week and this quarter, and name one thing you would refuse to do.

Tap to reveal ↓Diagnosis — there is no single fault; there are four, and they compound.

1. The unit is operating outside its rating. 1750 kVA is above the 1600 kVA ONAN rating; the 2000 kVA figure requires the fans, and the fans are isolated. So the transformer is at about 109 % of its actual cooling class, not 88 % of its nameplate.
2. The ambient is elevated. Blocked louvres raise room temperature; every 6 °C of extra hot spot doubles the ageing rate (page 13). Combined with (1), the hot spot is likely well above 108 °C.
3. Harmonic loading. A neutral at 60 % of phase current means substantial triplen content, so the true heating is worse than the kVA suggests, and the transformer’s delta winding is carrying circulating triplen current.
4. Moisture ingress. A fully pink breather has been saturated for an unknown time; the transformer has been breathing damp Sri Lankan air on every load cycle. Expect reduced BDV and paper hydrolysis accelerated by the high temperature.

This week (free or nearly free, and in this order):
· Clear the louvres and restore ventilation. Measure room temperature before and after.
· Change the silica gel and check the oil level and the breather oil seal.
· Repair or replace the fan contactor and prove the fans start on the thermostat — this alone restores 400 kVA of rating.
· Take an oil sample: DGA plus BDV, moisture and acidity. Three years without a sample on a stressed unit is the real management failure here.
· Log load and temperature continuously for a week rather than relying on the afternoon alarm.

This quarter:
· Power-quality survey (phases and neutral); compute THDi and K-factor; plan mitigation or a K-rated replacement.
· Reassess the rating against the true ambient (IEC 60076-7 cyclic loading), and plan either load transfer or a second transformer — specifying its %Z to match if it will ever be paralleled (page 11).
· Establish a maintenance standard: breather monthly, thermography quarterly, DGA annually, and a commissioning-style test set with an SFRA fingerprint.

What I would refuse: to silence or raise the temperature alarm setting so the afternoon nuisance stops. The alarm is the only honest instrument in this story, and every other finding says it is telling the truth. I would also refuse to declare the transformer “fine because it has not tripped” — thermal ageing produces no symptom until the insulation tears during an unrelated through fault.

Marking: 2 marks per correct diagnosis with its mechanism (8), 2 for a sensibly ordered week-one plan, 2 for the refusal with justification.

Derivations & Traps

Four results the module leaned on, rebuilt from first principles — because a formula you can reconstruct is one you will still trust when the answer looks wrong.

DERIVATION 1 · WHERE 4.44 COMES FROM — AND WHEN IT IS NOT 4.44 CORE
Faraday: e(t) = −N dΦ/dt Assume a sinusoidal flux (which is what a sinusoidal applied voltage forces): Φ(t) = Φm sinωt e(t) = −N Φm ω cosωt → amplitude Em = NωΦm For any sinusoid, RMS = amplitude/√2 : E = N ω Φm/√2 = N (2πf) Φm/√2 = (2π/√2) f N Φm 2π/√2 = 6.2832/1.4142 = 4.4429 E = 4.44 f N Φm = 4.44 f N Bm A WHERE THE CONSTANT COMES FROM, SEEN ANOTHER WAY: Average |e| over a half cycle = 4 f N Φm (pure integration, no waveform assumed) RMS = FORM FACTOR × average, and for a sine the form factor is (1/√2)/(2/π) = π/(2√2) = 1.1107 → E = 1.1107 × 4 f N Φm = 4.44 f N Φm So 4.44 = 4 × the form factor of the waveform. Change the waveform and the constant changes with it: sine form factor 1.11 → 4.44 square form factor 1.00 → 4.00 (SMPS transformers) peaky wave form factor >1.11 → <4.44 for the same RMS voltage → MORE flux for the same voltmeter reading

The consequence that catches people out. On a distorted supply, an ordinary RMS voltmeter no longer tells you the flux. A flat-topped waveform (the usual signature of heavy rectifier load, A56) has a lower peak for the same RMS, so it produces less peak flux and the transformer is magnetically relaxed. A peaky waveform does the opposite and can push a core near its design limit into saturation at a perfectly normal RMS voltage. This is why flux-sensitive equipment is specified against a peak or a volt-second limit, not an RMS one.

DERIVATION 2 · WHY MAXIMUM EFFICIENCY IS WHERE THE LOSSES ARE EQUAL CORE
Maximising η is the same as minimising (total loss)/(output) — easier algebra: L(x) = [Pi + x²Pcu,fl] / [x S cosφ] = Pi/(x S cosφ) + x Pcu,fl/(S cosφ) \_____________/ \_______________/ falls as 1/x rises as x dL/dx = −Pi/(x² S cosφ) + Pcu,fl/(S cosφ) = 0 → Pcu,fl = Pi/x² → x²Pcu,fl = Pi → xopt = √(Pi/Pcu,fl) Second derivative d²L/dx² = +2Pi/(x³Scosφ) > 0 → it is a MINIMUM of loss-per-output, i.e. a MAXIMUM of efficiency. ✓ TWO COROLLARIES THAT EARN MARKS: 1. cosφ CANCELLED. The optimum LOAD is independent of power factor; the efficiency VALUE at that load is not. 2. The condition is "variable loss = fixed loss", which is a GENERAL result: it reappears for transmission lines, motors, and any system with one constant and one square-law loss term.

This is also the design lever. A manufacturer choosing Pi : Pcu,fl = 1:8 puts the peak at 35 % load (a distribution unit that idles most of the day); a ratio of 1:1 puts it at 100 % (a unit expected to run flat out). Ask a supplier where the efficiency peak sits and you learn what duty the transformer was designed for — a far better question than “what is the efficiency?”

DERIVATION 3 · THE LOAD-SHARING LAW STRETCH
Two transformers on a common bus. They share the SAME no-load voltage E and the SAME terminal voltage V, so they must have the same volt-drop: E − V = I₁Z₁ = I₂Z₂ (ohms, referred to the same side) → I₁/I₂ = Z₂/Z₁ — current divides INVERSELY with impedance. Now convert to nameplate quantities. Each transformer's %Z is quoted on ITS OWN rating, so on a COMMON base Sb: Zpu,common = (%Z/100) × (Sb/Srated) Substituting, and with the same terminal voltage so S ∝ I: S₁/S₂ = Z2,pu/Z1,pu = [(%Z₂/100)(Sb/S2r)] / [(%Z₁/100)(Sb/S1r)] S₁/S₂ = (S1r/%Z₁) ÷ (S2r/%Z₂) SPECIAL CASES THAT MAKE IT MEMORABLE: %Z₁ = %Z₂ → S₁/S₂ = S1r/S2r: perfect proportional sharing. Equal ratings, %Z₁<%Z₂ → the LOWER-impedance unit takes MORE load. Utilisation of unit i = (Si/Sir) ∝ 1/%Zi → utilisation depends ONLY on %Z, not on rating at all. That last line is the whole of page 1 in one sentence: the unit with the lowest %Z always reaches 100 % first, whatever size it is.
DERIVATION 4 · WHY PER-UNIT IMPEDANCE IS THE SAME ON BOTH SIDES STRETCH
Take an impedance Z₂ on the secondary. Referred to the primary it becomes a²Z₂. Now express each in per-unit on its own side, with the SAME Sbase: Zbase,1 = V₁²/Sbase Zbase,2 = V₂²/Sbase Zpu (primary side) = a²Z₂ / (V₁²/Sbase) and V₁ = aV₂, so V₁² = a²V₂² = a²Z₂ Sbase / (a²V₂²) = Z₂ Sbase/V₂² = Zpu (secondary side) ✓ identical The a² in the referral is exactly cancelled by the a² hidden in the base. CONSEQUENCE: in a per-unit network diagram the transformer becomes a plain series impedance with NO ratio at all. Voltage levels disappear; a 220 kV line, a 33 kV transformer and a 400 V busbar all live in the same arithmetic. That is why every fault study, load flow and stability program on earth works internally in per-unit — and why %Z, which is just 100×Zpu, is the number printed on the nameplate rather than a value in ohms.

One caution. The cancellation is exact only when the base voltages are chosen in the ratio of the transformer’s turns. If you pick base voltages that do not respect the ratio — or forget an off-nominal tap — a residual ideal transformer reappears in the per-unit model, and your fault currents will be quietly wrong. Choose the base voltages by walking through the network transformer by transformer.

  • 2
    For the power-electronics side of the family: Transformers and Inductors for Power Electronics: Theory, Design and Applications (in your material folder) — the high-frequency, small-core end, where the same E = 4.44 fNBmA governs a ferrite the size of a coin. Essential if you work with SMPS, drives or welding sets.
  • 3
    For installation practice: Electrical Installation Designs, 4th ed. (in your material folder) — substation layout, protection coordination, cable and switchgear selection: the context a transformer actually lives in.
  • 4
    The two industry references worth buying (not in the folder): The J&P Transformer Book (Heathcote) — the practising engineer’s handbook on construction, oil, testing, maintenance and failure; and Power Transformers: Principles and Applications (Winders) — short, clear, and unusually good on paralleling and loading.
  • 5
    For design depth (research tier): Transformer Design Principles (Del Vecchio et al.) — electromagnetic and mechanical design, short-circuit forces, impedance calculation from geometry.
  • 6
    The standards to know by number — IEC 60076 series: -1 general, -2 temperature rise, -3 insulation levels and dielectric tests, -5 short-circuit withstand, -7 loading guide for oil-immersed transformers (the source of the thermal model on page 13), -10 sound levels, -11 dry type. Plus IEC 60599 and IEEE C57.104 for DGA interpretation, IEC 60156 and 60296 for oil, and the efficiency regimes (EU EcoDesign 548/2014, IS 1180) that increasingly drive what is available to buy.

HONEST GAPS — WHAT THIS MODULE DID NOT COVER

Four areas were deliberately left out, and you should know they exist. Electromagnetic design from geometry — computing impedance, forces and losses from winding dimensions and finite-element models — belongs to the manufacturer and to Del Vecchio. Transient and impulse behaviour — lightning impulse distribution along a winding, inter-turn stress, surge arrester coordination and the dielectric tests of IEC 60076-3 — is a specialist field in itself. Ferroresonance was named but not analysed; it is a genuinely non-linear phenomenon that deserves its own treatment. And HVDC converter transformers, with their DC bias, harmonic duty and special insulation, are an advanced topic that builds on everything here.

What you do have is the complete working set: the physics, the circuit, the performance calculations, the system behaviour, the thermal life model and the diagnostic vocabulary — enough to specify, evaluate, operate and defend a transformer in any industrial setting, and to know precisely when to call the specialist.
