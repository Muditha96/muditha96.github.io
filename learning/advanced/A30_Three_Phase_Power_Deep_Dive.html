Why Three Phases at All?

One generator, three windings, 120° apart — the most efficient way ever found to move bulk power.

Definition

Three-phase power delivers energy on three AC conductors whose voltages are equal in magnitude but offset in time by 120° (one-third of a cycle). That single arrangement gives constant total power, a self-starting rotating magnetic field, and ~50% less copper than three separate single-phase circuits would need.

Pioneered by Nikola Tesla, Galileo Ferraris and Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrowolsky in the 1880s–90s, the polyphase system won the "War of the Currents" because it could be generated, transmitted, transformed and converted to torque with no moving contacts. Today essentially all generation, transmission and industrial loads above a few kilowatts are three-phase — including your micro card's VFD, servo and compressor drives.

The core idea to hold onto: a single-phase supply delivers power that pulses to zero twice per cycle. Add two more phases spaced 120° apart and, at every instant, what one phase loses another gains — the sum is a smooth, constant value. That is the whole reason three-phase exists.

Phasors & the 120° Mathematics

Three rotating vectors that always sum to zero — the algebra behind the smooth power.

The Three Instantaneous Voltages

Each phase is a sine wave of the same peak Vm and frequency, shifted by 120°:

v₁(t) = Vm·sin(ωt) v₂(t) = Vm·sin(ωt − 120°) v₃(t) = Vm·sin(ωt − 240°) where ω = 2πf Key identity (balanced system): v₁ + v₂ + v₃ = 0 at every instant → the three phasors form a closed triangle; their vector sum is zero.

Because the three add to zero, the neutral conductor carries no current in a perfectly balanced load — a fact that lets utilities run a thin neutral (or none at all in delta). This is also why a balanced three-phase load needs no return path proportional to the phase currents.

Line vs Phase — The Two Voltages Everyone Confuses

"400/230 V" isn't two systems — it's one system measured two ways.

The Distinction

Phase quantity = measured across one winding/load (line-to-neutral in a wye). Line quantity = measured between two lines (line-to-line). Because two phase voltages 120° apart subtract as phasors, the line value is √3 larger:

In a WYE (star) connection: V_line = √3 × V_phase I_line = I_phase e.g. V_ph = 230 V → V_L = √3·230 ≈ 400 V In a DELTA connection: V_line = V_phase I_line = √3 × I_phase The √3 always appears once — on voltage (wye) or on current (delta).

So a "400 V / 230 V" supply is a single wye system: 400 V between any two lines, 230 V from any line to neutral. North-American "208 V / 120 V" is the same idea (√3·120 ≈ 208). The √3 is just the geometry of subtracting two unit vectors 120° apart: |1∠0 − 1∠−120°| = √3.

Where √3 Comes From — Geometrically
Line voltage is the phasor difference of two phase voltages — a 2·cos(30°) = √3 relationship. No magic, just trigonometry.

The √3 Power Triangle

Real, reactive and apparent power — and why three-phase power never pulses.

The Three Powers

AC loads draw current that may lag (inductive) or lead (capacitive) the voltage by angle φ. That splits power into the famous power triangle:

Active (real) power: P = √3·V_L·I_L·cos φ [W] ← does work Reactive power: Q = √3·V_L·I_L·sin φ [VAR] ← stores/returns Apparent power: S = √3·V_L·I_L [VA] ← what the cable carries Relationship: S² = P² + Q² Power factor PF = cos φ = P / S

cos φ is the power factor. A motor at PF 0.8 draws 25% more current than its real power suggests — that "wasted" reactive current still heats your cables and is billed by utilities. (Power-factor correction, page 9, fixes it.)

PF = cos φ = P/S. Shrink φ (correct the PF) and S → P: the cable carries only useful current.
Why Total Power Is Constant — The Headline Result

Sum the instantaneous power p = v·i over all three phases of a balanced load. The pulsating 2ω terms (each at the same frequency but 120° apart) cancel exactly, leaving a constant:

p₁+p₂+p₃ = 3·V_ph·I_ph·cos φ = constant (no 2ω ripple!) Single phase: p(t) = V·I·cos φ − V·I·cos(2ωt+φ) ← pulses to ~0 Three phase: the three 2ωt terms sum to zero ← perfectly smooth

This is the deep "why." A single-phase motor's torque pulses 100/120 times a second (hence vibration and the need for capacitors); a three-phase motor receives uniform power and therefore uniform torque — smoother, quieter, and self-starting (page 6).

The Rotating Magnetic Field

The crown jewel: three fixed coils + three phases = a magnetic field that spins on its own.

How Stationary Coils Make a Spinning Field

Place three stator windings 120° apart in space and feed them the three phase currents (120° apart in time). Each coil's field pulses along its own axis; added vectorially, the resultant is a field of constant magnitude that rotates at the supply frequency. This is the Ferraris–Tesla rotating field — the reason a three-phase motor needs no brushes, commutator or starting capacitor.

As L1→L2→L3 rise and fall in turn, the field vector sweeps around the bore. The rotor is dragged along behind it — no electrical connection to the rotor needed.
Synchronous Speed — How Fast the Field Spins

The field's rotation speed depends only on supply frequency and the number of magnetic pole pairs wound into the stator:

n_s = 120·f / P [rpm] (P = number of poles, f in Hz) 50 Hz, 2-pole: n_s = 3000 rpm 60 Hz, 2-pole: 3600 rpm 50 Hz, 4-pole: 1500 rpm 60 Hz, 4-pole: 1800 rpm 50 Hz, 6-pole: 1000 rpm 60 Hz, 6-pole: 1200 rpm

A synchronous motor (or alternator) locks exactly to n_s. An induction motor (page 7) runs slightly slower — that gap is "slip," and it's what makes induction torque possible. A VFD changes f to set n_s and therefore speed, which is exactly how variable-speed drives work.

The Induction Motor & Slip

The workhorse that converts three-phase electricity into ~90% of the world's industrial torque.

Why the Rotor Must Lag — Slip

In a squirrel-cage induction motor the rotor has no external supply. The rotating stator field sweeps past the rotor bars, induces a voltage and current in them (Faraday), and that current — interacting with the field — produces torque (Lorentz). But if the rotor ever caught up to synchronous speed, the bars would see no relative motion, induce nothing, and make no torque. So the rotor must always run a little slower. That lag is slip:

slip s = (n_s − n_r) / n_s (typically 1–5% at full load) Rotor speed: n_r = n_s·(1 − s) Rotor (slip) freq: f_r = s·f Full-load example: 1500 rpm field, 1450 rpm rotor → s ≈ 3.3%

At standstill s = 1 (max induction, high inrush current ~6× rated); as the rotor accelerates, slip falls and current drops to its running value. This is why direct-on-line starting causes a large inrush — and why we use star-delta or soft starters (page 8).

The Torque–Speed Curve
The motor settles where load torque crosses the curve's steep right-hand (stable) branch — just below n_s. Push past breakdown torque and it stalls.
Connection to your micro card & VFD

Your micro card says "motors self-start — no starter needed." Now you know why: the rotating field provides the starting torque automatically. And the VFD deep dive (A08) exploits n_s = 120f/P — vary f and the whole torque-speed curve shifts, giving smooth speed control without changing the motor.

Star-Delta Starting

The classic trick to tame motor inrush — and the √3 maths that explains the famous "⅓".

The Problem & the Fix

Direct-on-line (DOL) start draws ~6–8× rated current and a large torque shock. Star-delta starting connects the motor windings in star to start (each winding sees only V_L/√3) then switches to delta to run (full line voltage). Because torque and current scale with voltage², the star step cuts both starting current and starting torque to one-third of the DOL delta values:

Voltage per winding in star: V_ph(star) = V_L / √3 Since I ∝ V and T ∝ V² : I_star = ⅓ · I_delta T_start(star) = ⅓ · T_start(delta) Derivation: (V_L/√3)² / V_L² = 1/3 ← the √3 squared = 3

The catch: you also lose ⅔ of the starting torque, so star-delta only suits loads that start unloaded (pumps with open valves, fans, conveyors). For loaded starts, a VFD or soft-starter is the modern choice — it ramps voltage/frequency smoothly with full torque control.

Power Factor Correction & Harmonics

Two invisible costs in every factory — and how engineers cancel them.

Power-Factor Correction (PFC)

Inductive loads (motors, transformers) draw lagging reactive power Q. Adding capacitors in parallel supplies leading VARs that cancel the lagging ones, shrinking S toward P — so the same real work flows on less current. Benefits: lower line losses (I²R), freed-up transformer/cable capacity, and avoidance of utility PF penalties.

Capacitor kVAR needed to go from PF₁ → PF₂: Q_c = P · (tan φ₁ − tan φ₂) Example: 100 kW load, PF 0.75 → 0.95 tan(acos 0.75)=0.882 , tan(acos 0.95)=0.329 Q_c = 100·(0.882 − 0.329) ≈ 55 kVAR of capacitors

Caution: over-correction makes the system leading and can resonate with line inductance. Modern plants use automatic PF controllers that switch capacitor banks in steps, and detuned reactors to avoid harmonic resonance (below).

Harmonics — The Non-Linear-Load Problem

Rectifiers, VFDs and switch-mode supplies draw current in pulses, not sine waves. Those distorted currents contain harmonics (multiples of 50/60 Hz) that overheat neutrals and transformers, trip breakers and distort voltage. The standard metric is THD:

THD = √(ΣI²ₙ) / I₁ (n = 2,3,5,7…) Triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th…) add up in the NEUTRAL of a wye system → a balanced load can still have a neutral hotter than the phases! Mitigation: delta windings (trap triplens), line reactors, 12-pulse rectifiers, active/passive filters, IEEE 519 limits.

Triplen trap

The delta winding circulates 3rd-harmonic currents so they don't reach the line — a hidden job of that Dyn11 transformer.

IEEE 519

The reference standard capping voltage & current THD at the point of common coupling.

Faults, Protection, Recap & Quiz

How three-phase systems fail — and the one-line truths to carry away.

How Three-Phase Systems Fail
⚡ Single-phasing

One line lost (blown fuse/loose lug). The motor runs on two phases, current surges, windings cook. A phase-loss relay must trip it.

🔀 Phase-sequence reversal

Two lines swapped → motor & pump spin backwards. A phase-sequence relay blocks the start.

⚖️ Voltage unbalance

Even 2% unbalance causes ~6–10% extra heating (negative-sequence currents). Derate or fix the source.

🔥 Earth / ground fault

Insulation breakdown to frame. Earth-fault (RCD/ground) protection and proper bonding catch it.

💥 Line-to-line / 3-φ short

Massive fault current; clearing relies on correctly graded breakers & fuses (discrimination).

🌡️ Neutral overload (triplens)

Harmonic-rich loads overheat a "lightly loaded" neutral. Oversize the neutral or filter.

One-Page Recap
  • 1Three voltages, 120° apart, always sum to zero → constant power, constant torque.
  • 2The √3 links line & phase: wye → V_L=√3·V_ph; delta → I_L=√3·I_ph.
  • 3Power triangle: P=√3·V_L·I_L·cosφ, with S²=P²+Q² and PF=cosφ.
  • 4Three coils + three phases = a rotating magnetic field at n_s=120f/P — self-starting motors.
  • 5Slip makes induction torque; star-delta cuts start current/torque to ⅓ (√3²); correct PF & tame harmonics.
Quick Check — Tap to Reveal

Q1: Why is total three-phase power constant while single-phase power pulses to zero?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Each phase's instantaneous power has a constant term plus a 2ω pulsation. The three pulsation terms are 120° apart and sum to exactly zero, leaving only the constant 3·V_ph·I_ph·cosφ. Single-phase has no partners to cancel its 2ω term, so it dips to zero twice per cycle.

Q2: A wye supply reads 230 V line-to-neutral. What is the line-to-line voltage, and why?

Tap to reveal ↓A: √3·230 ≈ 400 V. Line voltage is the phasor difference of two phase voltages 120° apart: |V₁−V₂| = 2·V_ph·cos30° = √3·V_ph.

Q3: Why must an induction-motor rotor always run slower than synchronous speed?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Torque comes from current induced in the rotor bars by the rotating field. That induction requires relative motion (slip). At synchronous speed there'd be no relative motion, no induced current, no torque — so the rotor settles a few % below n_s.

Q4: Star-delta starting cuts both start current and torque to one-third. Where does the "⅓" come from?

Tap to reveal ↓A: In star each winding sees V_L/√3. Current ∝ V (÷√3) and torque ∝ V² (÷3). The (1/√3)² = 1/3 factor cuts current to ⅓ and torque to ⅓ vs the delta (running) connection.

Q5: A balanced wye load draws no neutral current — yet the neutral overheats. How?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th…) from non-linear loads are in-phase across all three phases, so instead of cancelling they add in the neutral — which can carry more than the phase conductors. Fix with delta windings, filters, or an oversized neutral.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals worked line by line, each closing with the insight the examiner is really testing.

WE1 · Delta Load — Line vs Phase, and the Full Power Triangle

A balanced delta-connected load of Z = 20 + j15 Ω per phase is supplied at 415 V, 50 Hz. Find phase current, line current, P, Q, S and power factor. Then repeat the load as wye-connected on the same supply and compare.

DELTA CONNECTION |Z| = √(20² + 15²) = √625 = 25 Ω φ = arctan(15/20) = 36.87° → cosφ = 0.80 lagging In delta, each phase sees the FULL line voltage: V_ph = V_L = 415 V I_ph = V_ph / |Z| = 415 / 25 = 16.6 A I_L = √3 · I_ph = 1.732 × 16.6 = 28.75 A P = √3 · V_L · I_L · cosφ = 1.732 × 415 × 28.75 × 0.80 = 16 534 W ≈ 16.5 kW Q = √3 · V_L · I_L · sinφ = 1.732 × 415 × 28.75 × 0.60 = 12 401 var ≈ 12.4 kvar S = √3 · V_L · I_L = 1.732 × 415 × 28.75 = 20 667 VA ≈ 20.7 kVA Check: √(16.5² + 12.4²) = √(272 + 154) = √426 = 20.6 kVA ✓ Cross-check per phase: P = 3 · I_ph² · R = 3 × 16.6² × 20 = 16 534 W ✓ WYE CONNECTION (same load impedances, same supply) V_ph = V_L / √3 = 415 / 1.732 = 239.6 V I_ph = 239.6 / 25 = 9.58 A and in wye I_L = I_ph = 9.58 A P = 3 · I_ph² · R = 3 × 9.58² × 20 = 5 508 W ≈ 5.5 kW RATIO: P_delta / P_wye = 16 534 / 5 508 = 3.00

Discussion / insight. The same three impedances draw exactly three times the power in delta as in wye, because delta applies √3 times the voltage to each impedance and power goes as V² — (√3)² = 3. This single ratio is the entire basis of star-delta starting (WE2), of dual-voltage motor windings (a 230/400 V motor is the same windings in delta or wye), and of the classic field mistake of energising a delta-connected 400 V motor's windings in delta on a 690 V supply — which applies √3 times rated volts, saturates the iron and burns it out. Note also that the power-factor and the impedance angle are identical in both connections: connection changes magnitude, never phase angle.

WE2 · Star-Delta Starting — Current and Torque Numbers

A 30 kW, 400 V, 4-pole induction motor has full-load current 55 A, locked-rotor current 6× FLC (DOL) and locked-rotor torque 1.8× full-load torque (DOL). Full-load torque follows from rated speed 1450 rpm. Compute DOL and star-delta starting current and torque, and comment on whether the starter can accelerate a conveyor needing 45% of FLT to break away.

FULL-LOAD TORQUE T_FL = 9550 · P(kW) / N(rpm) = 9550 × 30 / 1450 = 197.6 N·m DIRECT ON LINE I_start(DOL) = 6 × 55 = 330 A T_start(DOL) = 1.8 × 197.6 = 355.7 N·m STAR-DELTA (windings normally run in DELTA; the starter first connects them in STAR) In star each winding sees V_L/√3, so winding current falls by 1/√3, and the LINE current falls by a further 1/√3 (no √3 multiplication in star): I_start(star) = I_start(DOL) / 3 = 330 / 3 = 110 A (= 2.0 × FLC) T_start(star) = T_start(DOL) / 3 = 355.7 / 3 = 118.6 N·m (= 0.60 × T_FL) BREAKAWAY CHECK Load breakaway torque = 0.45 × 197.6 = 88.9 N·m Available in star = 118.6 N·m → margin = 118.6 / 88.9 = 1.33 ✓ accelerates TRANSITION CURRENT (the hidden problem) At changeover the motor is near full speed but the open-transition break re-energises the windings in delta at low slip: a transient peak of typically 1.5–3 × I_FL·(6) is possible for a few cycles.

Discussion / insight. Both current and torque fall by the same factor of 3 — star-delta buys a 67% reduction in starting current at the price of a 67% reduction in starting torque, and there is no way to separate the two. That is why star-delta only works for loads whose torque demand at low speed is genuinely small (fans, centrifugal pumps, unloaded compressors) and fails on loaded conveyors, crushers and positive-displacement pumps. Examiners like the follow-up: why not just use a soft starter or VFD? A soft starter reduces voltage smoothly (torque still falls as V², but the ramp is continuous and there is no open-transition transient); a VFD reduces frequency as well as voltage, keeping V/f — and therefore flux and torque capability — constant, so it can deliver full torque at a fraction of the current. That is the fundamental advantage of A08's frequency control over any voltage-only starter.

WE3 · Power-Factor Correction — kvar, Capacitance and the Delta/Wye Factor of 3

A factory draws 500 kW at 0.78 lagging power factor from a 400 V, 50 Hz supply. Correct the power factor to 0.95 lagging. Find the required kvar, the capacitance per phase for delta and for wye connection, the new line current, and the released transformer capacity.

EXISTING AND TARGET ANGLES cosφ₁ = 0.78 → φ₁ = 38.74° → tanφ₁ = 0.8025 cosφ₂ = 0.95 → φ₂ = 18.19° → tanφ₂ = 0.3287 REQUIRED COMPENSATION Q_C = P (tanφ₁ − tanφ₂) = 500 (0.8025 − 0.3287) = 500 × 0.4738 = 236.9 kvar → specify a 240 kvar bank (nearest standard, in stages) CAPACITANCE — DELTA CONNECTION (capacitors connected across the lines) Per-phase (per-capacitor) kvar = 236.9 / 3 = 78.97 kvar, at V_cap = V_L = 400 V C = Q_ph / (ω · V_cap²) with ω = 2π × 50 = 314.16 rad/s C_delta = 78 970 / (314.16 × 400²) = 78 970 / 50 265 600 = 1.571 × 10⁻³ F = 1571 µF per phase CAPACITANCE — WYE CONNECTION (capacitors to neutral) V_ph = 400/√3 = 231 V, per-phase kvar still 78.97 kvar C_wye = 78 970 / (314.16 × 231²) = 78 970 / 16 764 500 = 4.710 × 10⁻³ F = 4710 µF per phase RATIO: C_wye / C_delta = 4710 / 1570 = 3.00 NEW CURRENT AND RELEASED CAPACITY Before: S₁ = 500 / 0.78 = 641.0 kVA → I₁ = 641 000/(1.732 × 400) = 925 A After: S₂ = 500 / 0.95 = 526.3 kVA → I₂ = 526 300/(1.732 × 400) = 760 A Released capacity = 641.0 − 526.3 = 114.7 kVA (18% of the transformer, free) Current reduction = 925 − 760 = 165 A (18%)

Discussion / insight. The headline result is the factor of 3: a delta-connected bank needs only one third of the capacitance of a wye-connected bank for the same kvar, because each capacitor sees √3 times the voltage and Q ∝ V². Delta is therefore the standard connection for LV correction (less capacitance, cheaper cans) — at the cost of each capacitor being rated for the full line voltage. Two professional caveats that turn this from an arithmetic exercise into engineering. First, do not over-correct: pushing beyond unity makes the load capacitive (leading), which raises voltage, upsets generator AVRs and can cause self-excitation of a motor coasting down. Correct to 0.95–0.98, not 1.00, and use automatic staged switching for varying loads. Second — and this is the reason A56 exists — this whole calculation only addresses reactive power Q. If the 0.78 power factor is partly caused by rectifier loads (distortion power D), capacitors will not recover it, and worse, the bank will resonate with the supply inductance at hr = √(SSC/QC). With a 1000 kVA/5% transformer and 240 kvar, hr = √(20 000/240) = 9.1 — uncomfortably near the 11th harmonic, so this bank should be detuned.

WE4 · Unbalanced Four-Wire Load — Neutral Current and Sequence Components

A 400/230 V four-wire distribution board carries unbalanced single-phase resistive loads: Ia = 80 A∠0°, Ib = 60 A∠−120°, Ic = 35 A∠−240°. Find the neutral current, the zero- and negative-sequence components, and the NEMA percentage unbalance.

RESOLVE INTO RECTANGULAR FORM I_a = 80∠0° = 80.00 + j0 I_b = 60∠−120° = 60(−0.5 − j0.866) = −30.00 − j51.96 I_c = 35∠−240° = 35(−0.5 + j0.866) = −17.50 + j30.31 NEUTRAL CURRENT (Kirchhoff at the star point) I_N = I_a + I_b + I_c = (80 − 30 − 17.5) + j(0 − 51.96 + 30.31) = 32.50 − j21.65 |I_N| = √(32.50² + 21.65²) = √(1056 + 469) = √1525 = 39.1 A ∠−33.7° ZERO-SEQUENCE COMPONENT I_0 = ⅓(I_a + I_b + I_c) = ⅓ × 39.1 = 13.0 A and note I_N = 3·I_0 ✓ POSITIVE / NEGATIVE SEQUENCE (a = 1∠120°) I_1 = ⅓(I_a + a·I_b + a²·I_c) = ⅓(80∠0° + 60∠0° + 35∠0°) = 58.33 A (because rotating I_b by +120° and I_c by +240° aligns all three) I_2 = ⅓(I_a + a²·I_b + a·I_c) = ⅓(80∠0° + 60∠120° + 35∠240°) = ⅓[(80 − 30 − 17.5) + j(0 + 51.96 − 30.31)] = ⅓(32.50 + j21.65) |I_2| = 13.0 A Unbalance factor = I_2/I_1 = 13.0/58.33 = 22.3% (severe) NEMA PERCENTAGE UNBALANCE (current form) mean = (80 + 60 + 35)/3 = 58.33 A max deviation = |35 − 58.33| = 23.33 A %unbalance = 23.33/58.33 × 100 = 40.0%

Discussion / insight. Three lessons stack up here. (1) The neutral is doing real work — 39 A, half the heaviest phase — purely from fundamental-frequency unbalance, before any harmonics are considered. Add triplen harmonics from the single-phase electronic loads and the neutral can exceed the phase conductors entirely (see A56). (2) The identity IN = 3·I0 falls straight out of the arithmetic, which is why zero-sequence current is sometimes just called "neutral current ÷ 3", and why a core-balance CT around all three phases measures 3I0 and forms the basis of earth-fault protection. (3) The two unbalance definitions give very different numbers (22.3% sequence-based, 40% NEMA) because they measure different things — always state which convention you used; standards specify the sequence-based ratio, field practice often uses NEMA. Practical action: redistribute the single-phase loads (moving ~20 A from phase A to phase C nearly balances the board), because unbalance costs neutral heating, extra losses, and — if the same unbalance appears in voltage — negative-sequence rotor heating in every motor on the board at roughly 2 × (%unbalance)² extra loss.

Problem Set

Ten problems in ascending difficulty. Attempt each on paper before revealing — the struggle is the learning.

P1. A wye-connected supply has 240 V line-to-neutral. State the line-to-line voltage and the phase angle between Van and Vab.

Tap to reveal ↓A: VL = √3 × 240 = 415.7 V. The line-to-line phasor leads its reference phase voltage by 30°: Vab = Van − Vbn = √3·Van∠+30°. The 30° is not decoration — it is exactly the phase shift a Dyn11 transformer introduces, which is why transformers must have matching vector groups before they can be paralleled (A32).

P2. A 415 V, 3-phase motor draws 42 A at 0.86 power factor. Find kVA, kW and kvar.

Tap to reveal ↓A: S = √3·V·I = 1.732 × 415 × 42 = 30.2 kVA. P = S·cosφ = 30.2 × 0.86 = 26.0 kW. Q = S·sinφ = 30.2 × 0.510 = 15.4 kvar. Check: √(26.0² + 15.4²) = √(676 + 237) = 30.2 ✓. Trap: the formula is √3·VL·IL, not 3·VL·IL — the 3 belongs with phase quantities (3·Vph·Iph), and √3 with line quantities. Both give the same answer; mixing them gives an answer 1.73× wrong.

P3. A 6-pole induction motor on a 50 Hz supply runs at 960 rpm at full load. Find synchronous speed, slip, rotor frequency, and the speed if the supply were 60 Hz.

Tap to reveal ↓A: ns = 120f/P = 120 × 50/6 = 1000 rpm. Slip s = (1000 − 960)/1000 = 0.04 = 4%. Rotor (slip) frequency fr = s·f = 0.04 × 50 = 2 Hz — which is why a rotor-bar fault shows sidebands at ±2·s·f around the line frequency in a current spectrum (A42/A55). At 60 Hz: ns = 120 × 60/6 = 1200 rpm, and at the same slip the shaft would run ≈ 1152 rpm. Caution: running a 50 Hz motor on 60 Hz at the same voltage reduces flux (V/f falls by 17%), so torque capability drops even though speed rises — the V/f principle behind every VFD (A08).

P4. A balanced delta load of 30 Ω per phase (pure resistance) is fed at 400 V. One line fuse blows. What power is now dissipated, as a fraction of the original?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Original: each of three 30 Ω phases sees 400 V → P = 3 × 400²/30 = 16.0 kW. With one line open, the phase directly across the two remaining healthy lines still sees 400 V (P = 5.33 kW), while the other two phases are now in series across those same two lines (60 Ω total, P = 400²/60 = 2.67 kW). Total = 5.33 + 2.67 = 8.0 kW = exactly half. Insight: single-phasing does not simply remove one third of the load — the remaining lines carry redistributed, unbalanced current, which is why for a motor single-phasing is so destructive (current in the surviving phases rises by ~1.7×) and why phase-failure protection (ANSI 46/47) is not optional.

P5. A 1000 kVA, 400 V transformer has 5% impedance. Find full-load current and prospective three-phase short-circuit current at its terminals. Which breaker rating do you specify?

Tap to reveal ↓A: IFL = 1 000 000/(1.732 × 400) = 1443 A. ISC = IFL/0.05 = 28.9 kA (equivalently SSC = 1000/0.05 = 20 MVA). Specify switchgear with a breaking capacity comfortably above this — commonly 36 kA at 400 V — and remember to add the contribution of any parallel transformer or on-site generation, and that motor contribution briefly raises the peak. Two follow-ons examiners like: (1) a lower %Z gives better voltage regulation but higher fault current and more expensive switchgear — that trade-off is why 4–6% is the usual LV distribution choice; (2) this same SSC sets the harmonic resonance order with any capacitor bank, hr = √(SSC/QC) — see A56.

P6. Explain, with numbers, why a three-phase transmission line uses about 75% of the conductor material of a single-phase line delivering the same power at the same voltage and loss.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Take P watts at line voltage V and unity PF. Single-phase (two conductors): I1φ = P/V. Loss = 2·I1φ²R1. Three-phase (three conductors, balanced so the neutral carries nothing and can be omitted): I3φ = P/(√3·V), so I3φ = I1φ/√3. Loss = 3·I3φ²R3 = 3·(I1φ²/3)·R3 = I1φ²R3. For equal losses: 2·I1φ²R1 = I1φ²R3 → R3 = 2R1, i.e. each three-phase conductor may have half the cross-section. Material ∝ (number of conductors) × (area): single-phase = 2 × A, three-phase = 3 × A/2 = 1.5A → ratio 1.5/2 = 0.75. Hence the famous 25% copper saving — the economic reason the entire world grid is three-phase, and the reason the neutral can often be omitted or reduced (until harmonics arrive and reverse that conclusion).

P7. A 400 V feeder supplies 250 kW at 0.82 lagging. A 100 kvar detuned capacitor bank (7%) is switched in. Find the new power factor and comment on the capacitor voltage rating required.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Initial Q₁ = P·tanφ₁ = 250 × tan(34.92°) = 250 × 0.6982 = 174.6 kvar. A 7% detuned bank delivers more than its capacitor rating at the busbar: Qeff ≈ QC/(1 − p) = 100/0.93 = 107.5 kvar. New Q₂ = 174.6 − 107.5 = 67.1 kvar → φ₂ = arctan(67.1/250) = 15.03° → cos φ₂ = 0.966. Capacitor voltage: VC = VL/(1 − p) = 400/0.93 = 430 V continuous, so specify capacitors rated ≥ 480 V (with margin for tolerance, harmonics and supply overvoltage). Fitting standard 415 V cans behind a detuning reactor is a well-known premature-failure cause. Also verify the tuned order ht = 1/√0.07 = 3.78 sits below the 5th ✓ (A56).

P8. A three-phase 4-wire circuit feeds 120 A per phase of LED lighting with 40% third-harmonic content. Compute the neutral current and compare it with the phase RMS. What does this do to the "neutral can be smaller" rule?

Tap to reveal ↓A: I₃ per phase = 0.40 × 120 = 48 A. Being zero-sequence (3 × 120° = 360° ≡ 0°, i.e. all three in phase), triplens add arithmetically: IN = 3 × 48 = 144 A. Phase RMS = √(120² + 48²) = √(14 400 + 2304) = 129.2 A. So IN/Iphase = 1.11 — the neutral carries MORE than the phases, at 150 Hz where skin effect raises the effective resistance, and with no protective device watching it. The textbook rule ("balanced load ⇒ zero neutral current ⇒ reduce the neutral") is valid only at the fundamental and inverts completely for single-phase electronic loads. Correct practice: full-size or 200% neutral, treat the circuit as four loaded conductors for derating, and thermographically inspect neutral terminations. Full treatment in A56.

P9. Two 500 kVA transformers, both 400 V, are to run in parallel. One is Dyn11, the other Dyn1. The impedances are 4.5% and 5.5%. Discuss whether they can be paralleled and how load would divide if the vector groups matched.

Tap to reveal ↓A: They cannot be paralleled at all. Dyn11 gives +30° and Dyn1 gives −30° phase displacement, a 60° difference; connecting them would place 60° of phase difference across the interconnection, driving an enormous circulating current limited only by the two impedances — effectively a short circuit. Vector group compatibility is an absolute prerequisite (along with the same voltage ratio, the same phase sequence and correct polarity). If the groups did match, load divides inversely with per-unit impedance: transformer A takes (1/4.5)/((1/4.5)+(1/5.5)) = 0.2222/(0.2222+0.1818) = 55% and B takes 45%. Total usable capacity is therefore limited by the first unit to reach 100%: A hits its 500 kVA when total load = 500/0.55 = 909 kVA, not 1000 kVA — a 9% capacity loss caused purely by mismatched impedance. This is why parallel transformers should be specified with matched %Z.

P10. Synthesis. A plant has a 1000 kVA/5% transformer at 400 V, 620 A maximum demand, 0.79 lagging power factor, 200 kvar of plain capacitors installed, roughly 40% of the load being six-pulse VFDs, and a 2.4% voltage unbalance. Produce a prioritised engineering assessment.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Work through the four numbers the plant already gives you. (1) Capacity. S = √3 × 400 × 620 = 429 kVA of 1000 kVA — headroom exists, but 0.79 PF wastes it: at 0.95 the same 339 kW would need only 357 kVA, freeing ~72 kVA. (2) Resonance — do this first, it is a hazard. SSC = 1000/0.05 = 20 000 kVA; with 200 kvar in, hr = √(20 000/200) = 10.0 — essentially on the 11th harmonic, which every six-pulse drive produces. With half the bank in, hr = √200 = 14.1 (near the 13th). The plain bank is unsafe in a 40%-VFD plant: expect capacitor and fuse failures. Action: retrofit 5.67% or 7% detuning reactors with correctly voltage-rated capacitors (≥480 V), interlocked off on generator. (3) Power factor is partly distortion. With 40% VFD load, some of the 0.79 is distortion power D, which capacitors cannot correct — so measure true PF and the spectrum before sizing any more kvar, and expect that the achievable cos φ₁ correction lifts true PF less than the arithmetic promises. Then check TDD against IEEE 519: ISC = 28.9 kA, ISC/IL = 28 900/620 = 46.6 → the 20–50 band, TDD limit 8%. (4) Unbalance. 2.4% exceeds EN 50160's 2% expectation and implies roughly 2 × 2.4² ≈ 12% extra rotor heating in every motor — derate per NEMA MG-1 and hunt the cause (single-phase load distribution, a high-resistance joint, or one failed capacitor element, which would also explain item 2). Sequence: detune the bank → rebalance and re-measure → 7-day survey for TDD and true PF → then size correction/mitigation on measured data → finally re-check transformer loading with Factor-K. Full method in A56.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations to reproduce from memory, a symbol glossary, and the seven mistakes that cost marks and equipment.

Derivation 1 · Where √3 Comes From
Line voltage is the PHASOR DIFFERENCE of two phase voltages, not their sum. Take V_an = V∠0° and V_bn = V∠−120° V_ab = V_an − V_bn = V(1∠0°) − V(1∠−120°) = V[(1 + j0) − (−0.5 − j0.866)] = V[1.5 + j0.866] |V_ab| = V√(1.5² + 0.866²) = V√(2.25 + 0.75) = V√3 ∎ ∠V_ab = arctan(0.866/1.5) = +30° GEOMETRIC VIEW (the version to sketch in an exam) Two equal phasors separated by 120°: their difference is the base of an isosceles triangle with equal sides V and apex angle 120°. Dropping a perpendicular gives two right triangles with base V·cos30°, so |V_ab| = 2·V·cos30° = 2V(√3/2) = √3·V ∎ THE SAME GEOMETRY, APPLIED TO CURRENT IN DELTA In delta, each LINE current is the difference of two PHASE currents: I_L = I_ph1 − I_ph2 → |I_L| = √3·|I_ph|, lagging by 30° SO THE SYMMETRY IS: WYE : V_L = √3·V_ph , I_L = I_ph DELTA: V_L = V_ph , I_L = √3·I_ph Either way: S = 3·V_ph·I_ph = √3·V_L·I_L

What it teaches. √3 is never a fudge factor — it is always 2cos30°, the geometry of subtracting two phasors 120° apart. The accompanying 30° phase shift is equally important and is usually the part students forget: it is the physical origin of transformer vector groups (Dyn11 = delta primary, wye secondary, secondary lagging by 30° in the "11 o'clock" position) and therefore of the paralleling rules in A32. Notice too that S = 3·Vph·Iph = √3·VL·IL in both connections — the 3 travels with phase quantities and the √3 with line quantities, and that is the whole content of the most common arithmetic error in three-phase work.

Derivation 2 · Why Total Instantaneous Power Is Constant
For one phase, with v = √2·V·sin(ωt) and i = √2·I·sin(ωt − φ): p(t) = v·i = 2VI·sin(ωt)·sin(ωt − φ) Use the identity 2·sinA·sinB = cos(A−B) − cos(A+B): p(t) = VI·cosφ − VI·cos(2ωt − φ) └ constant ┘ └ pulsates at TWICE line frequency ┘ Now sum all three phases. The constant terms simply add: 3·VI·cosφ The pulsating terms are displaced by 2 × 120° = 240° from each other: cos(2ωt − φ) + cos(2ωt − φ − 240°) + cos(2ωt − φ − 480°) Since 480° ≡ 120°, these are three unit cosines at 0°, 240° and 120° — three vectors evenly spaced by 120° — whose sum is exactly ZERO. Therefore: p_total(t) = 3·V_ph·I_ph·cosφ = CONSTANT, for all t ∎ Equivalently: p_total = √3·V_L·I_L·cosφ, with no ripple term at all.

What it teaches. This is the deepest reason the world is three-phase, and it is a mechanical reason as much as an electrical one. Constant instantaneous power means constant torque: a three-phase motor produces smooth shaft torque, whereas a single-phase motor's torque pulses to zero 100 times a second, which is why single-phase machines need starting mechanisms, vibrate more, and are limited to small ratings. It also means a three-phase generator experiences constant mechanical loading on its prime mover — no 100 Hz torque ripple to fatigue the shaft and coupling. The corollary is examinable: the moment the load becomes unbalanced or distorted, the cancellation is incomplete and torque ripple returns — which is exactly how 5th/7th harmonics produce a 6× line-frequency (300 Hz) pulsation in A56 and a diagnosable vibration signature in A42.

Derivation 3 · The Star-Delta ⅓ Factor
A motor's windings are designed to run in DELTA on line voltage V_L. The starter first connects them in STAR on the same supply. STEP 1 — voltage across each winding Delta (run): V_w = V_L Star (start): V_w = V_L/√3 → a factor 1/√3 = 0.577 STEP 2 — current in each winding (winding impedance Z unchanged) I_w,star / I_w,delta = (V_L/√3)/V_L = 1/√3 STEP 3 — LINE current (this is where the second √3 appears) In delta: I_L = √3 · I_w (line current is √3 × winding current) In star: I_L = I_w (line current IS the winding current) I_L,star / I_L,delta = (I_w/√3) / (√3·I_w) = 1/3 ∎ STEP 4 — torque Induction motor torque ∝ V_w² (at a given slip) T_star / T_delta = (1/√3)² = 1/3 ∎ CONCLUSION Starting current falls to 1/3 AND starting torque falls to 1/3. The reduction ratio is identical — you cannot have one without the other.

What it teaches. The 1/3 arrives by two independent factors of 1/√3 — one from the reduced winding voltage, one from the loss of the delta's √3 line/phase multiplication — and being able to say which is which is what distinguishes a real understanding from a memorised ratio. The engineering consequence is the honest limitation of star-delta starting: it is a fixed, un-tunable 3:1 trade with an open-transition current transient at changeover, suitable only for loads with low breakaway torque. Compare the alternatives on the same basis: a soft starter reduces voltage continuously (torque still ∝ V², but smoothly and with no transition step), while a VFD reduces voltage and frequency together, holding V/f — hence flux — constant, which is the only way to obtain full torque at low speed and low current (A08).

Seven Exam & Field Traps
  • 1Mixing √3 and 3. S = √3·VL·IL = 3·Vph·Iph. Using 3 with line quantities inflates every answer by 1.732. Write down which quantities you have before choosing the formula.
  • 2Line voltage as a sum. Vab = Van − Vbn, a phasor difference (giving √3 and +30°), not 2V and not Van + Vbn.
  • 3Forgetting the ×3 capacitance factor. For the same kvar, Cwye = 3·Cdelta, because each delta capacitor sees √3 times the voltage and Q ∝ V². Also: never over-correct past unity, and always check hr = √(SSC/QC) — a plain bank in a converter-rich plant is a resonance waiting to happen (A56).
  • 4ns = 120f/P with P as pole-pairs. P is the number of poles (always even). A "4-pole" motor has 2 pole-pairs and 1500 rpm at 50 Hz; using pole-pairs in the same formula doubles the answer.
  • 5Assuming the neutral is idle. True only for a balanced fundamental. Unbalance puts real current in it (WE4), and zero-sequence triplen harmonics add arithmetically — a neutral can exceed the phase conductors, unprotected (P8, A56).
  • 6Treating star-delta as free. Current and torque both fall to exactly ⅓; you cannot reduce inrush without losing the same fraction of torque. Also remember the open-transition transient at changeover, and that a delta-wound motor connected in delta on √3× its rated voltage will saturate and burn.
  • 7Ignoring phase sequence and vector group. Swapping any two phases reverses rotation — dangerous for pumps, compressors and conveyors, hence ANSI 47 phase-sequence relays. And transformers of different vector groups (Dyn11 vs Dyn1) can never be paralleled, whatever their ratings match (P9, A32).

Eight-Step Hands-On Ladder
  • 1Measure a real board. With a true-RMS meter, record all three line-to-line and line-to-neutral voltages. Verify the √3 ratio to within 1%, and compute the NEMA percentage unbalance. Almost no one does this, and it takes three minutes.
  • 2Draw the phasor diagram from your own readings to scale, and confirm the 30° shift between Van and Vab geometrically. Sketching it once beats reading it ten times.
  • 3Clamp the neutral of a lighting or socket distribution board. Compare it with the phase currents and explain the number using both unbalance and triplen harmonics.
  • 4Read a motor nameplate and decode it fully: 400/690 V Δ/Y meaning, FLC, cos φ, rated speed → number of poles, slip and full-load torque via T = 9550P/N.
  • 5Trace a star-delta starter in a real panel: the three contactors, the interlocks, the timer, and where the open-transition transient occurs. Then compute its starting current and torque for the motor fitted (WE2).
  • 6Do your site's fault-level arithmetic: from the transformer nameplate, compute IFL, ISC and SSC, and write them on the single-line diagram alongside the switchgear rating.
  • 7Size a PF correction bank for a real feeder from its metered kW and PF, choose delta connection, compute the capacitance, then check hr = √(SSC/QC) for every stage combination and specify detuning if needed.
  • 8Simulate an unbalanced four-wire load in Python or a spreadsheet: enter three phasor currents, output IN, I0, I1, I2 and both unbalance definitions. Then verify your WE4 answers with it.
Self-Assessment — Do You Own This Subject?

Foundation

Can you derive √3 as 2cos30° from a phasor difference, and state the wye/delta line-phase relations for both voltage and current without looking them up?

Working

Given any two of {kW, kVA, kvar, PF, current, voltage}, can you find the rest — and size a PF correction bank with the correct delta capacitance?

Diagnostic

Shown an unbalanced four-wire board, can you compute IN and the sequence components, and explain both the neutral heating and the motor rotor heating it causes?

Design

Can you compute a site's fault level, choose a starting method with justified current/torque numbers, and check capacitor resonance before installing anything?

When all four are true, you can read any three-phase installation from its nameplates alone — and you are ready for the modules that build on it: A08 (VFDs), A32 (transformers), A51 (starters), A55 (motors) and A56 (power quality).
