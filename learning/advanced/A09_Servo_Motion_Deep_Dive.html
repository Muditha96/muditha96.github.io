Nobody Touched the Servo. The Servo Broke Anyway.

A LABELLING LINE, THREE WEEKS AFTER A MECHANICAL IMPROVEMENT

A web-feed axis ran beautifully for two years: 750 W servo, belt to a driven roller, settling inside 60 ms, registration mark held to ±0.3 mm. Then the workshop replaced the 76 mm roller with a 120 mm one so the machine could run faster per revolution. Same motor, same drive, same program, same gains.

Now the axis rings audibly at the end of every move, registration wanders by ±1.5 mm, and after twenty minutes of production the drive trips on overload. The supplier has been called twice. Auto-tune has been run five times and each time produces a machine that is either sluggish or unstable. Everybody is looking at the servo, and there is nothing wrong with the servo.

The roller is an aluminium tube, and a tube’s inertia scales with the cube of its radius — its mass grows with radius, and inertia adds another r² on top. Going from 38 mm to 60 mm radius multiplied the load inertia by (60/38)³ = 3.9 times:

Aluminium tube, 3 mm wall, 500 mm long: J = m·r², m = 2πr·t·L·ρ r = 38 mm : m = 0.97 kg, Jload = 1.40 × 10⁻³ kg·m² r = 60 mm : m = 1.53 kg, Jload = 5.50 × 10⁻³ kg·m² Motor inertia Jm = 1.35 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m² INERTIA RATIO before: 10.3 : 1 after: 40.7 : 1 And the belt is not rigid. With a transmission stiffness of about 1500 N·m/rad, the ANTI-RESONANCE that limits every velocity loop is far = (1/2π)√(K/Jload) before: 165 Hz after: 83 Hz The velocity loop was tuned at 80 Hz. It is now sitting exactly on top of the machine's own mechanical resonance — which is what “ringing” sounds like.

There is a second, independent problem hidden in the same change. Accelerating to 500 rpm in 100 ms now needs 2.95 N·m against a motor rated 2.39 N·m continuous — inside the peak rating, so the axis moves, but over a full duty cycle the RMS torque has crept past continuous, which is why it trips after twenty minutes rather than immediately. Diagnosis on page 7, resonance mathematics on page 10, and the four possible fixes — only one of which is cheap — on page 10 as well.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Before reading on — which single change fixes both problems at once, at the lowest cost?

B — the gearbox, because reflected inertia divides by the ratio SQUARED. A 3:1 reduction turns 5.50×10⁻³ into 6.11×10⁻⁴ kg·m², an inertia ratio of 4.5:1 — better than the machine ever had. Torque at the motor divides by 3 as well, so the thermal overload disappears at the same time, and with the ratio back in the comfortable zone the velocity loop can be tuned properly again instead of being trapped between sluggish and unstable. The price is motor speed: the motor must now run three times faster, so check it against the rated speed before ordering.

One honest caveat, and it is on page 10: a gearbox does not raise the 83 Hz load-on-belt mode — that frequency belongs to the roller and the belt, and only a stiffer belt or a lighter roller moves it. What the gearbox does is weaken the coupling to it, which combined with jerk limiting is what makes the machine quiet.

A makes it worse, immediately. Raising gain on a lightly damped mechanical resonance is how you turn ringing into oscillation. Gain cannot fix a mechanical limit; it can only find it faster.

C is the expensive answer and it is only half an answer: a bigger motor raises available torque and increases Jm, so the ratio improves — but the anti-resonance at 83 Hz is set by the load and the belt, and it does not move.

D is a legitimate patch, and often the right emergency action tonight: a notch at the resonance lets you keep some bandwidth. It does nothing about the thermal overload, and it is a filter compensating for a machine that should have been designed differently.

What a Servo System Actually Is

Not a type of motor — a type of loop. Three of them, nested, each correcting the one outside it.

position loop & feedback velocity loop current (torque) loop
THE DEFINITION, SAID PROPERLY CORE

A servo system is a closed-loop motion system in which a measured output — position, velocity or torque — is continuously compared with a commanded value, and the error is used to drive the actuator until the error is small. The motor is only one component; the loop is the machine.

That is why “servo motor” is a slightly misleading phrase. The motor in a modern servo axis is an ordinary permanent-magnet synchronous machine (page 3). What makes it a servo is the encoder bolted to its back, the drive that closes three loops around it, and the fact that the drive knows the rotor angle at every instant. Remove the feedback and the same motor becomes an ordinary PMSM on a VFD.

Fit one when you need verified position, high dynamics, high duty, or coordination with other axes — and be able to say which of those four you are buying.

WHY THREE LOOPS, AND WHY IN THAT ORDER CORE
  • 1
    Current (torque) loop — innermost, 1–3 kHz. Current is what actually produces torque, and the electrical time constant (L/R, typically 1–10 ms) is far faster than anything mechanical. Closing current first turns the motor into a near-ideal torque source, which is what the loops above assume.
  • 2
    Velocity loop — middle, 50–300 Hz. Its job is to make speed follow a command despite load torque, friction and inertia. It is a PI controller acting on a torque source; its achievable bandwidth is set by mechanics — inertia ratio, stiffness, backlash — not by the drive.
  • 3
    Position loop — outermost, 10–50 Hz. Usually proportional only (gain Kv), because the velocity loop below it already contains an integrator in the physics: velocity integrates into position. Adding integral action here is a classic route to overshoot and hunting.
  • 4
    The separation rule. Each inner loop should be roughly 4 to 10 times faster than the loop that commands it. Break that rule and the loops interact: the outer loop sees the inner loop’s dynamics as a phase lag it cannot compensate, and the axis becomes impossible to tune. See A10 for the general theory.
  • 5
    Feedforward jumps the queue. Velocity and acceleration feedforward inject the predicted command directly into the inner loops, so the feedback loops only have to correct what the model got wrong. This is how machine tools achieve small following error without heroic gains (page 6).

The Motor: PMSM, Kt and the Envelope

Two constants and one graph tell you almost everything a servo motor can do — and the graph has two regions that behave completely differently.

HOW A PMSM MAKES TORQUE CORE

A permanent-magnet synchronous motor carries magnets on the rotor and a three-phase distributed winding in the stator. The drive uses the encoder to know the rotor angle continuously, and energises the stator so that its field sits exactly 90 electrical degrees ahead of the rotor magnets at all times. That is the whole idea of field-oriented control (page 5), and it produces the cleanest torque relationship in electrical machines:

T = Kt · Iq torque is directly proportional to the quadrature current E = Ke · ω back-EMF is directly proportional to speed In SI units these two constants are NUMERICALLY EQUAL: Kt [N·m/A] = Ke [V·s/rad] — a consequence of energy conservation (derived on page 18) Mechanical power out = Tω = KtI·ω Electrical power in = EI = Keω·I → equal only if Kt = Ke CATALOGUE UNITS TRAP: Ke is often quoted in V/krpm. Ke[V·s/rad] = Ke[V/krpm] × 60/(2π × 1000) = Ke[V/krpm] × 0.00955 EXAMPLE — a 750 W, 3000 rpm servo: rated torque T = P/ω = 750/(3000×2π/60) = 750/314.2 = 2.39 N·m if Kt = 0.72 N·m/A → rated current = 2.39/0.72 = 3.3 A peak torque 3× → 7.2 N·m at 10 A — and that is what the DRIVE must supply
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A servo motor is rated 3000 rpm and its back-EMF constant is 60 V/krpm. The drive runs from a 400 V supply (DC bus ≈ 566 V). What limits the top speed?

B. At 60 V/krpm the motor generates 60 V of back-EMF for every 1000 rpm (line-to-line, in the usual catalogue convention). The drive can only push current into the winding while it has voltage headroom above the back-EMF. With a 566 V bus, allowing for modulation limits and the winding drop, useful headroom runs out somewhere around 5000–5500 rpm — and well before that the available torque is already falling, because there is less voltage left to force current through the inductance.

This is why the envelope on the previous illustration bends over at high speed: the flat region is a current (thermal) limit and the falling region is a voltage limit. A drive can trade some of that back with field weakening (negative id, page 5), buying speed at the cost of torque.

D is a real constraint in its own right — a 20-bit encoder at 6000 rpm is a serious data rate — but modern serial encoders are specified well past the motor’s mechanical limit. A is the answer of somebody who has not met the DC bus.

RESOLUTION IS NOT ACCURACY CORE

A 23-bit encoder resolves 0.15 arc-seconds. It is not accurate to 0.15 arc-seconds — typical system accuracy is tens of arc-seconds, and the machine it is bolted to is worse still. Four separate error sources sit between the count and the part:

  • 1
    Encoder accuracy itself — graduation error, interpolation error within one signal period, and installation eccentricity. Eccentricity produces a once-per-revolution sinusoidal error and is the dominant term in a badly fitted encoder.
  • 2
    Transmission error — ball-screw lead error and wind-up, gearbox transmission error, belt stretch, coupling compliance. A motor-mounted encoder measures the motor; everything downstream of it is invisible to the loop.
  • 3
    Backlash and reversal error — a lost-motion band that no gain can remove; it appears as a step in the following error at every direction change, and it is what makes a circular test show a “spike” at the quadrant crossings (see A40).
  • 4
    Thermal growth — steel expands about 11.7 µm per metre per °C. A 1 m axis warming 5 °C moves 58 µm, which is hundreds of encoder counts and nothing whatever to do with the servo.

The design decision that follows: a motor encoder gives a stable, high-bandwidth, easy-to-tune loop but cannot see downstream error. A linear scale on the load (full closed loop, or “dual feedback”) sees everything — and puts the compliance and backlash inside the loop, which makes the axis far harder to tune and can make it unstable. Machine tools solve this with dual-loop control: velocity from the motor encoder, position from the scale. That is the correct answer and it is not free.

Why this is worth the algebra. In the rotating frame the currents are constant at steady state, so an ordinary PI controller can drive the error to exactly zero — something a PI simply cannot do against a sinusoid. Everything the drive does afterwards (torque limits, field weakening, thermal models) becomes arithmetic on two DC numbers instead of three moving ones.

The Cascaded Loops & Feedforward

Velocity gain is inertia times bandwidth. Following error is speed divided by position gain. Two formulas explain most of what a servo does.

THE VELOCITY LOOP — WHY GAIN IS PROPORTIONAL TO INERTIA CORE

Below the velocity loop sits an ideal torque source. The mechanics it drives are, to first order, just an integrator: torque produces acceleration, which integrates to speed.

Plant: ω(s)/T(s) = 1/(Jtotal s) (a pure integrator) Proportional velocity gain Kvp [N·m per rad/s]: T = Kvp(ω* − ω) → closed loop ω/ω* = 1/(1 + s Jtotal/Kvp) First order, with bandwidth ωbw = Kvp/Jtotal (rad/s) ⇒ Kvp = Jtotal · ωbw THIS IS THE MOST USEFUL EQUATION IN SERVO TUNING. · Gain and inertia are locked together. Change the machine's inertia and the SAME gain gives a different bandwidth — which is precisely why the labelling axis on page 1 stopped working when a roller was changed. · Doubling the load inertia halves the bandwidth at fixed gain. · It also tells you what to type: for Jtotal = 6.9×10⁻⁴ kg·m² and a target of 50 Hz (314 rad/s), Kvp = 6.9e−4 × 314 = 0.217 N·m per rad/s. The integral term adds stiffness against constant load torque. Express it as an integral TIME rather than a gain: Ti ≈ 3–5 × (1/ωbw) — slow enough not to eat phase margin
THE POSITION LOOP AND FOLLOWING ERROR CORE
Position loop is normally PROPORTIONAL only, with gain Kv [1/s]: ω* = Kv · (x* − x) At constant velocity v, the loop must hold a steady error to produce the speed command at all — so the axis lags behind by: following error e = v / Kv Kv = 30 s⁻¹, feed 300 mm/s → e = 10 mm (!) Kv = 60 s⁻¹, feed 300 mm/s → e = 5 mm MACHINE-TOOL UNITS: Kv is often quoted in m/min per mm. Kv [1/s] = Kv [m/min/mm] × 16.67 so “Kv = 2” on a machine tool means 33.3 s⁻¹. STABILITY LIMIT: Kv ≤ ωbw,velocity/4 (rad/s), roughly. A 50 Hz velocity loop (314 rad/s) supports Kv up to about 78 s⁻¹. You cannot buy accuracy by raising Kv past what the velocity loop supports; you buy oscillation.

Following error is not a fault — it is the loop working. It only becomes a fault when it is uncontrolled. Two axes moving together with different Kv values will each lag by a different amount, so a straight diagonal line becomes a slightly curved one; this is why matched Kv across interpolating axes is a commissioning requirement on any machine that cuts or draws a path.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

An axis runs at 300 mm/s with Kv = 30 s⁻¹, so it lags 10 mm behind the command. You need the lag below 0.1 mm. What do you do?

C — velocity feedforward. The error exists because the position loop must generate the speed command out of an error signal. Feedforward hands the velocity command straight to the velocity loop, so the position loop only has to correct the residue: with 100 % feedforward the steady-state following error at constant speed goes to nearly zero without touching a single feedback gain. Add acceleration feedforward and the error during acceleration collapses too. This is standard practice on every CNC and packaging machine.

A is arithmetically correct and physically impossible. Kv = 3000 s⁻¹ would need a velocity loop above 12 000 rad/s — nearly 2 kHz — which no mechanical system will tolerate. The axis would scream and trip.

B trades one problem for a worse one. Integral action in the position loop eventually drives the error to zero, but it stores history: after a long move it overshoots, and on a machine with backlash or stiction it hunts. Almost no commercial motion controller enables it by default, and for good reason.

D is wrong, but it is the belief that leaves machines running with 10 mm of lag and an operator compensating in the part program.

Tune feedback for stability. Tune feedforward for accuracy. Doing it in the other order is the most common tuning mistake in industry.
  • 1
    Anti-windup. Whenever the drive saturates — torque limit, current limit, a jammed axis — the integrator keeps accumulating error it cannot act on. Without clamping or back-calculation the axis lurches when the obstruction clears. Every commercial drive implements it; every custom controller forgets it once.
  • 2
    Filters cost phase. A low-pass on the velocity feedback smooths encoder noise and lets you raise gain — but every filter adds phase lag, which subtracts from the very margin you were buying. A notch filter is cheaper in phase than a low-pass, which is why it is preferred for resonance (page 10).
  • 3
    Sampling is a delay. A loop sampled at 1 kHz carries roughly 1.5 ms of effective delay, which costs about 27° of phase at 50 Hz. This is why faster drives really do tune to higher bandwidths, and why the fieldbus cycle time (page 12) is part of the control design, not an IT detail.

REFLECTED INERTIA — EVERY MECHANISM, ONE IDEA CORE

The motor does not feel the load; it feels the load as transformed by the mechanism between them. Every transmission scales inertia by the square of its ratio, because kinetic energy must be conserved: ½Jω² on one side has to equal ½Jω² on the other.

The rule is really about stiffness, not inertia. A direct-drive torque motor happily runs a 100:1 inertia ratio because the load is bolted to the rotor — there is no spring in between, so there is no resonance to excite. The same 100:1 through a long belt is unusable. When somebody quotes “keep it under 10:1”, what they mean is: keep the transmission resonance far above your control bandwidth, and the inertia ratio is the convenient proxy for that.

THE TWO-MASS MODEL — WHERE THE RINGING COMES FROM STRETCH
Motor JM and load JL joined by a torsional spring K. Two frequencies matter: ANTI-RESONANCE ωar = √(K / JL) Above it the motor and load stop moving together: the motor spins against the spring and the LOAD NO LONGER FOLLOWS. This caps how fast you can genuinely control the load, no matter what the motor encoder says. RESONANCE ωr = ωar √(1 + JL/JM) The gain peak. Loop gain crossing here means oscillation. It is the frequency you put a NOTCH FILTER on.   • 1
    Stiffness beats mass. ωar goes as √K, so doubling the belt stiffness only buys 41 % more bandwidth — but halving the load inertia buys the same 41 %, and a 3:1 gearbox divides it by nine. Reduce inertia first, stiffen second, filter last.
  • 2
    Backlash is worse than compliance. A spring is linear and can be modelled and notched; backlash is a non-linear dead band whose effective gain changes with amplitude, so the axis can be stable during a large move and buzz during a small one. Preloaded nuts, anti-backlash gearing and helical or bellows couplings exist for this reason.
  • 3
    Where compliance hides: long couplings, belt spans, keyways and set screws (a set-screw coupling on a keyed shaft is a hinge), plastic gearing, unsupported screw ends, thin machine frames, and the machine feet themselves. On a bad day the resonance you are chasing is the frame, not the drivetrain.
  • 4
    Measure, do not guess. Most modern drives can run a frequency-response measurement and plot the mechanical Bode response in an hour. Doing that once on a problem machine replaces a week of gain-fiddling with a number you can act on.

JERK — THE THIRD DERIVATIVE THAT DECIDES HOW A MACHINE FEELS STRETCH

Jerk is the rate of change of acceleration, and since torque is proportional to acceleration, jerk is the rate of change of torque. A trapezoidal profile demands an instantaneous torque step at each corner — and a step contains energy at every frequency, including whichever one your machine happens to resonate at. That is why a mechanically perfect axis can still leave a part vibrating.

  • 1
    What S-curve buys. Limiting jerk rolls the torque on and off, removing the high-frequency content that excites structural modes. Machines settle faster, belts last longer, liquids do not slosh, and delicate products stay on the conveyor — and it usually improves effective cycle time even though the move itself is longer, because settling time falls.
  • 2
    What it costs. A full S-curve adds roughly tjerk = a/j to each of the four corners; the move gets longer by about a/j overall for the usual double-S formulation. Choose j so the added time is 10–20 % of the move, not 100 %.
  • 3
    Input shaping is the sophisticated alternative: instead of smoothing the whole profile, the controller convolves the command with a short impulse sequence tuned to cancel one specific resonant mode. It removes residual vibration almost completely, at the cost of needing to know the frequency — and of failing if the frequency moves (a winder whose roll grows, for instance).
  • 4
    Registration and print-mark correction add a live offset to a running profile so the axis phases itself to a mark on the product. This is the profile layer of the flying-shear, labeller and cut-to-length machines that dominate Sri Lankan packaging plants.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A pick-and-place makes a 250 mm move in 0.6 s and the part wobbles at the end of every move, adding 0.25 s of settling before the gripper can open. What is the best first change?

B. The wobble is residual vibration excited by the torque step at the end of deceleration — the axis has arrived, but the structure and the part are still moving. Jerk limiting removes the high-frequency content of that step. Typically it lengthens the move by 30–80 ms and removes 250 ms of settling: a net cycle-time gain, which is the counter-intuitive part.

A makes it worse — higher acceleration means a bigger torque step and more excitation, so the machine gets faster to the point and shakes harder when it gets there.

C addresses the wrong thing. Raising Kv tightens the servo’s control of the motor; the wobble is in the structure and the part, mostly outside the loop. Beyond a point it makes the drive fight the vibration and injects more energy.

D is a real engineering answer and sometimes the right one — stiffening the gripper mount or adding damping fixes the mechanism rather than working around it. But it is hardware, and B is a parameter you can test in five minutes. Try the free experiment first.

THE SIZING MISTAKES THAT REACH PRODUCTION STRETCH
  • 1
    Sizing on peak torque alone. The axis moves, so it looks correct. Then the drive trips on I²t overload after twenty minutes of running — exactly the page-1 symptom. RMS torque is the thermal check and it is the one that gets skipped.
  • 2
    Forgetting gravity on a vertical axis. A vertical axis needs holding torque at standstill, more torque going up than down, and a brake that is engaged before the drive is disabled — with the sequencing built into the program, not left to chance. Counterbalances and gas springs reduce the motor but complicate the dynamics.
  • 3
    Ignoring gearbox efficiency and its own inertia. A planetary at 0.9 efficiency adds 11 % to every load torque, and its input-stage inertia can be comparable to a small motor’s rotor.
  • 4
    Sizing at rated speed instead of continuous-duty speed. The continuous torque curve droops at high speed on many servo motors. Read the torque available at your actual operating speed, not at zero.
  • 5
    Ambient temperature and mounting. Catalogue continuous torque assumes a specified ambient (often 40 °C) and a large aluminium mounting plate acting as a heatsink. Mount the motor on a thin steel bracket inside an unventilated guard in a Sri Lankan factory and you will not get catalogue performance. Derate, or measure.
  • 6
    No margin for reality. Aim for RMS torque at 70–80 % of continuous and peak at 70–80 % of peak. The remaining 20 % pays for friction that grows, product that gets heavier, and the day production asks for a faster cycle.

Tuning — Deliberately, Not Hopefully

THE PROCEDURE, IN ORDER CORE
  • 1
    Get the inertia right first. Either enter the calculated Jtotal or run the drive’s inertia identification move. Every gain that follows is proportional to it (Kvp = J·ωbw), so a wrong inertia makes every later step wrong by the same factor.
  • 2
    Leave the current loop alone unless the motor is not the drive’s own. It is set from L and R at the factory and is rarely the problem.
  • 3
    Velocity loop, proportional only. Raise Kvp until the axis buzzes or squeals at a small jog, then back off to half that value. Note the frequency you heard — that is your mechanical resonance, and it is free information.
  • 4
    Add the integral term as an integral time Ti ≈ 3–5 × (1/ωbw). Too fast and it eats phase margin and overshoots; too slow and the axis is soft against load disturbance.
  • 5
    If a resonance blocks you, filter it — then try more gain. A notch at the measured frequency costs far less phase than a low-pass. Most drives allow two or three notches; needing more than two is a sign the machine, not the tuning, is the problem.
  • 6
    Position loop last: Kv ≈ ωbw,velocity/4. Verify with a real move, not a step.
  • 7
    Now add feedforward — velocity first, then acceleration — and watch the following-error trace collapse without touching a feedback gain (page 6).
  • 8
    Prove it on the duty cycle, not on a jog. Record following error, torque and speed for a full production cycle at production rate, and check the torque trace against the motor’s continuous rating (page 9). Save the parameter set and the traces — that file is the machine’s fingerprint for its whole life.

FILTERS, OBSERVERS AND THE LIMITS OF AUTO-TUNE STRETCH

Notch filter

f, Q, depth

Removes gain at one frequency for almost no phase cost elsewhere. Needs the frequency to be stable — a winder whose roll grows will walk out of the notch.

Low-pass

phase cost

Cheap noise reduction, but every pole costs phase margin. Set it as high as the noise allows, not as low as the noise tempts you.

Disturbance observer

est. load

Estimates the unmeasured load torque and cancels it — better disturbance rejection without raising feedback gain.

Adaptive / auto-tune

one point

Identifies inertia at one operating condition. Excellent starting point; blind to backlash, to varying inertia and to structural modes it did not excite.

Vibration suppression

<50 Hz

Vendor functions that damp low-frequency machine sway (gantries, tall arms) by shaping the command. Very effective when the frequency is known.

Gain switching

2 sets

One gain set for moving, a softer one at standstill (or with a gripped part). Standard on machines whose inertia changes between full and empty.

When auto-tune is genuinely the right tool: a new axis with unknown inertia, a well-built stiff mechanism, and a competent modern drive. When it is not: anything with backlash, anything whose inertia changes during the cycle, anything you have already had to notch, and anything where the auto-tune result changes each time you run it — which, as on page 1, is the machine telling you the answer does not exist.

CABLES, EMC AND THE FAULTS THEY CAUSE CORE

A servo drive switches hundreds of volts in tens of nanoseconds, several thousand times a second, into a cable that runs next to your feedback wiring. Everything below is standard practice, and every item on the list corresponds to a real fault that appears intermittently and gets blamed on the drive.

  • 1
    Screened motor cable, bonded 360° at BOTH ends — through an EMC gland at the drive and at the motor. A screen bonded by a “pigtail” wire is an inductor and does almost nothing at the frequencies that matter.
  • 2
    Separate the feedback cable from the motor cable — different trays where possible, crossing at 90° where they must meet. Encoder faults that appear only when the axis is at one end of the cable chain are usually this, plus a tired connector.
  • 3
    Keep the motor cable short and within the drive’s stated length. Long cables raise capacitive charging current, worsen reflections and dv/dt stress at the motor terminals, and can trip the drive on earth-leakage detection. Beyond the limit, use a dv/dt or sine filter.
  • 4
    Common-mode current causes bearing damage. High dv/dt drives a capacitive current through the motor bearings, pitting and fluting the races — the same electrical-discharge mechanism described in A55 and detectable early by vibration analysis (A42). Cures: proper 360° bonding, a low-impedance earth between motor and drive, insulated bearings or a shaft grounding ring on larger machines.
  • 5
    One earth star, and mean it. Drive, motor frame, machine frame and panel bonded to a common point with wide, short conductors. Most “noise” problems are actually earth-loop problems (see A46).
  • 6
    Thermal design of the panel. Drives derate above 40 °C and above 1000 m altitude, and they need the vertical clearance the manual specifies. In a Sri Lankan factory a sealed panel in direct sun can sit 15 °C above ambient; size the panel cooling with the drive losses (roughly 3–5 % of throughput power) and check it in the afternoon, not in the morning.

The idea worth carrying away: electronic camming replaced the mechanical line shaft. A twentieth-century packaging machine had one motor, a shaft down its length, and cams and gears defining every relationship — changing the product meant machining new cams. The modern equivalent is a virtual master axis and a downloadable cam table, so a product changeover is a recipe selection. That, more than any single component, is what “servo” changed about machine building.

THE NETWORK IS PART OF THE LOOP STRETCH
Where the loops actually close, and what the bus must deliver: DRIVE-BASED (cyclic synchronous POSITION mode, CSP): controller sends a position setpoint every cycle; the drive closes position, velocity and current locally. → bus cycle 0.5–4 ms is fine; the drive's own loops run at kHz. CONTROLLER-BASED (drive in torque or velocity mode): the position loop lives in the controller, so the LOOP DELAY IS THE BUS. → needs 125–500 µs cycles and tight jitter, or bandwidth collapses. Delay costs phase: φ ≈ 360° × f × tdelay a 1 ms round trip at 50 Hz = 18° of phase margin, gone JITTER MATTERS MORE THAN AVERAGE CYCLE TIME. A bus that is usually 250 µs but occasionally 900 µs injects a disturbance at exactly the rate it stumbles. This is why motion buses use hardware synchronisation — EtherCAT distributed clocks, PROFINET IRT, CIP Sync — rather than best-effort Ethernet. See A03 EtherCAT and A37 TSN.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A flying shear must cut a moving web every 300 mm. Which architecture is right?

C. A flying shear must do two different things within one master revolution: match web speed exactly while the knife is engaged, then return quickly and wait. That is not a constant ratio, so gearing cannot express it — it is a profile as a function of master position, which is precisely what a cam table is. Adding registration correction lets the cam phase itself to a printed mark, so the cut lands on the mark rather than at a fixed pitch, absorbing web stretch.

B is the classic near-miss. Gearing the knife to the web at a fixed ratio gives correct average pitch and a knife that is at the wrong speed at the moment of contact — a torn edge, or a broken knife.

A cannot work: a timer is open loop against a web whose speed varies, so the cut length drifts with every speed change.

D is what the machine used to be, and it works — until you need a different cut length, at which point it needs a new cam machined and fitted. The whole economic argument for servo motion is that C is a recipe change and D is a workshop job.

If you can write a cam table — and explain why the follower must match master velocity at the engagement point — you can build a flying shear, a rotary filler, a labeller or a cut-to-length line, which is most of the packaging industry in this country.

Safe Motion

A servo axis is a machine-safety problem wearing a control-engineering costume. The good news: the drive itself can be the safety device.

THE OLD WAY AND THE NEW WAY CORE

Traditionally, making an axis safe meant removing power: a contactor between drive and motor, dropped by a safety relay. It works, and it has three costs — the axis coasts uncontrolled, the drive loses its position reference and needs re-homing, and a contactor switching a PWM output is an unhappy contactor.

Modern drives implement safety functions internally to IEC 61800-5-2, certified to a Performance Level (ISO 13849-1) or SIL (IEC 62061). The power stage is disabled through two independent channels inside the drive, so no motor contactor is needed — and functions richer than “off” become possible.

FIVE THINGS THAT ARE ROUTINELY GOT WRONG STRETCH
  • 1
    STO does not hold a vertical axis. Removing torque from a hoist or a vertical slide means it falls. Vertical axes need SBC with a correctly sized, correctly sequenced brake — brake engaged before torque is removed — and a periodic brake test, because a worn brake fails silently.
  • 2
    Safe speed needs safe feedback. Ordinary encoders are not safety devices. Certified functions rely on a safety-rated encoder, or two diverse sources cross-checked (for example encoder plus the drive’s own back-EMF estimate). Do not assume a drive’s SLS is rated without reading its safety manual.
  • 3
    A safety function is not a control function. “We set the speed limit in the program” is not SLS. The distinction is independence and diagnostic coverage — a single PLC variable is neither.
  • 4
    Setup mode is where people get hurt. Production runs behind interlocked guards; commissioning and changeover happen with the guard open, an enabling device in hand, and the axis moving. That mode is what SLS, SDI and SLP exist for, and it is the mode most often left unengineered.
  • 5
    The required Performance Level comes from a risk assessment, not from a catalogue. ISO 12100 for the assessment, ISO 13849-1 for PLr and architecture, IEC 62061 for SIL, IEC 61800-5-2 for what the drive provides. The chain must be complete — sensor, logic and drive all at the required level. See A52.

Commissioning & Fault-Finding

The order you do things in decides whether commissioning takes a morning or a fortnight — and every alarm is a sentence, not a number.

COMMISSIONING, IN ORDER CORE
  • 1
    Mechanics first, with the motor uncoupled if possible. Turn the mechanism by hand: it should be smooth, free of notches and free of slack. Anything you can feel with your hand, the servo will have to fight for the machine’s whole life.
  • 2
    Power and safety wiring proved — earthing and 360° screens, STO wiring tested to prove it actually disables the drive, and the E-stop chain verified physically rather than on paper.
  • 3
    Motor and feedback identification — correct motor file, encoder type, and the magnetic pole alignment / auto-phasing routine (page 5). Then confirm the direction convention: positive command must give positive machine motion and increasing position count. Ten minutes here saves a day of confusion later.
  • 4
    Limits, in three layers: hardware over-travel switches wired to the drive or safety circuit, software limits inside them, and a following-error window inside those. Test each layer by driving into it deliberately, slowly, before anything runs at speed.
  • 5
    Homing — choose the method (limit switch plus index, home sensor plus index, absolute encoder with a stored offset, or torque/hard-stop homing) and verify repeatability over ten cycles with a dial gauge. Homing repeatability is the ceiling on the machine’s accuracy, and it is often the real cause of “the machine drifts”.
  • 6
    Tune at low speed, then at production speed (page 10), and record traces of the full duty cycle.
  • 7
    Set the protection to protect the machine: torque limit low enough that a jam is detected before something bends, following-error window tight enough to catch a slipping coupling, over-speed set sensibly. A drive left at factory limits will happily destroy a mechanism.
  • 8
    Back everything up — drive parameters, motion program, cam tables and the tuning traces — and put the file where the next engineer can find it in two years. This is the single most neglected step in the industry.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

Eight ideas that carry the module, six ways real axes fail, and six questions that test whether the ideas transferred.

THE EIGHT IDEAS CORE
  • 1
    A servo is a loop, not a motor. Three nested loops — current, velocity, position — each 4 to 10 times faster than the one commanding it.
  • 2
    FOC makes the motor a torque source. Two rotations of the reference frame turn three AC currents into id (flux, held at zero) and iq (torque), and then T = Ktiq.
  • 3
    Kvp = Jtotal × ωbw. Gain and inertia are locked together, which is why changing the machine changes the tuning — and why nobody should change a driven element without telling the control engineer.
  • 4
    Following error = v/Kv. Reduce it with feedforward, not with gain. Feedback buys stability; feedforward buys accuracy.
  • 5
    Reflected inertia scales with 1/n². A gearbox is the most powerful inertia tool you have — but it does not move the load-on-spring frequency √(K/JL); only stiffness or real load inertia does.
  • 6
    Size on RMS torque, not on peak. Peak tells you whether it moves; RMS tells you whether it survives the shift.
  • 7
    Jerk decides how the machine feels. A trapezoid steps torque and rings the structure; an S-curve trades a little move time for a lot of settling time.
  • 8
    Most servo faults are mechanical or electromagnetic. Inertia, stiffness, backlash, earthing and screening — the loop is usually the messenger, not the culprit.
SIX WAYS REAL AXES FAIL
1 · Undersized on RMS torque
Peak checks passed, thermal check skipped. Fingerprint: works perfectly, trips on overload after 10–30 minutes, worse in the afternoon. Prevention: compute Trms over the whole cycle including dwell, keep it under 80 % of continuous, and re-check whenever production speeds the machine up.
2 · Inertia ratio raised by a mechanical change
A bigger roller, a heavier fixture, a longer arm — and gains that no longer fit. Fingerprint: sluggish or ringing, never both right; auto-tune gives a different answer each run. Prevention: treat driven-element changes as control changes; recompute J and the anti-resonance.
3 · Resonance and compliance
Belts, long couplings, set-screw joints, thin frames. Fingerprint: a specific frequency in the audible buzz and in the following-error trace; the axis is stable on big moves and buzzes on small ones (that one is backlash). Prevention: stiffen, reduce inertia, notch — in that order.
4 · Feedback corrupted by EMC
Unbonded screens, feedback laid beside the motor cable, pigtail earths. Fingerprint: random encoder or comms faults, often position-dependent in the cable chain, often worse as the machine ages. Prevention: 360° bonding both ends, separation, one earth star — and replace tired cable-chain cables before they fail.
5 · Regeneration with no path
High-inertia axis decelerating hard into a drive with no or undersized brake resistor. Fingerprint: over-voltage trips only on deceleration, worse at higher speed or faster cycles. Prevention: compute ½Jω² × stops per second and size both the ohms and the watts; consider a shared DC bus on multi-axis machines.
6 · Vertical axis without brake discipline
Brake released before torque is available, or engaged after the drive is disabled, or never tested. Fingerprint: the axis drops a few millimetres at enable or disable — and one day, all the way. Prevention: correct sequencing in the program, SBC through the safety system, and a periodic brake test (page 13).
✓ QUICK CHECK — SIX QUESTIONS

Q1: Why must the current loop be far faster than the velocity loop?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The velocity loop is designed on the assumption that its output — a torque command — is produced instantly. If the current loop is slow, that assumption fails: its dynamics appear as extra phase lag inside the velocity loop, eating the phase margin and forcing the gains down. Making the inner loop 4–10× faster pushes its dynamics far enough out of the way that the outer loop can treat the motor as an ideal torque source. It is also physically easy to do, because the electrical time constant L/R is much shorter than any mechanical one.

Q2: An axis has JM = 2.0×10⁻⁴ and reflected load 1.8×10⁻³ kg·m². What velocity gain gives a 60 Hz bandwidth, and what is the inertia ratio?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Jtotal = 2.0×10⁻⁴ + 1.8×10⁻³ = 2.0×10⁻³ kg·m². ωbw = 2π(60) = 377 rad/s.
Kvp = J·ω = 2.0×10⁻³ × 377 = 0.754 N·m per rad/s.
Inertia ratio = 1.8×10⁻³/2.0×10⁻⁴ = 9:1 — at the top of the comfortable band, so 60 Hz is achievable if the transmission is stiff. Check √(K/JL) before committing: you want the anti-resonance above about 180 Hz.

Q3: A machine must index 400 mm in 0.8 s. Using the 1/3 rule, what velocity and acceleration are needed, and does it reach full speed?

Tap to reveal ↓A: v = 1.5 d/t = 1.5 × 0.4/0.8 = 0.75 m/s; a = 4.5 d/t² = 4.5 × 0.4/0.64 = 2.81 m/s².
Check: dmin = v²/a = 0.5625/2.81 = 0.20 m, which is less than 0.4 m, so the profile is trapezoidal and it does reach full speed — running at 0.75 m/s for the middle third, exactly as the rule intends.

Q4: Why does velocity feedforward reduce following error when raising Kv would destabilise the axis?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Following error exists because the position loop must manufacture the velocity command out of a position error: e = v/Kv. Feedforward supplies that velocity command directly from the trajectory generator, so the feedback loop only has to correct the residual. Crucially it is open loop — it adds no phase lag and moves no pole, so it cannot destabilise anything. Raising Kv instead pushes the loop crossover towards the mechanical resonance, which is where instability lives. Feedback for stability, feedforward for accuracy.

Q5: A vertical axis drops about 3 mm every time it is enabled. What is happening, and what is the fix?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The brake is releasing before the drive is producing holding torque, so for a few tens of milliseconds gravity is unopposed. (The mirror-image fault at disable is torque being removed before the brake has engaged.)

Fix: correct the sequencing — enable the drive, wait for the “servo ready / torque present” signal, then release the brake; on disable, engage the brake, wait for its mechanical delay (typically 30–100 ms, and it is on the datasheet), then remove torque. Most drives have brake-control parameters for exactly this and they are frequently left at defaults. Also verify the brake still holds its rated torque — a worn or oil-contaminated brake gives the same symptom, and on a safety-relevant axis the brake test is a specified function (page 13).

Q6: A machine builder proposes running a 40:1 inertia ratio through a toothed belt because “the drive has vibration suppression”. Respond.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Vibration suppression and notch filters remove gain at a frequency; they do not add stiffness. With a 40:1 ratio through a belt, the load-on-belt mode √(K/JL) is low, so the achievable bandwidth for controlling the load is capped at roughly a third of it — no matter what the drive does — and the motor encoder will happily report a beautifully settled motor while the load is still swinging.

The professional response is a number, not an opinion: ask for JL, JM and the belt stiffness, compute √(K/JL), and compare it with the bandwidth the cycle time demands. If the machine needs 60 Hz and the mechanics offer 25 Hz, the specification is not achievable and no drive feature changes that. Then offer the alternatives in cost order: reduce the load inertia, fit a reduction (which fixes the ratio and the torque), stiffen the transmission, or accept a longer cycle time.

Note the honest exception: a 40:1 ratio is entirely normal on a direct-drive table, because there is no spring in between. The number alone is not the problem — the number plus compliance is.

WE1 · SIZE A BALL-SCREW AXIS FROM SCRATCH CORE

Horizontal table, moving mass 150 kg. Ball screw 1200 mm long × 40 mm diameter, 20 mm lead, direct coupled, efficiency 0.9. Guides: µ = 0.01 plus 30 N of preload drag. Duty: move 600 mm in 0.9 s, once every 3.0 s. Candidate motor: 1 kW, 3000 rpm, 3.18 N·m continuous, 9.5 N·m peak, JM = 6.7×10⁻⁴ kg·m². Does it work?

STEP 1 — INERTIA (everything referred to the motor shaft) screw: m = 7850 × π × 0.020² × 1.2 = 11.84 kg J = ½mr² = ½ × 11.84 × 0.020² = 2.368×10⁻³ table: J = m(p/2π)² = 150 × (0.020/6.2832)² = 1.520×10⁻³ coupling: 0.200×10⁻³ Jload = 4.088×10⁻³ kg·m² INERTIA RATIO = 4.088e−3 / 6.7e−4 = 6.1 : 1 ✓ comfortable STEP 2 — PROFILE (1/3 rule) v = 1.5 × 0.6/0.9 = 1.00 m/s a = 4.5 × 0.6/0.81 = 3.333 m/s² motor speed = v/p = 1.00/0.020 = 50 rev/s = 3000 rpm ✗ NO MARGIN motor accel α = (a/p)·2π = 166.7 × 6.2832 = 1047 rad/s² STEP 3 — TORQUE friction force = 0.01 × 150 × 9.81 + 30 = 44.7 N Tfriction = F·p/(2πη) = 44.7 × 0.020/(6.2832 × 0.9) = 0.158 N·m Taccel = (JM+Jload)α = 4.758e−3 × 1047 = 4.98 N·m Tpeak = 4.98 + 0.16 = 5.14 N·m vs 9.5 peak → 54 % ✓ Tdecel = 4.98 − 0.16 = 4.82 N·m (friction helps on the way down) STEP 4 — RMS OVER THE 3.0 s CYCLE (0.3 s each phase, 2.1 s dwell) ΣT²t = 5.14²(0.3) + 0.158²(0.3) + 4.82²(0.3) = 7.93 + 0.01 + 6.98 = 14.92 Trms = √(14.92/3.0) = 2.23 N·m vs 3.18 continuous → 70 % ✓

Verdict: three checks pass and one fails. The axis needs exactly 3000 rpm, which is the motor’s rated speed — no margin for a faster cycle later, and continuous-duty torque is often derated at rated speed. Fix it in the mechanics, not the motor: a 25 mm lead drops the requirement to 2400 rpm (80 %). Recomputing, the inertia ratio rises to 7.7:1, the peak torque becomes 5.07 N·m and Trms falls slightly to 2.18 N·m — all still comfortable. One component change converts a marginal axis into a specified one, which is why sizing is done before the drawings are released, not after.

WE2 · THE OPTIMUM GEAR RATIO STRETCH

Return to the page-1 roller: JL = 5.497×10⁻³ kg·m², JM = 1.35×10⁻⁴ kg·m², required load motion 0 → 500 rpm in 0.10 s (αload = 523.6 rad/s²). Which reduction ratio minimises the motor torque, and what does the answer cost?

Motor torque as a function of ratio n (pure inertia, reduction n:1): αmotor = n · αload Jtotal = JM + JL/n² T(n) = (JM + JL/n²)·n·αL = (JMn + JL/n)·αL dT/dn = (JM − JL/n²)αL = 0 → nopt = √(JL/JM) and at that ratio JL/n² = JM — the reflected inertia EQUALS the motor inertia. The famous 1:1 “inertia match” is not a rule of thumb; it is the solution of this derivative. NUMBERS: nopt = √(5.497e−3/1.35e−4) = √40.7 = 6.38 : 1 → motor speed = 500 × 6.38 = 3190 rpm ✗ above a 3000 rpm rating T(1) = (1.35e−4 + 5.497e−3) × 523.6 = 2.95 N·m T(5) = (6.75e−4 + 1.099e−3) × 523.6 = 0.93 N·m speed 2500 rpm ✓ T(6.38) = (8.61e−4 + 8.62e−4) × 523.6 = 0.90 N·m speed 3190 rpm ✗ n = 5 gives 97 % of the theoretical benefit with 500 rpm of margin in hand.

Two lessons, both general. First, the optimum is flat — anywhere within about ±30 % of nopt costs only a few per cent of torque, so choose a standard ratio and a speed you are comfortable with rather than chasing the exact number. Second, the constraint that actually binds is almost never torque — it is motor speed, gearbox input-speed rating, or the available catalogue ratios. Optimise, then round to what you can buy.

What the derivation ignores: gearbox inertia (add it to JM’s side), efficiency, backlash and cost. For a load dominated by friction or gravity rather than inertia the analysis is different — there, higher reduction always reduces motor torque, and speed alone limits you.

WE3 · FOLLOWING ERROR, MISMATCHED AXES AND CONTOUR ERROR CORE

A two-axis gantry cuts a 45° diagonal at 300 mm/s path feed. X is tuned to Kv = 30 s⁻¹, Y to Kv = 25 s⁻¹, feedforward off. How far is the cut from where it should be — and what happens on a circle?

Axis velocities on a 45° path: vx = vy = 300/√2 = 212.1 mm/s ex = vx/Kv,x = 212.1/30 = 7.07 mm ey = 212.1/25 = 8.48 mm BOTH axes lag — but they lag by DIFFERENT amounts, and only the difference bends the path. The component perpendicular to a 45° path is: contour error = (ey − ex)/√2 = 1.41/1.414 = 1.00 mm off the line If both axes had Kv = 30: ex = ey = 7.07 mm — the tool is 10 mm behind along the path, but the PATH IS STILL STRAIGHT. Lag is harmless; MISMATCH is not. This is why matched Kv is a commissioning requirement. CIRCLES ARE LESS FORGIVING. Tracking radius R at path feed F (ω = F/R), each axis follows a sinusoid and the lag shrinks the circle: ΔR/R ≈ ½(ω/Kv)² (small-angle, feedforward off) R = 50 mm, F = 500 mm/s → ω = 10 rad/s, Kv = 30: ΔR = 50 × ½ × (10/30)² = 2.8 mm of radius lost Turn on velocity + acceleration feedforward and both effects nearly vanish. That is why no CNC in the world runs with feedforward off.
WE4 · REGENERATION AND A BRAKE RESISTOR CORE

A rotary indexer has Jtotal = 1.2 kg·m² referred to the motor, runs to 500 rpm, decelerates in 0.25 s, and indexes every 2.0 s. Drive: 400 V class, chopper threshold 780 V, bus capacitance 940 µF. About 30 % of the kinetic energy is absorbed by friction and motor losses. Specify the braking resistor.

ΔEcap = ½C(V2² − V1²) = ½ × 940×10⁻⁶ × (780² − 566²) = ½ × 940e−6 × 288 044 = 135 J — only 12 % of it. → the chopper and resistor must take the remaining ~1016 J every index. WE5 · WHY THE SLOWER PROFILE IS THE FASTER MACHINE STRETCH

A pick-and-place makes a 250 mm move with v = 0.625 m/s and a = 3.125 m/s², and then waits 0.25 s for the part to stop wobbling before the gripper may open. Jerk limiting at j = 60 m/s³ is available. Is it worth it?

TRAPEZOIDAL tmove = d/v + v/a = 0.25/0.625 + 0.625/3.125 = 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.600 s + settling 0.250 s → 0.850 s per move S-CURVE (double-S, jerk-limited) extra time ≈ a/j = 3.125/60 = 0.052 s → tmove = 0.652 s residual vibration largely removed, settling falls to ~0.05 s → 0.702 s per move SAVING = 0.148 s per move = 17 % of the move-plus-settle time On a machine doing one such move per 1.2 s cycle: new cycle 1.05 s → +14 % throughput, from a parameter change.

The general principle: cycle time is move time plus settling time, and the two trade against each other. Aggressive profiles minimise the first and inflate the second. Most machines that feel “maxed out” are not torque-limited at all — they are waiting for a structure to stop ringing, and the cure is jerk limiting, input shaping, or stiffness — not a bigger motor.

How to prove it on a real machine in twenty minutes: trace following error at the end of a move. If it decays with visible oscillation, you are settling-limited and this example applies to you. If it snaps flat and the torque trace is saturated during acceleration, you are torque-limited and you need page 9 instead.

Friction: F = 0.02 × 80 × 9.81 = 15.7 N → at pinion 0.471 N·m → at motor 0.471/(8 × 0.95) = 0.062 N·m
Tpeak ≈ 2.95 N·m

THE TRAP — the speed check fails. ωpinion = 1.2/0.03 = 40 rad/s → motor = 320 rad/s = 3056 rpm, above the 3000 rpm rating.

Resolve it, and show the trade: drop to 6:1 → motor speed 2292 rpm ✓, but Jref = 0.072/36 + 8e−5 = 2.08×10⁻³, ratio 6.9:1, αmotor = 1440 rad/s² and Tpeak = 2.38e−3 × 1440 ≈ 3.43 N·m. Lower ratio buys speed margin and costs torque — that is the trade of WE2, met in the wild. Alternatives worth a mark: a larger pinion (raises speed capability, worsens inertia), a slightly longer move time, or a higher-speed motor.
P4  [8 marks] · THE SHORTEST LEGAL CYCLE

An axis needs 4.0 N·m for 0.25 s, then 0.6 N·m for 0.25 s, then 3.6 N·m braking for 0.25 s, then dwells at 0.05 N·m. The motor is rated 2.0 N·m continuous. What is the shortest cycle time the motor will survive?

Tap to reveal ↓Moving contribution: ΣT²t = 16.0(0.25) + 0.36(0.25) + 12.96(0.25) = 4.00 + 0.09 + 3.24 = 7.33
Dwell contributes 0.05² × td = 0.0025 td — negligible, but note that you kept it.

Require Trms ≤ 2.0:   √(7.33/(0.75 + td)) ≤ 2.0
→ 7.33/(0.75 + td) ≤ 4.0 → 0.75 + td ≥ 1.833
→ td ≥ 1.08 s  →  minimum cycle time 1.83 s

Comment for the last two marks: that is the thermal limit with zero margin, so the honest engineering answer is about 2.2–2.3 s (RMS at ~80 % of continuous), leaving room for friction that grows and a hot afternoon. Note also what this problem shows: the dwell is part of the motor rating. Every time production shortens the cycle without changing a single motion parameter, they are re-sizing your motor.
P5  [10 marks] · TWO-MASS RESONANCE

JM = 5×10⁻⁴, load JL = 6×10⁻³ kg·m² (at the motor shaft), coupling stiffness K = 900 N·m/rad. Find the anti-resonance and resonance frequencies, the maximum sensible velocity bandwidth, and state what a 2:1 belt reduction would and would not change.

Tap to reveal ↓ωar = √(K/JL) = √(900/6×10⁻³) = √150 000 = 387 rad/s = 61.6 Hz
Inertia ratio = 6e−3/5e−4 = 12 : 1
ωr = ωar√(1 + 12) = 387 × 3.606 = 1397 rad/s = 222 Hz
Maximum sensible velocity bandwidth ≈ ωar/3 = 129 rad/s = 20.5 Hz for genuine control of the load (you may run the motor loop faster, but above 61.6 Hz the motor and the load are no longer moving together).

A 2:1 reduction: refers BOTH the inertia and the stiffness by n²: Jref = 1.5×10⁻³ and Kref = 225 N·m/rad. So √(Kref/Jref) = √150 000 = 387 rad/s — UNCHANGED. What does change is the inertia ratio, 12:1 → 3:1, which lowers the resonant peak to ωar√4 = 774 rad/s (123 Hz) and greatly weakens the interaction, and it divides the required motor torque by 2.

The examinable point: a gearbox changes the RATIO and the TORQUE, never the load-on-spring frequency. To move that you must stiffen the coupling (∝√K) or reduce the real load inertia.
P6  [8 marks] · FOLLOWING ERROR AND FEEDFORWARD

An axis has Kv = 25 s⁻¹ and runs at 400 mm/s. (a) What is the following error? (b) With 90 % velocity feedforward? (c) Cutting a 100 mm-radius circle at 200 mm/s with feedforward off, what radius error results?

Tap to reveal ↓(a) e = v/Kv = 400/25 = 16 mm
(b) With 90 % of the velocity command supplied by feedforward, the feedback loop only has to generate the remaining 10 %: e ≈ 1.6 mm — a tenfold improvement without touching a gain.
(c) ω = F/R = 200/100 = 2 rad/s.
ΔR/R ≈ ½(ω/Kv)² = ½(2/25)² = 0.0032 → ΔR = 0.32 mm — the circle is cut undersize by that amount, all the way round.

Worth a mark: the radius error grows with the square of feed rate, so doubling the feed quadruples the error — the classic reason a part is in tolerance at 50 % feed and out of tolerance at 100 %. Also note that a circular test (the ballbar of A40) separates this smooth radius reduction from backlash spikes at the quadrants, because the two look completely different on the plot.
P7  [10 marks] · BRAKE RESISTOR

A machine decelerates J = 0.8 kg·m² from 1200 rpm in 0.40 s, 20 times per minute. Assume 30 % of the energy is absorbed by losses; chopper threshold 780 V. Specify the resistor and state two installation requirements.

Tap to reveal ↓ω = 1200 × 2π/60 = 125.7 rad/s
E = ½Jω² = ½ × 0.8 × 15 790 = 6316 J per stop
To the bus: 0.70 × 6316 = 4421 J

Peak power = 4421/0.40 = 11.05 kW → R = 780²/11 050 = 55 Ω
Average power = 4421 × (20/60) = 1474 W → specify ≥ 2 kW continuous

Installation requirements (any two): (1) R must not be below the drive’s stated minimum resistance, or the chopper transistor fails — check the manual before ordering; (2) the resistor gets genuinely hot and must be mounted outside the enclosure, or inside it with the panel cooling sized for another 1.5 kW; (3) it needs a thermal cut-out wired into the drive’s enable or the safety chain, so a stuck chopper cannot start a fire; (4) resistor cable is a switched high-voltage circuit — screened, short and kept away from feedback wiring.

Design comment: 1.5 kW average is substantial. Before buying the resistor, ask whether the deceleration must be that fast, and whether a shared DC bus or a regenerative front end pays for itself — at 20 stops a minute this is roughly 13 000 kWh a year thrown away as heat.
P8  [10 marks] · DIAGNOSIS FROM A PATTERN

An axis trips on following error, but only on the fast return stroke, and only after the machine has been running for about an hour. It never trips in the morning. Diagnose systematically and give the tests you would run, in order.

Tap to reveal ↓What the pattern says. Following error means the axis could not keep up — almost always torque saturation. Two clues narrow it hard: fast stroke only → the demand is highest there, so the margin is smallest; only when warm → something is temperature-dependent.

Candidate causes, in likelihood order:
1. Marginal sizing — Trms or peak too close to the rating, so the drive’s thermal model reduces available current as it heats (I²t derating), and the fastest stroke is the first to fail.
2. Rising mechanical friction — a bearing, a guide, a seal or a dragging brake that worsens as grease thins or a component expands.
3. Motor temperature reducing torque — winding resistance rises, and NdFeB magnets lose roughly 0.1 %/°C of remanence, so a hot motor makes measurably less torque per amp.
4. Panel or ambient temperature derating the drive — a sealed panel in an afternoon, exactly the Sri Lankan case (page 11).
5. Following-error window set tighter than the tuning supports — the fault is real but the threshold is the wrong one.

Tests, in order: (a) trace torque, speed and following error for a cold cycle and a hot cycle and overlay them — if the torque trace is clipping, it is saturation and the question is only which cause; (b) measure motor and panel temperature at the moment of failure; (c) with the motor uncoupled, turn the mechanism by hand cold and hot, and compare the drive’s no-load torque both ways; (d) confirm the brake fully releases (measure its coil voltage and listen); (e) recompute Trms for the current cycle time, which may not be the one the machine was sized for.

What not to do: widen the following-error window. That deletes the machine’s only warning that it is being overworked, and the next thing to fail will be mechanical.
P9  [10 marks] · SPECIFY THE ARCHITECTURE

A cut-to-length line needs six axes: unwind (tension), two feed rollers, a flying-shear knife, a stacker and a discharge conveyor. Cut length 300–3000 mm, web speed to 120 m/min, cut to a printed registration mark. Specify the control architecture, the network, the motion modes and the safety approach, with reasons.

Tap to reveal ↓Architecture: one motion controller (PLC with motion, or a dedicated motion CPU) plus six drives running in cyclic synchronous position (CSP), each closing its own position, velocity and current loops. Do not close position loops across the network for this machine — there is no need, and it would make the bus a control-loop element (page 12).

Master: a virtual master axis representing line position. Everything gears or cams to it, so the line can be jogged, phased and restarted coherently and no axis inherits another’s error.

Modes per axis: unwind — torque/tension control with a dancer or load-cell loop and a diameter calculation; feed rollers — electronic gearing to the virtual master; knife — electronic camming, with the cam matching web speed through the cut and returning fast (WE-style reasoning of page 12); stacker and discharge — independent moves triggered by the master position.

Registration: hardware touch-probe / latch input on the mark sensor, feeding a phase correction into the cam. At 120 m/min = 2 m/s, one 10 ms PLC scan is 20 mm of error — so this must be hardware latching, not ladder logic.

Network: a synchronised motion bus — EtherCAT with distributed clocks, PROFINET IRT or equivalent — at a 500 µs to 1 ms cycle with low jitter (A03, A37).

Safety: guarding with interlocks; STO on every drive; SS1 where a coast is unacceptable; SLS plus an enabling device for threading and setup, which is the mode where hands are near the knife; safety over the same bus (FSoE or PROFIsafe) to avoid a parallel wiring loom. Risk-assess to ISO 12100 and determine PLr per ISO 13849-1 (A35, A52).

One thing to check before quoting: the knife axis is a high-inertia, high-acceleration, high-duty axis — size it on RMS torque across the shortest cut length (the highest cut rate), and compute the regeneration, because it decelerates hard on every cut.
P10  [12 marks] · SYNTHESIS — AND A REFUSAL

The page-1 labelling machine has been fitted with the 3:1 gearbox and now runs correctly. Production now asks for a 30 % faster cycle, and the maintenance manager suggests “just turning the gains up, since the gearbox gave us headroom”. Give a complete engineering answer, with numbers, and state one thing you would refuse.

Tap to reveal ↓What 30 % faster actually demands. If the same move is made in 0.77 of the time, velocity rises by 1.30× and acceleration by 1.69× (a ∝ 1/t²). Torque scales with acceleration, so accel torque rises by the same 1.69, and Trms rises similarly while the cycle also gets shorter — a double squeeze on the thermal check (P4).

Run the four checks again, in order (page 9):
1. Speed: with the 3:1 gearbox the motor already runs 3× roller speed; ×1.3 more may exceed rated speed. This is the check most likely to fail first.
2. Peak torque: geared accel torque was about 0.98 N·m; ×1.69 → ~1.66 N·m, still inside a 7.2 N·m peak. Fine.
3. RMS torque: recompute over the new cycle including the shorter dwell — this is the check that decides.
4. Inertia ratio: unchanged at 4.5:1, so tuning headroom exists.

On “turn the gains up”: partly right and dangerously incomplete. The gearbox did restore tuning headroom, so a higher velocity-loop bandwidth is now legitimate — but the 83 Hz load-on-belt mode has not moved (P5), so bandwidth is still capped near ωar/3, and gains alone add no torque and no speed. Gains do not make an axis faster; the profile does, and the motor has to pay for it.

What I would actually do: (a) recompute the four checks with the new profile; (b) get the speed increase from jerk-limited profiling and acceleration feedforward first — on a machine that rings, most of the 30 % is usually hiding in settling time (WE5); (c) only then raise the bandwidth, with a measured frequency response rather than by ear; (d) re-verify regeneration, because a 1.69× faster deceleration raises peak regen power by the same factor (page 11); (e) re-run the risk assessment if guard-opening times or stopping distances change — a faster machine has a longer stopping distance, which changes the safety-distance calculation (A35).

What I would refuse: to raise the gains and the speed without recomputing Trms and without a trace, and to widen the following-error window or the overload threshold to stop the resulting alarms. That converts a machine that warns into a machine that breaks, and the failure will arrive as a torn winding or a snapped belt during a production run rather than as an alarm on a Tuesday afternoon.

Marking: 3 for the a ∝ 1/t² scaling, 4 for the four checks in order, 3 for the correct treatment of gains versus profile, 2 for the refusal with its justification.

Derivations & Traps

Four results the module leaned on, rebuilt from first principles — because a formula you can reconstruct is one you will still trust at 2 a.m. on a commissioning job.

DERIVATION 1 · WHY Kt AND Ke ARE THE SAME NUMBER CORE
Take an ideal, lossless machine — no resistance, no friction, no iron loss. Energy must balance instantaneously: ELECTRICAL POWER IN = MECHANICAL POWER OUT E · I = T · ω Substitute the two defining relations: (Keω) · I = (KtI) · ω Ke · ωI = Kt · ωI → Ke = Kt exactly, in SI units: Ke in V·s/rad (= Wb) and Kt in N·m/A CHECK THE DIMENSIONS — they are the same quantity, flux linkage: V·s/rad = Wb N·m/A = J/A = (V·A·s)/A = V·s ✓ WHY DATASHEETS DISAGREE ANYWAY: Ke quoted in V/krpm → multiply by 60/(2π×1000) = 0.00955 line-to-line vs phase → a factor of √3 peak vs RMS → a factor of √2 3/2 from the power-invariant vs amplitude-invariant Park transform Verification that always works: Trated/Irated from the same datasheet must equal the Kt that datasheet prints. If it does not, you have the wrong convention, not the wrong motor.
DERIVATION 2 · VELOCITY-LOOP GAIN IS INERTIA × BANDWIDTH CORE
Assume the current loop is fast enough to be ideal, so the drive is a pure torque source. Newton's second law for rotation, in Laplace form: T(s) = Jtotal · s · ω(s) → PLANT ω(s)/T(s) = 1/(Jtotals) Close a proportional loop of gain Kvp around it: T = Kvp(ω* − ω) ω = (1/(J s)) · Kvp(ω* − ω) ω (1 + Kvp/(J s)) = (Kvp/(J s)) ω* ω/ω* = Kvp / (J s + Kvp) = 1/(1 + s·J/Kvp) A first-order lag with time constant τ = J/Kvp, hence bandwidth ωbw = Kvp/Jtotal ⇒ Kvp = Jtotal · ωbw THREE CONSEQUENCES, ALL PRACTICAL: 1. Gain must scale with inertia. Change the machine, change the gains — which is the whole of page 1 in one line. 2. Bandwidth is what you should specify, not gain. “60 Hz velocity loop” transfers between machines; “Kvp = 0.75” does not. 3. The result assumes ONE rigid inertia. The moment a spring appears between motor and load, the plant is no longer 1/(Js) and this formula gives the gain for a bandwidth the mechanics may refuse to deliver (page 7).
DERIVATION 3 · REFLECTED INERTIA AND THE OPTIMUM RATIO STRETCH
PART A — why inertia divides by n². Energy is energy, whichever side of the gearbox you stand on: ½ Jload ωload² = ½ Jref ωmotor² with ωmotor = n · ωload ½ Jload ωload² = ½ Jref n² ωload² → Jref = Jload/n² (torque divides by n, speed multiplies by n) The same argument, with v = ωp/2π, gives the ball-screw result Jref = m(p/2π)² and the rack result Jref = mr². One idea, every mechanism. PART B — the ratio that minimises motor torque for an inertial load. T(n) = (JM + JL/n²) · αmotor and αmotor = n · αload T(n) = (JMn + JL/n) · αload dT/dn = (JM − JL/n²) · αload = 0 nopt = √(JL/JM) and there JL/nopt² = JM — the celebrated 1:1 INERTIA MATCH is simply where this derivative vanishes. HOW FLAT IS THE OPTIMUM? Write r = n/nopt: T(r)/Tmin = (r + 1/r)/2 → r = 0.8 or 1.25 costs only 2.5 % r = 0.5 or 2.0 costs 25 % So choose a standard ratio near nopt and spend your attention on the SPEED limit, which is the constraint that usually binds (WE2). CAVEAT: this optimises TORQUE for a purely INERTIAL load. For a friction- or gravity-dominated load, higher reduction always helps and only speed limits you.
DERIVATION 4 · FOLLOWING ERROR, AND WHY FEEDFORWARD DELETES IT CORE
The position loop is proportional: the velocity command IS the error, scaled. ω* = Kv · e where e = x* − x At constant commanded velocity v, the axis must actually be moving at v, so the loop has to be producing ω* = v. The only way it can produce v is to HOLD an error: v = Kv · e → e = v/Kv The error is not a defect — it is the mechanism by which the loop makes the command. A P-only loop CANNOT track a ramp with zero error; this is the standard type-1 system result (see A10). NOW ADD FEEDFORWARD. Let the trajectory generator inject its own velocity: ω* = Kv·e + Kff·v* At steady state we still need ω* = v, so: v = Kve + Kffv → e = v(1 − Kff)/Kv Kff = 0 → e = v/Kv (no feedforward) Kff = 0.9 → e = 0.1·v/Kv (ten times better) Kff = 1.0 → e = 0 (perfect, in theory) WHY IT CANNOT DESTABILISE ANYTHING: Kffv* is a function of the COMMAND, not of the measurement. It appears in the numerator of the closed-loop transfer function and nowhere in the denominator — so it moves no pole, adds no phase lag, and changes no stability margin. It can only be wrong. WHY Kff = 1 is not used blindly: the model is never exact (delays, filters, friction), so 90–98 % is typical, with acceleration feedforward Kff,a·J·a* handling the transients and the remaining error mopped up by feedback.

The page-1 roller swap was a sensible mechanical improvement made by competent people, and it cost three weeks of production. A two-line change-note — “driven inertia changes; recompute inertia ratio and re-tune” — would have prevented all of it.

HONEST GAPS — WHAT THIS MODULE DID NOT COVER

Four areas were left out deliberately. Multi-axis kinematics — forward and inverse transforms for delta, SCARA and articulated robots, singularities and Jacobians — belongs with A15. Modern control for servos — state-space, observers, H-infinity, iterative learning control and repetitive control for cyclic machines — is where the research literature actually is, and it is genuinely useful for high-precision applications. Linear and direct-drive systems got a paragraph rather than the page they deserve, particularly thermal management and the metrology of linear scales. And drive power-stage design — the inverter itself, modulation strategies, current sensing — is the subject of A29 and A28.

What you do have is the complete working set: how the loops fit together, where every gain comes from, how to reflect and size any mechanism, how to shape a move, how to tune deliberately, how to wire it so the feedback can be believed, and how to make it safe — enough to specify and commission an industrial axis, and to know precisely which of those four gaps you have walked into when a machine surprises you.
