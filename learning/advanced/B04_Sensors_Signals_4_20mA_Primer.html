Cold Open — Four Instruments, All Reading, All Lying

DEODORISER 2, AN EDIBLE-OIL REFINERY NEAR SAPUGASKANDA CORE

A refinery takes crude coconut and palm oil and, in the last stage, strips the free fatty acids and odour compounds out under deep vacuum and high temperature. The whole stage is a temperature loop, a vacuum loop, a steam-flow measurement and a level measurement. Four instruments. Everything else — the pumps, the valves, the PLC, the operators — is downstream of what those four numbers say.

In February the plant started missing its peroxide-value and colour specification on grade changes. By June the maintenance log had 61 entries. Three vendors had been called. The DCS graphics were beautiful, every trend was smooth, and not one alarm had been raised by any of the four instruments. Every loop was “in service”, every reading was inside its range, and every one of the four numbers on the operator's screen was wrong.

That last sentence is the whole reason this primer exists. A sensor that dies is a small problem — the reading goes to zero or the alarm fires and someone replaces it in an hour. A sensor that lies plausibly is an expensive problem, because every human decision downstream is made in good faith on a number that is not true. The four faults below are the four classic ways a measurement chain lies while looking healthy, one from each layer of the chain.

temperature steam flow level vacuum fault
FOUR FAULTS, ONE VESSEL CORE
⚠ D1 · the installation layer

The oil temperature RTD was replaced in January with a 6 mm element pushed into an 8 mm bore thermowell, dry, and inserted only 45 mm into a 200 mm line, just downstream of an elbow. The reading is correct at steady state and only 1.1 °C low. But its time constant went from 12 s to 96 s. The temperature loop was then “retuned” to stop it hunting, which detuned it, and grade-change settling went from 6 to 22 minutes.

⚠ D2 · the maths layer

The stripping-steam flow is an orifice plate with a differential-pressure transmitter. DP is proportional to the square of flow, so somewhere in the chain a square root must be taken. In the 2019 DCS it was taken in the transmitter. In the 2024 PLC migration the transmitter was replaced with one configured linear and nobody added the root in the PLC. At the normal 62 % operating point the flow reads 38.4 %. The mass balance was out by 38 % and the plant paid LKR 2.4 M for a steam-leak survey that found nothing.

⚠ D3 · the electrical layer

A chart recorder was added to the same steam-flow loop in March, in series, with its own 250 Ω input. The loop already had a 340 Ω intrinsic-safety barrier, a 250 Ω PLC input and 620 m of cable. The 24 V supply ran out of voltage at 13.37 mA. The indication now has a hard ceiling at 58.6 % of range — above that, the reading simply stops rising, perfectly smoothly, with no alarm.

⚠ D4 · the earthing layer

The level transmitter's cable shares 38 m of tray with the 55 kW feed-pump VFD motor cable, and its shield is clamped to earth at both ends. A 1.8 V potential difference between the two earth bars pushes 2.0 A around the shield, and the level reading carries 0.64 mA pk-pk of hash — 4.0 % of span, 0.24 m. Six nuisance high-level trips a month, always at night, always “unrepeatable”.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Of the four faults, which one would a routine annual calibration of the instrument have caught?

None — and this is the single most useful idea on the page. A bench calibration disconnects the transmitter from the process, from its cable, from its barrier and from the PLC, and then checks the one thing that was never wrong: the transmitter's own input-to-output relationship. D1 lives in the thermowell (removed for the calibration). D2 lives in the PLC's scaling code (not present at the bench). D3 lives in the loop's voltage budget — and the bench calibrator has a short lead and no barrier, so it always passes. D4 lives in the cable tray.
A bench calibration certifies the transmitter A loop check certifies the transmitter + cable + barrier + burden + card + scaling + the number on the screen Three of the four faults above are only visible from the right-hand end of that second line.
This is why a competent commissioning engineer insists on an end-to-end loop check: inject a known signal at the field terminals and read the number on the HMI. It is slower, it needs two people and a radio, and it is the only test that would have found D2 and D3. Page 15 shows how to run one properly.

What Transfers from Mechanical Engineering — and What Does Not

YOU HAVE BEEN DOING MEASUREMENT THEORY FOR YEARS CORE

THE THREE THINGS THAT DO NOT TRANSFER CORE
  • 1
    A mechanical gauge cannot fail silently in the middle of its scale. If the Bourdon tube splits, the needle drops to zero and you can see it. An electronic transmitter can output a perfectly plausible 11.3 mA while measuring nothing at all — and 11.3 mA is 45.6 % of range, which looks like a normal Tuesday. Every diagnostic idea in this primer exists to defeat that one difference.
  • 2
    The signal path has its own physics. A brass linkage does not pick up 50 Hz from a nearby cable, does not care whether the panel earth and the field earth are at the same potential, and never runs out of voltage. A pair of wires does all three. Pages 6, 7 and 12 are entirely about this.
  • 3
    Somewhere the number becomes an integer, and integers have edges. A dial has infinite resolution within the width of the needle. An analogue input card has 4 095 or 27 648 steps and nothing in between, and a scaling equation written by a human who may have got it wrong. D2 in the cold open is exactly this: no physical part failed; a line of configuration was missing.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A pressure transmitter's datasheet says ±0.1 % of span. The transmitter is ranged 0–25 bar and the process actually runs at 2.0 bar. How accurate is the reading, as a percentage of what it is reading?

±1.25 % of reading — and this is the single most misread line on any instrument datasheet. “% of span” means the error is a fixed number of engineering units, computed from the full scale, and it does not shrink when the reading does.
span = 25.0 bar error = 0.1 % of 25.0 = 0.025 bar, everywhere on the scale at 20.0 bar 0.025 / 20.0 = 0.125 % of reading fine at 2.0 bar 0.025 / 2.0 = 1.25 % of reading ten times worse at 0.5 bar 0.025 / 0.5 = 5.0 % of reading the instrument is now decorative
The practical rule that falls straight out of this: never range an instrument far wider than the process needs. A 0–25 bar transmitter on a 0–3 bar process is not “safe headroom”; it is an eightfold accuracy penalty you paid for voluntarily. This is called turndown, and page 9 puts numbers on it. Compare it with a datasheet that says “% of reading” — that error does shrink with the reading, which is why Coriolis flowmeters are quoted that way and orifice plates are not.
THE FIVE WORDS PEOPLE USE INTERCHANGEABLY AND SHOULD NOT STRETCH

Accuracy

How close to the true value. Needs a reference you trust. Systematic error.

Precision / repeatability

How close repeated readings are to each other. Random scatter. A precise instrument can be consistently wrong.

Resolution

The smallest change the chain can represent. Set by the ADC and the scaling, not by the sensor.

Sensitivity

Output change per unit input, dy/dx — the slope. A gain, not an error.

Uncertainty

The honest interval you are prepared to defend, with a coverage factor. What a metrologist writes instead of “accuracy”.

Drift

Any of the above changing with time or temperature. The reason calibration has an interval.

A worked distinction you can carry: a Coriolis meter with 0.1 % accuracy and 0.05 % repeatability is the right choice for custody transfer — you need the absolute number to be right. A cheap turbine meter with 2 % accuracy but 0.1 % repeatability is perfectly adequate for batch dosing, because you will calibrate the recipe against the actual product and only ever need the meter to do the same thing twice. Choosing on accuracy when you needed repeatability is one of the most common and most expensive specification errors in industry.

THE TRANSFER FUNCTION — THE ONE EQUATION UNDER EVERYTHING CORE

Fraden opens his Handbook of Modern Sensors with this and then spends 700 pages on special cases. A sensor is a function S = f(x) mapping the measurand x to an output S. Almost all industrial instrumentation forces that function to be a straight line, because straight lines are trivially invertible:

S = a + b·x a = offset (the zero) b = sensitivity (the span) Recovering x from S: x = (S - a) / b Real elements are not straight. Three ways the industry copes: 1 Restrict the range use only the part that is straight enough (a thermocouple over 200 °C) 2 Linearise in the transmitter a polynomial or lookup table inside the device Pt100: R(t) = R0 (1 + A t + B t²) A = 3.9083e-3, B = -5.775e-7 (IEC 60751) type K: an 8th-to-10th order polynomial per range (IEC 60584) 3 Linearise in the controller the square root for DP flow lives here or in the transmitter — and if both or neither do it, you get fault D2

The two-point calibration you will do on a bench is nothing more than solving for a and b from two known points. Apply 0 °C, trim the output to 4 mA; apply 300 °C, trim to 20 mA. Everything in between is assumed to lie on the line, which is why a five-point check (0, 25, 50, 75, 100 %) exists: it is the only way to see whether the assumption is still true.

💼

When a vendor quotes “linearity ±0.05 % BSL”, BSL is best straight line — the deviation from the line that minimises the worst error, not from the line through the endpoints. “Terminal-based linearity” uses the endpoints and always looks worse. Same instrument, different marketing.

Digital or Analogue — a Choice, Not a Fact

A BIT OR A NUMBER CORE

The same physical quantity can arrive as either. A tank has a level; you can bring it in as a float switch (one bit: full / not full) or as a hydrostatic transmitter (a number: 3.47 m). Which you choose changes the cost, the wiring, the program, the diagnostics and the failure behaviour — and engineers who default to “analogue is better” waste a great deal of money.

Everything upstream of it is a current; everything downstream is a voltage into an ADC. If you understand that one sentence you can debug most of the loops you will meet.

WHERE THE 0–10 V CONVENTION STILL WINS STRETCH

It is fashionable to sneer at 0–10 V. Be fair to it. Inside one enclosure, over 1.5 m of wire, from a PLC analogue output to a VFD speed reference, a voltage signal is cheaper, needs no burden resistor and no loop supply, and behaves perfectly. Machine builders across Ekala and Ja-Ela use it by the thousand and are not wrong. The rule is about distance and shared earths, not about superiority:

  • ✓
    Voltage is fine when the source and the receiver sit in the same enclosure, on the same earth bar, with less than a couple of metres of wire and nothing switching inductive loads next to it.
  • ✗
    Voltage stops being fine the moment the cable leaves the panel — because now the two ends are on different earths, the cable is in a tray with motor cables, and there is no way to tell a broken wire from a genuine zero. Use 4–20 mA, or an isolator, or both.

There is one more consideration that catches people converting between the two: a VFD's analogue input is typically 10–30 kΩ. Paralleling four drives onto one PLC analogue output drops the load to about 2.5–7.5 kΩ, which most AO cards can still drive — but each drive also has its own 0 V reference, and now you have four earth paths in parallel with your signal return. That is a ground loop with four branches. The right answer is one 4–20 mA output daisy-chained through all four drives in series — one current, four listeners, no shared reference. Page 6 explains why that works.

Discrete Sensors — Sinking, Sourcing, and the Leak That Is Not a Fault

THREE WIRES, AND WHICH WAY THE CURRENT GOES CORE

B03 introduced PNP and NPN as the commonest I/O mistake in the world. This page finishes the job: the transistor inside, what happens electrically when you get it wrong, the two-wire and NAMUR variants, and the three quiet failure modes that look like a faulty sensor and are not.

+24 V 0 V / common signal current PNP NPN
The rule in one line, and it is the only one you need to remember: The sensor and the input card must want the current to flow the same way. PNP (sourcing) sensor → needs a sinking input (card common on 0 V) PNP = card common 0 V NPN (sinking) sensor → needs a sourcing input (card common on +24 V) NPN = card common +24 V Get it wrong: no damage, no LED, no fault code, no clue. Just a sensor that "does not work".

Why does the naming feel backwards? Because PNP and NPN describe the transistor, and sinking/sourcing describes the terminal. A PNP output transistor sits between +V and the output pin, so when it turns on it sources current out of the sensor. An NPN output transistor sits between the output pin and 0 V, so it sinks current into the sensor. The sensor and the card are always opposite words: a sourcing sensor needs a sinking input. Say it out loud twice and it will stay.

💼

PNP dominates European machinery, and therefore most equipment imported into Sri Lanka from Germany and Italy. NPN is common on Japanese, Korean and older Chinese equipment. A plant that has bought from both has two sensor families in the same drawer, in identical M12 barrels, and the only difference is one letter on the label. Mark your stores bins. This single habit saves more hours per year than any tool you can buy.

NAMUR deserves a moment. It is the discrete world's answer to the diagnostic problem from page 4: because the “off” state still draws about 1 mA, a 0 mA reading means a broken wire and a very high reading means a short. It is standard on hazardous-area and safety-related proximity detection — you will meet it on burner management, on ESD valve position feedback, and on anything intrinsically safe. Its logic is exactly the logic of the 4–20 mA live zero, applied to one bit.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A machine has 16 two-wire proximity switches on one input card. With every switch physically clear of its target, six of the sixteen inputs read TRUE. Wiring checked, sensors swapped, same six. What is going on?

Leakage current — and it is not a fault in either device. A two-wire sensor must draw a residual current in its OFF state to power its own oscillator. A PLC input has a defined region where it must read FALSE and another where it must read TRUE, set by IEC 61131-2:
IEC 61131-2 DC input types, 24 V (the numbers that decide this argument) Type state 0 guaranteed state 1 guaranteed typical use Type 1 < 5 V and < 15 mA > 15 V and > 2 mA mechanical contacts, relays Type 2 < 5 V and < 30 mA > 11 V and > 6 mA 2-wire proximity sensors ← the fix Type 3 < 5 V and < 15 mA > 11 V and > 2 mA modern low-power electronic sensors Sensor leakage 1.7 mA into a Type 3 input needing only 2 mA to read TRUE -> sits 85 % of the way to the threshold. Cable capacitance, a warm panel or a slightly high supply pushes it over. Six of sixteen, apparently at random.
Three legitimate fixes, in the order a competent engineer tries them: (1) use a Type 2 input card, which is specified to ignore up to 30 mA in the OFF state; (2) fit a bleeder resistor across the input to divert the leakage — roughly 5.6 kΩ / 2 W across 24 V passes 4.3 mA and pulls the input firmly low; (3) change to 3-wire sensors, which have a proper 0 V return and negligible leakage. Replacing the card with an identical one, which is what usually happens, changes nothing and costs a day.
THREE MORE QUIET FAILURES ON DISCRETE INPUTS CORE
⚠ Input filter longer than the event

Every DC input has a hardware and software filter, typically 0.1–3 ms hardware and a configurable 0.1–20 ms software filter, to reject contact bounce and noise. A 1.2 ms pulse from a high-speed part detector into an input filtered at 3 ms is simply deleted. Combine with the scan cycle from B03 and you need the pulse to survive filter + scan.

⚠ Capacitive charging on long cables

A 300 m multicore has roughly 100 pF/m core-to-screen, so 30 nF. A 3-wire sensor turning on must charge that through its output impedance; turning off, the cable discharges through the input. On very fast signals this smears edges and on very sensitive Type 3 inputs it can hold an input on for milliseconds after the sensor releases.

⚠ No wetting current on a dry contact

Modern low-power inputs draw 2–3 mA. Gold-flashed relay contacts and limit switches need a minimum wetting current, often 5–10 mA, to punch through the oxide and sulphide film that grows in a humid coastal plant. Below it the contact makes intermittently — the classic “it works when I wiggle it”. The fix is the same bleeder resistor.

⚠ Sensor supply and card common on different 0 V

Two 24 V supplies, two 0 V rails, not bonded. The input current has no defined return path and behaves erratically with load. Bond the 0 V rails at one point, or use a single supply. This is the discrete cousin of the earthing problem on page 12.

Why Current and Not Voltage — the Four Real Reasons

FIRST, KILL THE MYTH CORE

Ask ten engineers why the process industry uses 4–20 mA and eight will say “because voltage drops over a long cable”. That answer is arithmetically wrong, and believing it means you cannot debug a real loop. Let us do the sum.

620 m of 1.0 mm² copper (310 m out, 310 m back), at 60 °C in a hot tray: R = ρL/A = 0.0175 × 620 / 1.0 = 10.85 Ω at 20 °C R(60 °C) = 10.85 × (1 + 0.00393 × 40) = 12.56 Ω A 0-10 V signal into a PLC analogue input of 100 kΩ, source impedance 100 Ω: error = (100 + 12.56) / (100 + 12.56 + 100 000) = 0.001124 = 0.112 % of reading ... which is smaller than the card's own accuracy. The IR drop is not the problem.

So why is a 0–10 V signal a genuinely bad idea over 620 m? Four reasons, none of them IR drop. Understand these four and you will never again guess at a loop problem.

REASON 1 — A REGULATED CURRENT SOURCE REJECTS INDUCED VOLTAGE CORE

A cable running beside a motor cable has a voltage induced in series with it — the transformer effect, from the changing magnetic field around the power conductors. Call it en. What that induced voltage does depends entirely on the output impedance of the source.

VOLTAGE SIGNAL into a high-impedance input The induced e_n is in series with the signal and the input draws no current, so essentially all of it appears across the input: error = e_n 100 mV of pickup on a 0-10 V signal = 1.0 % of span CURRENT LOOP driven by a 2-wire transmitter The transmitter is a regulated current source. Its job is to hold the loop current at the commanded value whatever the loop voltage does. Its output impedance R_out is enormous - megohms at DC. The induced e_n drives an extra current: i_n = e_n / (R_out + R_loop) 100 mV, R_out = 2 MΩ, R_loop = 600 Ω -> i_n = 0.05 µA = 0.0003 % of span Roughly 3 200 times better - and note it has nothing to do with cable resistance. Above the transmitter's regulation bandwidth (a few kHz) R_out falls and rejection degrades gracefully toward the passive case i_n = e_n / R_loop.

That last line matters for a modern plant: a VFD's switching noise is at 2–16 kHz with edges far faster than that, so it is above the transmitter's regulation bandwidth. The loop is not magic against VFD hash — which is exactly why fault D4 exists, and why page 12 is about screens and separation rather than about signal type.

REASON 2 — ONE CURRENT EVERYWHERE, SO JOINTS DO NOT MATTER CORE

Kirchhoff's current law says the current is the same at every point in a series loop. That single fact has a huge practical consequence: a corroded junction-box terminal, a marginal crimp, a long cable, an extra 40 Ω anywhere — none of them change the signal. They only eat voltage headroom (page 7). A voltage signal has no such protection: every resistance in the path is part of a divider with the input impedance, and every junction between the field and the panel is a place where the divider can change.

REASON 3 — THE LIVE ZERO CORE

The single cleverest thing about the standard: zero is not zero. The bottom of the range is 4 mA, not 0 mA. Everything below 4 mA is therefore free diagnostic space.

The live zero is the reason 4–20 mA outlived every competitor.

REASON 4 — POWER AND SIGNAL DOWN THE SAME TWO WIRES CORE

This is the reason that saves the most money. Because the lowest signal is 4 mA and never zero, the transmitter can live on its own signal current. It draws 3.5 mA or so to run its electronics and modulates the total between 4 and 20 mA to convey the measurement. Two wires carry the power out and the measurement back, with no third core, no local supply, no isolator per point.

Available power to a loop-powered transmitter, worst case (lowest current, lowest voltage): P = V_tx,min × I_min = 12 V × 4 mA = 48 mW That 48 mW budget is why: - loop-powered radar level gauges took until the 2000s to exist - loop-powered devices have slow update rates and small displays - Ethernet-APL matters: it delivers ~500 mW into Zone 0 (page 14), a 10x change And in a hazardous area it is better than convenient - it is the whole safety case. Intrinsic safety works by limiting the energy that can enter the area. A circuit that can only ever pass ~22 mA at ~26 V cannot produce an igniting spark. A 2-wire 4-20 mA loop is almost the definition of an intrinsically safe circuit, which is why IS barriers (page 7) exist as an off-the-shelf 340 Ω component.

Over a 300-instrument plant that is several kilometres of extra copper, extra glands, extra terminals and extra earthing arguments.     Remove burden, do not add volts. Ask what each resistor is for. A chart recorder that nobody reads, an indicator duplicated on the HMI, an old trip amplifier — take them out. Free, instant, and it improves reliability.
  • 2

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Where should the square root be taken — in the transmitter or in the PLC?

In the PLC, with the transmitter left linear in DP — that is the current best practice, and there is a real technical reason, not just a preference.
If the ROOT is taken in the TRANSMITTER, the 4-20 mA is linear in FLOW. At 10 % flow, Δp is only 1 % of the DP span. The transmitter must resolve a 1 % DP change and stretch it to 10 % of the current output - so any DP noise is amplified by the root's steep slope near zero: d(√x)/dx = 1/(2√x) -> at x = 0.01 the gain is 5.0 at x = 1.00 the gain is 0.5 Result: a noisy, jumpy signal at low flow, and a "low-flow cutoff" that the transmitter applies internally to hide it - which then hides real low flow too. If the loop stays LINEAR IN DP and the PLC roots it: the 4-20 mA carries the raw physics; the PLC can apply the cutoff, the damping and the root with full 32-bit precision and you can see the raw DP when diagnosing. You also stop the classic double-root and no-root errors, because there is exactly one documented place where the root is taken.
The rule that prevents fault D2 forever: the loop tag tells you. Name it FT-2104_DP if the signal is DP and FT-2104_FLOW if it is flow. Two extra characters, and the argument can never happen again.
FIVE MORE SCALING TRAPS CORE
⚠ Re-ranged transmitter, unchanged PLC

Someone re-ranges a pressure transmitter from 0–25 bar to 0–10 bar in the field with a HART communicator to improve resolution — a good decision — and does not tell anyone. The PLC still converts 20 mA to 25 bar. Every reading is now 2.5× too high. Ranging lives in two places and must be changed in both.

⚠ Suppressed zero on a wet-leg level

A DP level transmitter with a wet impulse leg sees a negative DP when the tank is empty, so the calibrated range is something like −180 to +420 mmH₂O. Enter 0–420 in the PLC and the empty tank reads 30 %. Elevation and suppression are normal, deliberate, and must be documented on the loop sheet.

⚠ Gauge vs absolute vs differential

A vacuum measurement quoted in “bar” can mean bar a, bar g or mbar abs. At the deodoriser's 3 mbar abs the difference between absolute and gauge is the entire measurement. Always write the suffix.

⚠ Clamping before alarming

Well-meant code clamps the scaled value to 0–100 % before the alarm logic sees it. Now a transmitter sitting at 3.6 mA — a declared device fault — is clamped to a lovely, plausible 0 % and no alarm ever fires. Range-check the raw value first, then clamp. Page 11.

Accuracy, Error and Uncertainty — a Tolerance Stack-Up in Disguise

YOU HAVE DONE THIS BEFORE, IN MILLIMETRES CORE

Stack five machined parts and ask how much the assembly can vary. Add the tolerances for the absolute worst case; root-sum-square them for the realistic case. A measurement chain is the same arithmetic with different units — and the same two answers, one of which is honest and one of which is defensible.

WORST CASE (arithmetic sum) e_wc = Σ |eᵢ| assumes every error is at its limit, in the same direction, at the same moment. Correct for a guarantee. Pessimistic - the probability is vanishingly small. ROOT-SUM-SQUARE (quadrature) e_rss = √( Σ eᵢ² ) assumes the errors are independent and roughly normal. Correct for a realistic expectation, and what GUM / ISO 17025 calls combined standard uncertainty. Rule of thumb: with n similar terms, e_rss ≈ e_wc / √n. Use worst case for a safety or custody-transfer claim. Use RSS for control performance and for arguing about a mass balance. Never mix them silently, and always say which one you used.

SAMPLING, ALIASING AND THE FILTER YOU MUST NOT SKIP CORE

Once the signal is sampled, Nyquist applies: any component above half the sampling frequency does not disappear — it folds back and appears as a slow, entirely fictitious oscillation that you cannot distinguish from a real process movement.

Sampling at T = 1 s (f_s = 1 Hz). For τ_total = 96 s it is 9.6 s - so fault D1 also made the loop massively oversampled, which is harmless, and disguised the problem further because the trend looked beautifully smooth.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

An operator complains a flow reading is “too jumpy”. You increase the transmitter damping from 0.5 s to 8 s. The trend becomes beautifully smooth. What have you actually done to the control loop?

You moved the problem into a place where nobody will find it. Transmitter damping is a real first-order filter inside the control loop, not a display setting. It costs phase margin exactly like the thermowell air gap does, and the PID will eventually have to be detuned to compensate.
Ask first: what is the noise? real process turbulence -> damping is legitimate. Match it to the loop: τ_damp ≈ 0.1 × τ_process is usually invisible and useful. pump pulsation at a fixed frequency -> fix it hydraulically (a pulsation damper, a different tapping point) or accept and filter deliberately. 50 Hz or VFD pickup -> an earthing fault (page 12). Damping hides a wiring defect and it will come back as something worse. aliasing -> sample faster or filter before the ADC. Damping does help here, because it is analogue and upstream of the converter. Diagnostic that separates them in 30 seconds: put a scope or a fast trend on the raw mA. Process noise is broadband and random. 50 Hz is a clean sinusoid. VFD hash is a burst at the carrier frequency. Aliasing changes frequency when you change T_sample - nothing else does. That last test is definitive and free.

UPSCALE OR DOWNSCALE? THE DECISION NOBODY DOCUMENTS CORE

Every NE 43 transmitter has a configurable failure direction — on fault, drive the current to 3.6 mA (downscale) or to 21.5 mA (upscale). Choose the direction that drives the PROCESS to its SAFE state if the value were believed: Furnace / dryer TEMPERATURE controlling fuel downscale 3.6 mA -> controller believes it is cold -> maximum fuel ✗ DANGEROUS upscale 21.5 mA -> controller believes it is too hot -> fuel closed ✓ SAFE Tank LEVEL controlling a fill valve downscale -> believes empty -> keeps filling ✗ overflow upscale -> believes full -> stops filling ✓ SAFE Cooling-water FLOW with a low-flow trip downscale -> believes no flow -> trips the plant ✓ SAFE (spurious, but safe) upscale -> believes plenty of flow -> runs on with no cooling ✗ DANGEROUS There is no universally correct answer. WHY THE SCREEN IS EARTHED AT ONE END ONLY CORE

This is the argument that every plant has and few resolve, so here is the physics, clearly. A screen has two jobs and they conflict:

THE RULE THAT RESOLVES IT Low-frequency instrument signals (4-20 mA, RTD, thermocouple, mV): earth the screen at ONE end, at the control panel / marshalling side. Insulate and tape back the field end. Never leave it dangling against a gland. Both requirements at once (long run, both LF signal and HF environment): earth one end solidly and the other end through a capacitor (a "hybrid" earth, typically 10-100 nF) - low impedance at RF, open circuit at 50 Hz. Available as a proprietary gland or terminal. When in doubt on a 4-20 mA loop, one end, panel side. If that is not enough, the answer is an isolator, not a second earth.

And the practical detail that gets missed: a cable that passes through a marshalling cabinet and continues on has two screen sections. They must be bonded to each other and earthed once for the whole run — not earthed independently in each cabinet, which is fault D4 with extra steps.

THE INSTALLATION RULES THAT PREVENT ALL OF THIS CORE
  • 1
    Separation. Keep instrument cable at least 300 mm from power cable in parallel runs, 500 mm from VFD output cable. Where they must meet, cross at 90° — a perpendicular crossing couples almost nothing. Separate trays or a metal divider in one tray both work.
  • 2
    Twisted pair, always. Twisting makes the induced EMF in successive half-twists cancel. It is free, it is in every instrument cable already, and it is the only real defence against magnetic coupling. Never split a pair across two circuits.
  • 3
    The VFD motor cable is the aggressor, not the mains. A 50 Hz supply cable couples gently. A VFD output switching at 4 kHz with 5 kV/µs edges radiates broadband to tens of MHz. Screened motor cable, 360° glands at both ends, short and direct — treat this as the first fix, not the last.
  • 4
    One earth reference for the instrument system, bonded to the main earth at a single point, and separate from the “dirty” power/motor earth until that point. This is what an instrument earth bar is for, and it is usually the first thing value-engineered out of a project.
  • 5
    Galvanic isolators where the loop leaves your control — to a package skid, to another building, to a client's system, to anything with its own earth. About LKR 20–35 k per loop, and they eliminate an entire category of fault permanently.
  • 6
    Never run a thermocouple or RTD extension in a multicore with 24 V switched signals. Millivolt signals and inductive load switching in the same sheath is a guaranteed noise problem, and no amount of filtering fixes it.

HART, IO-Link and Ethernet-APL — What Rides on Top

HART — A DIGITAL CONVERSATION HIDDEN INSIDE THE ANALOGUE SIGNAL CORE

The cleverest retrofit in industrial history. HART superimposes a small frequency-shift-keyed signal on the same two wires as the 4–20 mA, at frequencies the analogue loop ignores — so a fully digital device can talk to you over cable installed in 1985, while the analogue reading carries on undisturbed.

HOW IT COEXISTS (Bell 202 FSK) logical 1 = 1200 Hz logical 0 = 2200 Hz ±0.5 mA peak, superimposed 1200 bit/s, about 2-3 updates per second The trick: the modulated signal is symmetric about zero, so its average is zero. The analogue receiver, with its low-pass filter, sees only the DC level. The HART modem, with its band-pass filter, sees only the AC. One pair, two independent channels, neither aware of the other. REQUIREMENT for HART to work at all: loop resistance between 230 Ω and 1100 Ω - the modem needs a load to develop a readable voltage. This is the real reason 250 Ω became universal, and the reason a "clever" 100 Ω burden kills HART on that loop. WHAT YOU GET FOR FREE ON A LOOP YOU ALREADY HAVE - up to four variables per device (PV, SV, TV, QV): a Coriolis meter sends mass flow on the analogue and density, temperature and volume flow over HART - remote re-ranging, damping and unit changes without going to the field - the device's own diagnostics: sensor drift, plugged line detection, electronics temperature, days since calibration, and the reason it went to 3.6 mA - tag, serial number, calibration date - a live asset register - a loop test command: tell the transmitter to output exactly 12.000 mA and walk to the panel to see what the HMI shows. This is the end-to-end check from page 15. HART versions worth knowing: HART 5 the installed base. 8-character tags, one device per loop (or multidrop at 4 mA). HART 7 long tags, WirelessHART, event notification, burst mode, better diagnostics. HART-IP encapsulates HART in TCP/UDP - how gateways, WirelessHART and Ethernet-APL devices deliver HART data to a host at Ethernet speed. Installed base is over 40 million instruments - HART is not legacy, it is the norm.

The uncomfortable industry statistic worth knowing: the large majority of installed HART devices have their digital capability never used. The plant bought the diagnostics, paid for the diagnostics, and reads only the 4–20 mA. A HART multiplexer or a HART-capable AI card costs a fraction of the instruments and turns a wall of dumb loops into a live condition-monitoring system. At the refinery, a HART read of TT-2104 would have reported the sensor drift and the response-time degradation behind fault D1 in about four minutes.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A technician puts a HART communicator on a loop and gets no response, though the 4–20 mA reads correctly. Everything is wired properly. Most likely cause?

Insufficient loop resistance, or something shunting the AC component — and it is almost always one of three things.
1 Burden too low. Total loop R below ~230 Ω. The FSK current has nowhere to develop a voltage. Fix: fit a 250 Ω HART resistor in the loop, or use the communicator's own internal 250 Ω if it has one. 2 A non-HART-transparent barrier or isolator. Older zener barriers and cheap isolators filter or clamp everything above DC. The datasheet must say "HART transparent" or quote a bandwidth above 2.2 kHz. 3 A power supply with too much output capacitance, which is an AC short circuit across the loop. Instrument-grade loop supplies specify a minimum AC impedance (typically > 250 Ω from 500 Hz to 10 kHz) precisely for this reason. Symptom: HART works when you disconnect the supply and use the communicator's own power, and fails when reconnected. That test is diagnostic. And the counter-intuitive one: on a healthy loop you can connect the communicator anywhere in the loop, including across the burden resistor at the PLC terminals, because there is one current everywhere (page 6). You do not have to walk to the field.

ETHERNET-APL — WHAT ACTUALLY REPLACES THE LOOP CORE

Fieldbuses (FOUNDATION Fieldbus, PROFIBUS PA) tried to replace 4–20 mA for thirty years and largely did not, because they solved the wrong problem and added a new commissioning discipline. Ethernet-APL is the credible successor, and it is worth understanding why.

THE FIVE-POINT CHECK, AND WHY FIVE CORE

One rule that separates the professionals: the reference must be at least four times better than the instrument — the 4:1 test accuracy ratio. Calibrating a ±0.1 % transmitter with a ±0.1 % calibrator proves nothing; you cannot tell which one is wrong. And the reference must have a current, traceable certificate, or the whole exercise is theatre.

HOW TO RUN A LOOP CHECK — THE PROCEDURE CORE
  • 1
    Get a permit and inhibit the alarms and trips. Write down every inhibit you place, and who is holding the list. This is the step that gets skipped and the step that causes incidents.
  • 2
    Disconnect the transmitter at its terminals and inject with a calibrator in mA SOURCE mode into the loop's field end — the loop supply is in the panel, so you are replacing the transmitter, not powering the loop. If the loop is 4-wire or has its own supply, open the loop and inject in place.
  • 3
    Inject 4.00, 8.00, 12.00, 16.00, 20.00 mA and have a second person read the value on the operator's HMI, not on the PLC's raw tag. The graphic is what people act on; test the graphic.
  • 4
    Check the arithmetic at 12 mA especially. Both ends can be right while the middle is wrong — a missing square root reads correctly at 4 and 20 mA and reads 25 percentage points low at 12 mA. This one reading finds fault D2.
  • 5
    Now check the top of range with the real transmitter connected. Command the transmitter to output 20 mA (a HART loop-test, or the device's own simulation mode) and confirm the reading reaches 100 %. This is the test that finds fault D3 — the calibrator is a strong source with unlimited compliance and will happily drive 20 mA through a loop the real transmitter cannot.
  • 6
    Inject 3.5 mA and 21.5 mA and confirm the fault alarms actually appear. Untested NE 43 logic is a comfortable assumption, not a diagnostic.
  • 7
    Measure the loop voltage at the transmitter terminals at 20 mA and record it against the datasheet minimum. Ten seconds with a multimeter, and it is the whole of page 7.
  • 8
    Remove the inhibits, sign the sheet, file it. The as-found/as-left record is the plant's memory. Without it, the next engineer is where you were in the cold open.

Step 5 deserves emphasis because it is the trap in the trap. A calibrator injecting current is a much stiffer source than a loop-powered transmitter: it has its own supply and enormous compliance. It will drive 20 mA through 850 Ω without complaint. Test with the calibrator and D3 passes perfectly. Only the real device, at the real top of range, proves the voltage budget.

EIGHT IDEAS TO CARRY OUT OF THIS PRIMER CORE
  • 1
    A sensor that dies is cheap. A sensor that lies plausibly is expensive. Everything in instrumentation design is aimed at the second case.
  • 2
    % of span is not % of reading. Range the instrument to the process, or pay the turndown penalty on every reading for the life of the plant.
  • 3
    The loop has a voltage budget. Write it on the drawing. Rmax = (Vs − Vtx,min) / 0.020.
  • 4
    Current beats voltage because a regulated source rejects series EMF, because there is one current everywhere, because of the live zero, and because two wires carry power and signal. Not because of IR drop.
  • 5
    Take the square root in exactly one place, and put it in the tag name.
  • 6
    Test the raw value before you scale or clamp it, and act on the NE 43 fault, not on the number.
  • 7
    One earth on an instrument screen. Twist beats magnetic, screen beats electric, only isolation beats a potential difference.
  • 8
    A bench calibration proves the transmitter. Only a loop check proves the loop. Inject at 12 mA and read the operator's screen.
SIX WAYS TO GET HURT, OR TO HURT THE PLANT CORE
⚠ Opening an IS loop in a hazardous area

Breaking a live intrinsically safe circuit is normally permitted — that is the point of IS — but only with certified equipment and under the plant's hot-work / gas-test regime. Never substitute an uncertified calibrator into an IS loop.

⚠ Injecting into a live loop with the trips enabled

You will trip the plant, or worse, you will inhibit a trip and forget. Write the inhibit list down and have someone else hold it.

⚠ Removing a thermowell from a live line

The well is the pressure boundary. Removing the element is safe; removing the well opens the process. People have been scalded doing this on a “shut down” line that still held hot oil.

⚠ Two current sources in one loop

Connecting a loop supply to a 4-wire transmitter's already-driven output destroys the AI card and sometimes the transmitter. Check the terminal legend before you land a single wire.

⚠ Shorting a burden resistor to “test” a loop

On a 2-wire loop this is harmless. On a loop with a live voltage source across it, it is a short circuit. Know which you have first.

⚠ Trusting a screen as a safety earth

A foil screen with a drain wire is not a protective conductor. Bond armour and enclosures properly; a screen is for signal integrity only.

QUICK CHECK — FOUR QUESTIONS CORE
1. A loop reads correctly at 4 mA and at 20 mA but is 25 percentage points low at 12 mA. What is it?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: a missing square root on a DP flow measurement. The error f − f² is zero at both ends and maximum at f = 0.5, where it is exactly 0.25 — 25 percentage points. It is the unmistakable signature, and it is why a loop check must include a mid-range point.
2. A 2-wire transmitter needs 12 V minimum. The supply measures 24.0 V under load. What is the largest total loop resistance you may fit?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: 600 Ω. Rmax = (24.0 − 12.0)/0.020 = 600 Ω, and that must cover the barrier, every burden resistor and the cable. With a 340 Ω barrier and 13 Ω of cable you have 247 Ω left — enough for exactly one 250 Ω input, and even then you are 3 Ω over. This is how ordinary loops end up marginal.
3. Why does earthing a screen at both ends sometimes make noise worse rather than better?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: it creates a conductive loop between two earth points that are not at the same potential. Any difference — 1.8 V at the refinery — drives a current around that loop (2.0 A through 0.9 Ω), whose magnetic field couples into the pair and whose voltage drop shares part of the signal's return path. Electrostatic screening only needs one connection; the second one buys nothing at 50 Hz and costs you a loop.
4. A transmitter is set for downscale (3.6 mA) failure. On which of these loops is that the dangerous choice?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: a furnace or dryer temperature that controls fuel. Downscale makes the controller believe the process is cold, so it calls for maximum fuel — exactly the wrong direction. On a tank level controlling a fill valve, downscale is also wrong (it believes the tank is empty and keeps filling). On a cooling-water low-flow trip, downscale is the safe choice. The rule: choose the direction that drives the process to its safe state if the value were believed — and always detect the fault band in code as well.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — THE VOLTAGE BUDGET THAT FAILED CORE

Question. FT-2104 is a 2-wire DP transmitter, minimum terminal voltage 12.0 V, on a loop with a 340 Ω zener barrier, a 250 Ω PLC input and 310 m (each way) of 1.0 mm² copper running at 60 °C. The 24 V supply measures 23.4 V under load. (a) Can the loop reach 20 mA? (b) A 250 Ω chart recorder is added in series. What is the new maximum indication? (c) Give three fixes with their consequences.

(a) cable resistance R₂₀ = ρ·2L/A = 0.0175 × (2 × 310) / 1.0 = 10.85 Ω R₆₀ = R₂₀ [1 + α(T-20)] = 10.85 [1 + 0.00393 × 40] = 10.85 × 1.1572 = 12.56 Ω available voltage for resistance = 23.4 - 12.0 = 11.4 V R_max = 11.4 / 0.020 = 570.0 Ω R_fitted = 340 + 250 + 12.56 = 602.56 Ω > 570 Ω -> NO I_max = 11.4 / 602.56 = 0.018919 A = 18.92 mA = (18.92-4)/16 = 93.2 % of span The loop was already 32.6 Ω over budget and clipped the top 6.8 % - unnoticed, because the process never ran above 90 %. (b) R_fitted = 602.56 + 250 = 852.56 Ω I_max = 11.4 / 852.56 = 0.013372 A = 13.37 mA = 58.6 % of span Above 58.6 % the indication is flat. No alarm, no discontinuity, no clue. (c) THREE FIXES 1 Signal splitter/isolator feeding the recorder from a second output. New loop R = 340 + 250 + 12.56 + ~0 = 602.6 Ω. Still 32.6 Ω over -> also drop the PLC burden to 100 Ω: R = 452.6 Ω, I_max = 25.2 mA, headroom 11.4 - 9.05 = 2.35 V. ✓ Cost ~LKR 28,000. Adds galvanic isolation. Chosen at the refinery. 2 Raise the supply to 30 V. Required V = 12.0 + 0.020 × 852.56 = 29.05 V, so 30 V works with 0.95 V spare. BUT on an IS loop the barrier's certified U₊ and the whole safety case are built on 24 V - this fix requires re-certification. Often illegal. 3 Remove the recorder. Free, instant, restores 18.92 mA. Does not fix the pre-existing 32.6 Ω overrun, so still clips at 93.2 %. Necessary but not sufficient. Discussion. Notice that the cable is only 2.1 % of the fitted resistance (12.56 / 602.56). Every instinct that says "long cable, therefore voltage drop" is looking at the smallest term. The barrier alone is 56 % of the budget. In a hazardous-area plant, the barrier is almost always the reason a loop is marginal, and the design error is not the cable - it is fitting two 250 Ω listeners behind it.
WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — SQUARE ROOT, TURNDOWN AND THE MASS BALANCE CORE

Question. An orifice-plate flow loop is ranged 0–40 m³/h at 0–2500 mmH₂O DP. The transmitter is configured linear in DP; the PLC applies no square root. The plant runs at a true 62 % of range for 624 h/month. (a) What does the PLC report? (b) What is the monthly apparent loss? (c) At what flow is the error worst, and how big is it? (d) If the transmitter is ±0.1 % of DP span, what is the turndown for 1 % flow accuracy?

WORKED EXAMPLE 4 — DYNAMICS, AND WHY THE TUNING WAS BLAMED CORE

Question. The element has τ₁ = 4 s. Correctly installed the well adds τ₂ = 8 s; with a dry 0.6 mm air gap it adds 92 s. (a) Find t63, t95 and t99 for both. (b) A 5 s PLC filter is added to “smooth” the faulty one — what happens? (c) What task period should the PID use in each case? (d) Explain the 6 → 22 minute settling time.

(a) Two first-order lags in series, unit step: y(t) = 1 - ( τ₁ e^(-t/τ₁) - τ₂ e^(-t/τ₂) ) / (τ₁ - τ₂) CORRECT τ = 4 s and 8 s t63 = 12.7 s t95 = 29.4 s t99 = 42.4 s FAULT D1 τ = 4 s and 92 s t63 = 96.1 s t95 = 279.7 s t99 = 427.7 s t95 ratio = 9.5× Note t63 ≈ τ₁ + τ₂ in both cases (12.7 vs 12, 96.1 vs 96) - a useful field estimate. But t63 alone hides the important change. Fit a first-order-plus-dead-time model using the two-point method (τ = 1.5(t63 - t28), θ = t63 - τ): single lag τ = 12 s t28 = 4.0 t63 = 12.0 -> τ = 12.0 s, θ = 0.0 s CORRECT 4 + 8 s t28 = 6.1 t63 = 12.7 -> τ = 9.9 s, θ = 2.8 s θ/τ = 0.28 FAULT D1 4 + 92 s t28 = 34.7 t63 = 96.1 -> τ = 92.0 s, θ = 4.0 s θ/τ = 0.04 A cascade always produces θ > 0 where a single lag produces none: y'(0) = 0, so the response starts flat, and a controller cannot distinguish that from real transport delay. The damage from D1 is not the dead time - it is that τ itself grew 9.3×, from 9.9 s to 92 s. (b) add τ₃ = 5 s: t28 = 39.8 s t63 = 101.2 s t95 = 284.8 s FOPDT fit: τ = 92.1 s (unchanged), θ = 9.1 s - the dead time has more than doubled from 4.0 s while the time constant did not improve at all. That is the worst possible trade for a controller: all of the cost, none of the benefit. The filter removes no error whatsoever, because the problem is not noise. A filter cannot recover information the sensor never had. (c) PID task period, T ≤ τ_total / 10: correct 12 / 10 = 1.2 s faulty 96 / 10 = 9.6 s Sampling adds an effective dead time of about T/2, so a 200 ms task on the correct installation adds 0.1 s to a 12 s process - negligible, and correct practice. On the faulty one the loop was massively oversampled, which is harmless and which made the trend look reassuringly smooth. (d) The measurement lag sits inside the control loop and contributes phase lag. When the effective time constant rose from 9.9 s to 92.0 s, the frequency at which the open loop reaches -180° fell by roughly the same factor, so the gain that was stable before now oscillates. The operator reduced the gain until the hunting stopped - the only visible remedy - and the loop became slow in both directions. Settling 6 min -> 22 min, 52 grade changes a month, 13.87 h of off-spec product, LKR 856,267. Discussion. The steady-state error from the air gap was 1.1 °C - well inside tolerance, and a five-point calibration would have passed the loop with a clean certificate. The entire LKR 856 k/month lived in the time domain, which almost no calibration procedure tests. If one number were added to plant practice, it should be a recorded t63 step response at commissioning, so degradation is visible later.
WORKED EXAMPLE 5 — RTD LEADS, AND WHY THE TRANSMITTER GOES IN THE HEAD CORE

Question. A Pt100 at 240 °C is to be read 310 m away. (a) Find R(240) and the sensitivity. (b) Error with a 2-wire connection in 0.5 mm² cable. (c) Error with 3-wire and a 0.35 Ω lead imbalance. (d) 4-wire? (e) What does a head transmitter cost you and save you?

(a) IEC 60751: R(t) = 100(1 + At + Bt²), A = 3.9083e-3, B = -5.775e-7 R(240) = 100(1 + 0.937992 - 0.033264) = 190.47 Ω sensitivity dR/dt = 100(A + 2Bt) = 100(3.9083e-3 - 2.772e-4) = 0.36311 Ω/°C (at 0 °C it is 0.39083 Ω/°C - platinum's sensitivity falls as it heats) (b) 2-WIRE lead resistance = 0.0175 × (2 × 310) / 0.5 = 21.70 Ω error = 21.70 / 0.36311 = +59.8 °C In 1.0 mm² it is still +29.9 °C. The instrument is not measuring temperature. (c) 3-WIRE the instrument measures (R_sensor + R_lead) and (R_lead) and subtracts, which is exact only if the two leads are equal. Residual = the imbalance: error = 0.35 / 0.36311 = 0.96 °C Sources of imbalance: different core lengths in a JB, one crimp poorer than another, one core spliced. A 0.35 Ω mismatch is roughly 10 m of 0.5 mm² core. (d) 4-WIRE excitation current forced down two leads; voltage measured on the other two, which carry no current, so I·R = 0 in them. Lead resistance cancels identically, for any values, matched or not. Residual error ≈ 0. This is a Kelvin four-terminal measurement - the same idea as measuring a shunt. (e) HEAD TRANSMITTER 4-wire connection over 100 mm inside the head, then 4-20 mA for the 310 m. Lead error ≈ 0, noise immunity of a current loop, HART diagnostics, and one pair instead of three or four cores. cost: ~LKR 22,000-45,000 per point, plus the head saves: 1-2 extra cores × 310 m, a special RTD input card, and the 59.8 °C Also worth knowing: excitation self-heating. A 1 mA excitation in 190.47 Ω dissipates 0.19 mW. In still air that is a few hundredths of a degree; in a dry, air-gapped well with no conduction path it is measurably more - one more reason the air gap in fault D1 is worse than it looks. Discussion. The head transmitter is not a luxury; over any distance beyond a few metres it is the only correct answer, and the comparison above is the argument to make when someone proposes saving LKR 30,000 per point by running RTD leads back to a "cheaper" card.

P2. A 4–20 mA loop reads 11.30 mA. The range is −50 to +250 °C. What temperature is that, and what should worry you about it?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: fraction = (11.30 − 4)/16 = 0.45625; EU = −50 + 300 × 0.45625 = 86.9 °C. What should worry you: nothing about the number itself — it is a perfectly ordinary, plausible mid-range value, and that is exactly the point. NE 43 cannot help here. A reading inside the valid band is only trustworthy if you also know the loop is healthy (voltage budget), the scaling is right (loop check) and the value is moving (stuck-signal check).
P3. Siemens S7 analogue input, 4–20 mA mode, range 0–600 kPa. The tag reads 20 736. What is the pressure, and what current is flowing?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: fraction = 20736/27648 = 0.75 exactly. Pressure = 0 + 600 × 0.75 = 450 kPa. Current = 4 + 16 × 0.75 = 16.00 mA. Sanity habit worth building: 27 648 = 210 × 27, and 3/4 of it is 20 736 — recognising the quarter points (6912, 13824, 20736) lets you check scaling arithmetic in your head at the panel.
P4. A loop supply is 24.0 V, the transmitter needs 10.5 V, and there is a 340 Ω barrier plus 480 m each way of 0.75 mm² cable at 55 °C. What is the largest burden resistor you may add?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: Rcable,20 = 0.0175 × 960 / 0.75 = 22.40 Ω; at 55 °C, ×[1 + 0.00393 × 35] = ×1.1376 = 25.48 Ω. Rmax = (24.0 − 10.5)/0.020 = 675.0 Ω. Available for burden = 675.0 − 340 − 25.48 = 309.5 Ω. So a single 250 Ω input fits with 59.5 Ω (1.19 V) of margin — and a second one does not. Note also that 250 Ω keeps you above the 230 Ω HART minimum, so do not be tempted to drop to 100 Ω if you want HART.
P5. A DP flow loop is correct at 4 and 20 mA but reads 70.7 % when it should read 50 %. What is wrong — and note it is not the same fault as D2.
Tap to reveal ↓ A: the square root is being taken twice. The transmitter roots it (so the mA is linear in flow) and the PLC roots it again. At a true 50 % flow the mA is at 50 %, and the PLC computes √0.50 = 0.7071 → 70.7 %. The signature is the mirror image of D2: over-reads in the middle, worst at the point where f − √f is greatest (f = 0.25, giving 25 percentage points high). Both faults are invisible at the endpoints, which is the recurring lesson.
P6. A thermocouple loop reads 22 °C low, consistently, at every temperature. The thermocouple, the transmitter and the card have all been bench-tested as good. Where is the fault?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: an uncompensated cold junction — almost certainly plain copper used for the last run into a hot panel. A thermocouple measures the difference between the hot junction and the point where its wire becomes copper. If that transition happens at a terminal rail sitting at 42 °C while the instrument's cold-junction compensation assumes its own terminals at 20 °C, every reading is low by the difference. The fix is correct compensating/extension cable all the way to the instrument's terminals. A constant offset at all temperatures is the diagnostic signature — a span error would grow with temperature.
P7. A level reading carries a clean 50 Hz sinusoid of 0.48 mA pk-pk. Range 0–8.0 m. How much apparent level is that, and what are the two candidate causes and their different fixes?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: 0.48/16 = 3.0 % of span = 8.0 × 0.03 = 0.240 m pk-pk. Two candidates: (1) inductive coupling from a parallel power cable — the fix is separation (≥ 300 mm) and confirming the pair is twisted; a screen does very little against magnetic coupling. (2) a ground loop from a screen earthed at both ends — the fix is a single earth at the panel, or a galvanic isolator. Distinguish them in one minute: put a clamp meter around the screen. Amperes in the screen means (2). Milliamps or nothing means (1).
P8. A PID loop on a process with τtotal = 45 s runs in a task with a 6 s period. Is that acceptable? What effective dead time does sampling add?
Tap to reveal ↓ A: the guideline is T ≤ τ/10 = 4.5 s, so 6 s is slightly too slow — it is within the T ≤ τ/4 = 11.25 s absolute limit, so the loop will function, but with measurably degraded performance and less phase margin. Sampling adds an effective dead time of about T/2 = 3 s, which is 6.7 % of τ — enough to matter on a tightly tuned loop. Move it to a 2–4 s task. Note the opposite error is also real: putting a 45 s process in a 10 ms task wastes CPU on every scan for no benefit (B03).
P9. Justify, with numbers, why a DP level transmitter calibrated on 20 °C oil reads low on the same tank at 240 °C. Take ρ20 = 918 and ρ240 = 792 kg/m³, true level 5.30 m.
Tap to reveal ↓ A: the transmitter measures Δp = ρgh and the scaling divides by the calibration density. Actual Δp = 792 × 9.81 × 5.30 = 41 179 Pa. Indicated h = Δp/(ρ20g) = 41 179/(918 × 9.81) = 4.572 m — a −13.7 % error, and nothing is faulty. Ratio check: 792/918 = 0.8627, and 5.30 × 0.8627 = 4.572 ✓. Fixes: temperature-compensate ρ in the PLC; or use radar, which is density-independent; or — if what you actually want is mass — keep the DP and stop converting to level, because Δp × A/g is the mass and the density cancels out entirely.
P10. Design a bad-quality handling scheme for a temperature loop that controls a dryer's burner. State the NE 43 failure direction you would configure, what the PLC does in each band, and one check that NE 43 cannot perform.
Tap to reveal ↓ A: configure UPSCALE (21.5 mA). On a burner, downscale would make the controller believe the dryer is cold and call for maximum fuel. Upscale makes it believe the dryer is too hot, and the fuel valve closes — the safe direction. PLC behaviour: <3.6 mA or >21.0 mA → set bad-quality, hold the last good PV, force the PID to MANUAL holding its last output (bumpless), raise a diagnostic alarm to maintenance and a process alarm only if the controller cannot hold; 3.6–3.8 and 20.5–21.0 → flag under/over-range, keep controlling, no operator alarm; 3.8–20.5 → normal. Test the raw value before scaling and clamping, or a clamp will hide the fault. The check NE 43 cannot perform: a frozen signal. Add a stuck-value test — if the PV has not moved by 0.1 % of span in 60 s while the plant is running, alarm it. And none of this substitutes for an independent hard-wired over-temperature trip, which is the actual safety function (A52).

DERIVATION 1 — THE LOOP VOLTAGE BUDGET STRETCH
A 2-wire transmitter is a series-connected regulated current sink. Kirchhoff's voltage law around the single loop, at the worst-case current I = 20 mA: V_supply = V_tx + I · ( R_barrier + ΣR_burden + R_cable ) The transmitter needs V_tx ≥ V_tx,min to stay in regulation. Therefore: V_supply ≥ V_tx,min + 0.020 × R_total R_total,max = ( V_supply - V_tx,min ) / 0.020 Cable resistance with temperature: R = ρ₂₀ · 2L / A · [ 1 + α(T - 20) ] ρ₂₀ = 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m, α = 0.00393 /°C for Cu Failure mode when the inequality is violated - this is the part that matters: the transmitter drops out of regulation and becomes a saturated series element. The current is then set by the circuit, not the measurement: I_clip = ( V_supply - V_tx,min ) / R_total and the indication rises normally, then flat-lines at I_clip. No discontinuity, no alarm, no NE 43 flag - because I_clip is inside the valid band.
DERIVATION 2 — WHY DP FLOW NEEDS A SQUARE ROOT, AND WHAT IT COSTS STRETCH
Incompressible, steady, along a streamline through a restriction of bore d in a pipe D, β = d/D: continuity A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ -> v₁ = β² v₂ Bernoulli p₁ + ½ρv₁² = p₂ + ½ρv₂² substitute: Δp = ½ρ( v₂² - β⁴v₂² ) = ½ρ v₂² (1 - β⁴) -> v₂ = √( 2Δp / (ρ(1-β⁴)) ) and Q = A₂v₂ with the discharge coefficient Cₔ (contraction and friction) and ε (expansibility, gases): Q = (Cₔ ε π d² / 4) · √( 2Δp / (ρ(1 - β⁴)) ) (ISO 5167) everything but Δp is design-constant, so Q = k√Δp THE TURNDOWN CONSEQUENCE, which is the real content: ln Q = ½ ln Δp + ln k differentiate: dQ/Q = ½ · dΔp/Δp A transmitter error e expressed as a fraction of DP span, at flow fraction f (so Δp/Δp_span = f²), gives a flow error as a fraction of reading: dQ/Q = e / (2 f²) For dQ/Q = 1 % with e = 0.1 % of span: f = √(0.001/0.02) = 0.2236 Turndown = 4.47 : 1. This is a property of the physics, not of the manufacturer, and no amount of money spent on the transmitter changes the f⁻² shape of the curve. Also note the noise consequence: d(√x)/dx = 1/(2√x), so rooting a signal amplifies its noise by 5× at 1 % of DP span and attenuates it by 0.5× at full span. This is why the root belongs in the PLC, where the raw DP is still visible.
DERIVATION 3 — SERIES-EMF REJECTION OF A CURRENT LOOP STRETCH
Model the transmitter as an ideal current source I₀ in parallel with its output impedance R_out. The loop contains R_loop (barrier + burdens + cable) and a series noise EMF e_n induced by a neighbouring conductor. Superposition: with I₀ suppressed (open), e_n drives a current through the series combination of R_out and R_loop: i_n = e_n / ( R_out + R_loop ) and the error voltage seen across a burden R_b is i_n R_b. As a fraction of the 16 mA span: error = e_n / ( 0.016 (R_out + R_loop) ) e_n = 100 mV, R_loop = 600 Ω: R_out = 2 MΩ (regulated, DC) error = 0.00031 % of span R_out = 600 Ω (passive/above bandwidth) error = 0.52 % of span voltage signal, high-Z input error = 100 mV / 10 V = 1.00 % of span Two conclusions worth remembering: 1 The rejection comes from R_out, i.e. from the transmitter's regulation - not from the wire being "current". A passive current divider gives you only the R_b/(R_out+R_loop) ratio, which is a factor of 2, not 3200. 2 Rejection degrades above the regulation bandwidth. VFD carrier hash at 4 kHz and its harmonics are outside it. That is the physics behind fault D4, and the reason the cure is mechanical (separation, screening, isolation) not electrical.
DERIVATION 4 — 3-WIRE RTD COMPENSATION AND ITS RESIDUAL STRETCH
Let the three leads have resistances R₁, R₂, R₃ and the element R_s. The instrument makes two measurements sharing the common return R₃: M₁ = R_s + R₁ + R₃ (through the element) M₂ = R₂ + R₃ (the compensating pair) M₁ - M₂ = R_s + (R₁ - R₂) If the leads are identical, R₁ = R₂ and the compensation is exact. Otherwise the residual is the imbalance ΔR = R₁ - R₂, converted to temperature by the local sensitivity: Δt = ΔR / (dR/dt) with, from IEC 60751, dR/dt = R₀(A + 2Bt) Pt100 at 240 °C: dR/dt = 0.36311 Ω/°C -> 0.35 Ω imbalance = 0.96 °C Pt100 at 0 °C: dR/dt = 0.39083 Ω/°C -> 0.35 Ω imbalance = 0.90 °C Note that Pt1000 has ten times the sensitivity (3.9083 Ω/°C at 0 °C) so the same lead imbalance produces one tenth of the error - which is the real reason Pt1000 is preferred for anything with long or awkward leads. 4-wire: current is forced through leads 1 and 4; the voltage is sensed on leads 2 and 3, which carry no current, so their I·R is identically zero. Cancellation is exact regardless of imbalance. This is a Kelvin four-terminal measurement.
DERIVATION 5 — CASCADED LAGS AND APPARENT DEAD TIME STRETCH
Two first-order lags in series, transfer function G(s) = 1 / ((1+τ₁s)(1+τ₂s)) Unit-step response by partial fractions: y(t) = 1 - ( τ₁ e^(-t/τ₁) - τ₂ e^(-t/τ₂) ) / (τ₁ - τ₂) (τ₁ ≠ τ₂) Key properties, all of which have field consequences: - y'(0) = 0. A single lag starts at full slope; a cascade starts flat. That initial flatness is the apparent dead time, and a controller cannot tell it from real transport delay. - t63 ≈ τ₁ + τ₂ to within a few per cent (12.7 vs 12; 96.1 vs 96). Useful in the field. - t95 is NOT longer than a single lag of the same Στ would give - it is slightly shorter, because the slowest pole is smaller than the sum. The penalty is θ, not t95, and that is what a controller feels. Do not confuse the two. - n identical lags of τ each: the response tends to a Gaussian-shaped delay of nτ with an effective dead time of roughly (n-1)τ/2 - the classic "lag chain looks like dead time" result. Four lags of 5 s behave much like 7.5 s of dead time plus a 12.5 s lag. Why a controller hates it (full treatment in A10 and A57): each lag contributes phase φ = -arctan(ωτ), so the frequency at which the open loop reaches -180° falls as lags are added, and the gain that was stable becomes unstable. Reducing the gain restores stability at the cost of speed - which is exactly what happened to Deodoriser 2's temperature loop.
TEN EXAM TRAPS CORE
  • 1
    “% of span” read as “% of reading”. The commonest error on any datasheet question. Compute the error in engineering units first, then divide by whatever the question asks about.
  • 2
    Forgetting that cable resistance is two conductors. 310 m of cable is 620 m of copper. Half the wrong answers in loop-budget questions are exactly a factor of two.
  • 3
    Using the supply's nameplate voltage. Use the voltage measured under load. A 24 V supply sagging to 22.8 V under a full rack costs you 60 Ω of budget.
  • 4
    Assuming the square root is somewhere. Find out where. Then write it on the tag. Both “neither” and “both” are real, common faults with mirror-image signatures.
  • 5
    Adding errors when the question wants RSS, or vice versa. State which method you used. An examiner marks the method, not just the number.
  • 6
    Quoting t63 when the question asks for settling. t63 is one τ. 95 % takes 3τ for a single lag and much longer for a cascade — and control performance depends on the cascade.
  • 7
    Claiming 4–20 mA is immune to noise. It is immune to series EMF within the transmitter's regulation bandwidth. It is not immune to ground loops, and not immune to VFD hash. Say the qualifier.
  • 8
    Confusing screen and armour. Armour is earthed at both ends for safety; a low-frequency signal screen is earthed at one. They are different conductors with different jobs.
  • 9
    Treating 3.8–4.0 mA as a fault. Under-range is a valid measurement below LRV. Only below 3.6 mA is it a fault. Getting the five NE 43 bands the wrong way round is a classic dropped mark.
  • 10
    Forgetting density on a hydrostatic level. If the question mentions temperature or two fluids, the density is the point of the question.

TEN EXERCISES, IN ORDER, ON REAL EQUIPMENT CORE
  • 1
    Measure a live loop without breaking it. Find any 4–20 mA input in a panel. Measure the DC volts across the burden resistor and divide by its value. Convert to % of span and compare with the HMI. Twenty minutes, no tools but a multimeter.
  • 2
    Build the voltage budget for that loop. Read the transmitter's datasheet minimum, measure the supply under load, count every resistance. Compute Rmax and compare with Rfitted. Write it on the drawing.
  • 3
    Make a loop simulator. A 24 V supply, a 10 kΩ potentiometer in series with a 1 kΩ resistor and a 250 Ω burden gives you an adjustable current source. Read it on a PLC AI and write the scaling code. This is the whole of pages 7–8 in hardware for under LKR 3,000.
  • 4
    Write a reusable scaling function block with LRV, URV, raw endpoints, NE 43 band detection, hold-last-good and a stuck-signal timer. Use it everywhere afterwards. This is the single most valuable artefact you will build this year.
  • 5
    Do a five-point as-found calibration on any transmitter you can legitimately take out of service, up and down, and plot the hysteresis. Write the certificate from page 15's list.
  • 6
    Run a real loop check. Inject 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 mA at the field terminals and read the HMI. Then do 3.5 and 21.5 mA and confirm the alarms. Get the permit; do it properly.
  • 7
    Do a step-response test. Plunge a spare RTD from ambient into a beaker of hot water and time t63 on a fast trend. Repeat with the element inside a dry thermowell. You will measure the D1 effect with your own hands.
  • 8
    Hunt a ground loop. Clamp a meter around the screen of an instrument cable at a field junction box. Anything above a few milliamps is fault D4 waiting to happen. Photograph what you find.
  • 9
    Borrow a HART communicator and read a device: tag, range, damping, failure direction, days since calibration, and any active diagnostics. Then use the loop-test command to output 12.000 mA and check the HMI. You will find something misconfigured on the first day.
  • 10
    Write your plant's loop-check procedure as a one-page form with an as-found/as-left table. Two hours of work; it becomes the document everyone uses.
