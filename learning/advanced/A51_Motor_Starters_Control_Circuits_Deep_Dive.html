The Motor Starting Problem

An induction motor at standstill is nearly a short circuit — every starter ever invented is a different answer to that one fact.

Definition

A motor starter is the combination of switching and protection devices that safely connects a motor to the supply, carries its running current, and disconnects it on command or on fault. Every starter must do four jobs: switch the motor on and off, carry its current continuously, protect it from overload, and let a small control signal command large power.

Why is this hard? At standstill the rotor has no back-EMF; the motor draws its locked-rotor current — typically 6–8× full-load current (FLC) — until it accelerates (see A30 for the slip physics). A 30 kW motor with FLC ≈ 55 A briefly demands ~400 A. That inrush stresses cables, dips the supply voltage for every neighbour, hammers the mechanical drivetrain with 1.5–2.5× rated torque, and heats the rotor. Starters manage how that first second happens.

The family tree

Three philosophies: hit it with everything (DOL), feed it less voltage (reduced-voltage), or feed it a different frequency (VFD).

Why it matters
  • 1Voltage dip. Inrush through the supply impedance dips the busbar voltage; contactors elsewhere can drop out below ~85% Un, and lighting flickers. Utilities and IEC 60034-12 limit starting current for large machines.
  • 2Thermal budget. Rotor heating during a start ∝ I²t is enormous — motors are rated for a limited number of starts per hour. Two hot starts back-to-back can cook a rotor cage.
  • 3Mechanical shock. DOL torque steps from zero to ~2× rated instantly — couplings, belts and gearboxes (A20) feel every start. Soft methods multiply drivetrain life.
  • 4The trade-off law. Any method that reduces starting current by feeding less voltage also reduces starting torque as V² — you cannot have low current and high torque from a fixed frequency. Only the VFD escapes (A08).
🧠 Quick check

A 22 kW motor (FLC 41 A) starts DOL with a locked-rotor multiple of 7.5. What current does the cable see at the moment of closing, and why doesn't the overload relay trip?

Tap to reveal ↓ Inrush ≈ 7.5 × 41 ≈ 308 A (plus a brief asymmetric DC-offset peak that can approach 2× that on the worst phase). The overload relay doesn't trip because it is a thermal device deliberately made slow: its trip class (Page 4) guarantees it rides through a normal start (e.g. Class 10 allows ~10 s at 7.2× Ie). Short-circuit protection is a separate, fast device — the division of labour that Page 11 formalises as coordination.

The Contactor — an Electromagnet with a Job

A relay grown up: a coil that costs watts commands contacts that carry kilowatts.

Anatomy & principle

A contactor is an electromagnetically operated switch designed for high make/break duty. Energise the coil (terminals A1–A2) → the magnetic circuit closes the armature → three main contacts (1-2, 3-4, 5-6) close together, connecting L1L2L3 to T1T2T3. De-energise → springs open them. The genius is the separation of circuits: a 3 W coil at 24 V DC switches a 55 kW motor at 400 V.

Coil behaviour matters: the open magnetic circuit draws an inrush ~6–10× the holding VA (AC coils) until the armature seats — size control transformers for it (A46). Below ~75–85% Un the magnet can't hold: the contactor drops out. That's a feature — it is your free undervoltage protection, and why 3-wire control (Page 3) doesn't restart after a power dip.

Control commands power — the split

Two worlds, one magnetic bridge. The control side decides; the power side obeys. All three main poles move on one carrier — they make and break together.

Utilization categories — the rating that actually matters

A contactor's headline amp rating is meaningless without its utilization category (IEC 60947-4-1), which encodes how hard making and breaking is:

DOL & the Seal-In — Industry's First Memory Bit

Direct-On-Line: the simplest starter, and the control-circuit idiom every automation engineer must be able to draw asleep.

The circuit

DOL applies full line voltage in one shot: MCCB/fuses → contactor K1 → overload relay F1 → motor. The magic is in the control circuit: pressing START energises K1, and K1's own auxiliary contact 13-14 closes in parallel with the START button — so the coil keeps itself alive when the button is released. That parallel contact is the seal-in (latch): a 1-bit memory made of copper, the ancestor of every PLC latch rung (A07).

Coil path: L → STOP(NC) → [ START(NO) ∥ K1:13-14 ] → F1:95-96(NC) → K1 coil → N START pressed → K1 pulls in → 13-14 closes → release START, K1 holds STOP or OL trip or voltage dip → coil path broken → K1 drops → 13-14 opens → stays off
Drawn as wired

The whole idiom in one frame: STOP and the OL contact in series (any of them kills it), START and the seal-in in parallel (either of them feeds it — but only K1 can keep feeding it).

2-wire vs 3-wire control — a safety decision

3-wire (momentary)

START/STOP pushbuttons + seal-in. After a supply dip the seal-in has opened → the machine stays off until a human presses START again. Required wherever unexpected restart endangers people (IEC 60204-1 §7.5).

2-wire (maintained)

A selector switch, float switch or thermostat simply holds the coil circuit closed. Power returns → motor restarts by itself. Correct for remote pumps and fans; dangerous for anything with an operator near moving parts.

This distinction — undervoltage release as restart protection — is the single most safety-relevant idea in basic motor control. A dropped contactor is not a bug to be "fixed" with an auto-restart relay unless the risk assessment (A35) says so.

🧠 Quick check

During a brownout the plant lights blink and every DOL machine stops. An apprentice proposes wiring the K1 seal-in contact before the STOP button "so it's neater". Why is the standard order — STOP first, then START∥seal-in — not negotiable?

Tap to reveal ↓ Functionally the series circuit is the same in steady state — but the rule exists for fault thinking: every series break device (STOP, OL 95-96, E-stop chain, door switches) must sit between the supply and the whole latch, so that any one of them opening guarantees the coil is dead regardless of which parallel path is closed. Bury a stop element downstream of only one branch and a welded seal-in or wiring error can hold the coil through a "stop". Same principle as A35's rule: stop functions live in series, start functions in parallel — and a wire-to-wire short must never bypass a stop (that is why safer circuits also earth-reference the control transformer so a ground fault blows the fuse instead of energising the coil).

Overload Relays & Trip Classes

The overload relay is a thermal model of the motor — it trips when the model overheats, hopefully just before the motor does.

Principle

An overload relay protects the motor against sustained overcurrent — jammed loads, lost phases, low voltage, too-frequent starts. The classic bimetal type passes motor current through small heaters that bend bimetal strips; sustained overcurrent bends them far enough to trip contact 95-96, breaking the contactor coil circuit. It stores a thermal image ∝ ∫I²dt — exactly the quantity that heats copper.

Electronic overloads compute the same I²t model from CTs — adding phase-loss and unbalance detection (a lost phase raises the remaining line currents ~√3× and cooks one winding fast), adjustable classes, and communication. Set the dial to the motor's nameplate FLC — not the cable rating, not "a bit higher so it stops nuisance-tripping". The OL guards the motor's thermal life; the mysterious nuisance trip is information (Page 12).

Trip classes — matching the start

IEC 60947-4-1 defines trip class as the maximum trip time at 7.2× set current from cold: the class number ≈ seconds. The relay must ride through the start yet still protect:

Inverse-time curves: more current → faster trip. Class 10 for normal machines, 20 for heavy starts, 30 for very high inertia (big fans, centrifuges). Class 10A trips even faster (2–10 s).

🧠 Quick check

A conveyor motor trips its Class 10 overload every Monday morning cold start (~12 s acceleration, fully loaded belt). Production asks you to "just wind the dial up 20%". What's the correct fix and why is the dial wrong?

Tap to reveal ↓ The trip is a class mismatch, not a set-current problem: a 12 s heavy start exceeds Class 10's ~10 s allowance at start current. Winding the dial above FLC would make the relay blind to genuine running overloads for the rest of the week — it lies to the thermal model. Correct: keep the dial at FLC and fit a Class 20 relay (or an electronic OL set to class 20), or reduce the start severity (unload the belt, star-delta/soft start if torque allows). Match the class to the start; match the dial to the motor.

Reversing Starters & Interlocking

Swap any two phases and the rotating field reverses (A30). The engineering is in making sure both contactors can never close at once.

The circuit

A reversing starter uses two contactors on one motor: K1 connects L1-L2-L3 → T1-T2-T3 (forward); K2 connects with two phases swapped (reverse). If both ever close together, L1 and L3 meet through a few centimetres of busbar: a bolted phase-to-phase short circuit. Interlocking is therefore not optional politeness — it is the design.

Three layers of interlock (use all three): 1 Electrical: K2's NC aux (21-22) in series with K1's coil, and vice-versa 2 Mechanical: pivoted lever between the two contactors — physically cannot both close 3 Button: FWD pushbutton's NC element breaks the REV coil path (and vice-versa)

Layer 1 fails if an aux contact welds; layer 2 covers that; layer 3 adds break-before-make at the human interface so pressing both buttons does nothing rather than something exciting. Direction change should pass through STOP (or a timer) — plugging (reversing at full speed) draws near locked-rotor current at ~2× slip frequency and is AC-4 duty that shreds contacts and shafts unless the machine is designed for it.

Interlocks, drawn

Cross-wired NC auxiliaries + the mechanical lever: electrically forbidden AND physically impossible. (Seal-ins and STOP omitted for clarity — they wrap this exactly as in Page 3.)

Where you meet it

Hoists & cranes

Up/down reversing with brakes and limit switches in the coil paths — limits are series stop elements.

Machine axes

Traverse motions with end-of-travel NC limit switches wired to break that direction only — the other direction still works to back off.

Gates & valves

Open/close actuators; torque and position switches decide the stop. Same idiom, smaller frame.

🧠 Quick check

A reversing starter has electrical interlocks only. K1's NC aux (used in K2's coil path) welds shut after years of jogging. Trace the failure: what single further event now causes a phase-to-phase short?

Tap to reveal ↓ The welded NC contact means K2's coil path is permanently enabled regardless of K1's state — the interlock is gone but nothing looks wrong. The next time an operator presses REV while FWD is running (or a PLC output glitches), K2 closes on top of K1 → L-L short through the swapped phases → busbar arc limited only by the upstream MCCB. This is why the mechanical interlock exists (it doesn't care about welded contacts), why mirror-contact feedback into a monitoring circuit matters, and why frequent-jog applications must be rated AC-4 so contacts don't weld in the first place.

Star-Delta — Starting at One Third

Reconnect the windings so each sees 58% voltage, and both line current and torque fall to exactly one third. Geometry does the work.

The idea & the arithmetic

A motor wound for Δ 400 V has each winding designed for 400 V. Connect the same windings in star and each sees only 400/√3 ≈ 230 V. Start in star (gentle), then — once near speed — reconnect to delta (full performance). The result is the famous ⅓ rule:

Delta (full voltage): Iphase,Δ = U/Z Iline,Δ = √3·U/Z Star (start): each winding sees U/√3 Iphase,Y = (U/√3)/Z = Iphase,Δ/√3 Iline,Y = Iphase,Y (star: line = phase) Ratio: Iline,Y / Iline,Δ = (U/√3Z)/(√3U/Z) = 1/3 Torque: T ∝ V² → (1/√3)² = 1/3 — current ÷3, torque ÷3, always together

Six motor leads (U1V1W1, U2V2W2) come to the panel. Three contactors do the work: K1 main (line to U1V1W1), K3 star (shorts U2V2W2 together = star point), K2 delta (connects U2V2W2 to the lines = delta). A timer (or current sensor) fires the transition at 75–85% speed.

Sequence & the transition problem

Open transition: cheap but the motor is briefly disconnected — its back-EMF drifts out of phase, and re-closing into delta produces a torque/current spike. Closed transition (Korndorfer-style resistors bridging the gap) buys smoothness for money.

Sizing bonus & the six-wire detail
  • 1Contactors K1/K2 carry phase current = 0.58 × line current — so both are sized at ~58% FLC. K3 (star) carries only ~33% FLC and is smaller again. This is why star-delta panels are cheaper than their three contactors suggest.
  • 2OL relay position matters: in the phase loop (after K1, in series with windings) set it to 0.58 × FLC; in the line set it to FLC. Mixing these up by √3 is the classic commissioning error.
  • 3The motor must be Δ-rated for the line voltage — nameplate "400/690 V Δ/Y" on a 400 V network: yes. "230/400 Δ/Y": no — star IS its 400 V connection; there is no lower-voltage start available.
  • 4K3 and K2 must be interlocked both electrically and mechanically — closing the star bridge onto a live delta is a three-phase short through the star contactor.
🧠 Quick check

A 400 V star-delta starter accelerates a fan to 60% speed in star, then the 8 s timer fires the transition — and the lights dip almost as badly as a DOL start. The fitter doubles the star time to 16 s but nothing improves. What's actually wrong?

Tap to reveal ↓ Star torque (⅓) is only just above the fan's torque curve, so the motor plateaus — it reaches its star-connection equilibrium speed (here ~60%) and simply sits there; more time changes nothing. At transition the slip is still huge, so delta re-connection draws close to locked-rotor current — the DOL-sized dip. Fixes: check the plateau (star current stops falling = plateau reached), and if the load can't be lightened (close the fan damper for the start!), move to a soft starter or VFD. Star-delta only works when ⅓ torque can genuinely reach ≥80% speed.

Autotransformer, Reactor & the Reduced-Voltage Menu

Between star-delta and the soft starter lies a century of clever compromises — and one beautiful square law.

Autotransformer starting — the x² machine

An autotransformer starter feeds the motor from taps (typically 50 / 65 / 80%). At tap fraction x, the motor sees x·U and draws x·ILR — but the transformer steps line current down again by x. So the supply sees:

Motor current = x · ILR Line current = x² · ILR Torque = x² · TLR 65% tap → line current and torque both = 0.42× DOL — same ratio as torque: the autotransformer is "fair"

Compare star-delta: fixed at 0.33, one step, needs six motor leads. The autotransformer gives choosable taps, works on any 3-lead motor, and delivers more torque per line-amp than any other reduced-voltage method (torque/line-current ratio = 1, like DOL). The Korndorfer connection makes the transition closed (star point opens first, part of the winding briefly acts as a series reactor, then full voltage) — no open-circuit spike. Price: a big, start-duty-rated transformer that must cool between attempts. Common on large pumps/fans and MV machines.

Primary resistance / reactor starting

Series resistors or reactors drop voltage across themselves: motor voltage rises as the motor accelerates and current falls (a natural "ramp"). Line current falls only as x (not x²) and torque as x², so the torque-per-amp is poor — but the circuit is simple and smooth, and reactor starting is still used at medium voltage. Resistor banks additionally waste the dropped energy as heat and need duty-cycle ratings. Historically important; today mostly displaced by soft starters, which are the same idea executed with silicon and feedback.

Why the square law rules everything

All fixed-frequency reduced-voltage methods obey T ∝ V², because both stator flux and rotor current scale with V and torque is their product. The methods differ only in what the supply pays: star-delta and the autotransformer make the line current fall as fast as the torque (both re-arrange the same windings/turns), while series impedance makes the line pay x for a torque of x². That single insight — compare torque fraction to line-current fraction — ranks every starter on Page 7's table, and explains why the VFD (which holds V/f, hence flux, constant — A08) is the only device that breaks the law.

A 90 kW pump motor (LRC 6.5×, LRT 1.6× rated) must start with line current ≤ 2.8× FLC, and needs at least 0.55× rated torque to break the pump away. Star-delta or autotransformer — show the arithmetic.

Tap to reveal ↓ Star-delta: line current 6.5/3 = 2.17× ✓ but torque 1.6/3 = 0.53× — just below the 0.55 requirement: marginal → fails the brief. Soft Starters — Phase Control with Feedback

Six thyristors, a microcontroller, and the old V² law — but now the voltage is a continuous, closed-loop ramp.

Principle

A soft starter puts two anti-parallel SCRs (thyristors) in each phase and delays their firing angle each half-cycle. More delay → less RMS voltage → less current and torque (still ∝ V²). As the motor accelerates, the controller advances the firing angle along a voltage ramp or — better — holds a current limit (typically 2.5–4× FLC) until the motor reaches speed. At full speed a bypass contactor shorts the SCRs out, eliminating their ~1 W/A conduction loss and heat.

Firing delay α ↑ → VRMS ↓ → I = V/Z ↓ , T ∝ V² ↓↓ Ramp: V from ~30% → 100% over tramp (2–30 s, set) Current limit: hold I ≤ Iset, let V find itself Kickstart: brief 80–100% pulse (~0.5 s) to break static friction, then drop to ramp

Chopped sinusoids are rich in harmonics during the ramp — brief and usually tolerable (unlike a VFD, there is no continuous distortion once bypassed). Notice what a soft starter is not: it cannot exceed line frequency, cannot deliver full torque at zero speed, and gives no speed control — it is a starting (and stopping) device only.

The waveform, drawn

Phase-angle control: the dashed sine is the supply; the filled slices are what the motor receives. RMS voltage is the control variable.

Features that earn their keep

Soft stop

Ramp the voltage down — the cure for water hammer and check-valve slam in pump systems. Often the real reason a soft starter is bought.

Current-limit start

Feedback holds line current at the set ceiling — the utility-friendly mode, and self-adjusting as load varies day to day.

Kickstart / breakaway

A short full-torque pulse defeats stiction on crushers and loaded screws, then the ramp takes over.

Protection suite

Electronic OL (selectable class), phase loss/reversal, underload, SCR overtemperature, starts-per-hour lockout — one box replaces several relays.

Inside-delta connection

Wire the starter inside the delta loop (6 wires): it switches phase current (58%) → a smaller frame for big motors.

Limits

No speed control, no zero-speed torque, needs cooling before re-start, and SCRs fail short — the line contactor upstream remains the isolation device.

🧠 Quick check

A soft starter on a loaded screw conveyor is set to voltage-ramp mode, 30% initial voltage, 20 s ramp. The motor hums for 6 seconds without turning, then the OL model trips. Explain, and name the two settings that fix it.

Tap to reveal ↓ At 30% voltage, torque = 0.3² = 9% of locked-rotor torque — nowhere near the screw's breakaway friction. The motor sits at zero speed drawing (0.3 × LRC) ≈ 2× FLC, pure rotor heating, until the thermal model quits. Fixes: (1) kickstart — a 0.5 s pulse at ~90% voltage to break stiction; (2) raise the initial voltage (or switch to current-limit mode with a higher ceiling) so starting torque exceeds load torque from the first half-cycle. General rule: torque must beat the load at every speed from 0 to full, or the start plateaus and cooks (same physics as the star-delta fan on Page 6).

The VFD as a Starter — and Choosing Between Them All

The drive doesn't reduce the voltage trick — it changes the question, feeding the motor a frequency where full torque needs only rated current.

Why the VFD escapes the V² law

Every fixed-frequency starter fights 50 Hz slip physics. A VFD (A08) instead starts the motor at a few Hz, keeping V/f — and therefore flux — constant. At 2 Hz the rotor sees a small slip frequency, so rated torque appears at roughly rated current, not 7× it. The drive then ramps frequency up; the motor is never more than a few % slip from synchronism. Starting stops being an event and becomes a trajectory.

DOL @50 Hz: torque 1.8× costs current 7× torque per amp ≈ 0.26 VFD @ low f: torque 1.5–2× costs current 1.5–2× torque per amp ≈ 1 — the best possible

Bonus: controlled deceleration (with braking resistor if needed), no transition events, starts-per-hour essentially unlimited (thermal, not mechanical, limits), plus permanent speed control and energy savings on variable-torque loads (fan laws — A47). Cost: price, continuous harmonics (mitigation in A08/A46), bearing-current and cable dV/dt care, and drive-side engineering.

🧠 Quick check

Same 30 kW fan, four quotes: DOL, star-delta, soft starter, VFD. The plant runs the fan at constant full speed 24/7 and the supply tolerates 3× inrush. Which quote wins on engineering (not habit), and when would the answer flip to VFD?

Tap to reveal ↓ Constant full speed removes the VFD's energy argument (and its continuous harmonics/losses ~2–3% become pure cost). A fan's torque ∝ n² start is easy — star-delta's ⅓ torque suffices, and 2.2× line current meets the 3× limit; the soft starter also qualifies and adds settable limits with no transition spike, no six-lead cable, and soft stop — typically the engineering winner at this size unless budget forces star-delta. The answer flips to VFD the moment flow modulation appears (damper control wastes the fan laws: 80% flow ≈ 51% power — A47), or the supply limit tightens below ~2×, or starts become very frequent.

Control-Circuit Craft — Jog, Sequence, Interlock, E-Stop

The power circuit is a catalogue choice; the control circuit is where your engineering shows.

Control supply choices

24 V DC (modern default)

PELV-safe for people, PLC-native (A07), safety-device friendly (A35). Needs a PSU; watch voltage drop on long button runs (A46's 2L rule). DC coils = quiet, no inrush hum, but need DC-13-rated switching.

230/110 V AC (via control transformer)

Drives big coils directly, tolerates long cable runs, no PSU to fail. 110 V "safety" tradition from machine-tool practice (IEC 60204-1 recommends control transformers, earthed one side, fused).

Either way the rule from A46 holds: one earthed control common, fused at the source, so an earth fault blows a fuse rather than silently energising a coil around its stop contact.

Pilot devices — the human interface grammar
  • 1Colours (IEC 60073): STOP = red, START = green (or black/white where red is reserved for emergency only), RESET = blue, abnormal = yellow. E-stop = red mushroom on yellow background, latching, twist/pull to release.
  • 2Contacts: stop functions use NC contacts (broken wire = stop — fail safe); start functions use NO (broken wire = can't start — fail safe again). This single convention is why control circuits survive their own faults.
  • 3Indicators: RUN lamp fed from a K1 aux (not from the button), FAULT from OL 97-98, plus lamp-test provision on anything an operator trusts.
  • 4Terminal discipline: every device numbered per the schematic (A46's wire-numbering craft) — a control circuit you can't trace at 3 a.m. is a control circuit that doesn't work at 3 a.m.
🧠 Quick check

A machine has local START/STOP and a PLC "remote run" output wired in parallel with the START button. Night shift reports the machine occasionally "starts itself" after a STOP. Diagnose the design error.

Tap to reveal ↓ The PLC output is a maintained (2-wire) signal in parallel with a momentary START: if the PLC output is still TRUE when the operator presses STOP, the coil re-energises the instant STOP is released — a self-start. Correct patterns: (1) the PLC output must be a pulse (one-shot) if used in parallel with a momentary button, or (2) route control through a proper local/remote selector so exactly one master exists at a time, and (3) feed the contactor status (13-14) back to a PLC input so its logic drops the run command on any stop. Hard-wired STOP must break the whole latch either way (Page 3's series rule).

Coordination & Sizing the Starter Branch

Three devices, three jobs, one tested team: the SCPD breaks faults, the contactor switches, the overload remembers.

Division of labour

A motor branch (A46) is SCPD → contactor → overload → motor. The SCPD (fuses or MCCB) handles the currents that would destroy — short circuits and earth faults. The contactor makes and breaks operational currents. The overload integrates the thermal story. No single device can do all three jobs well at motor scale; coordination is the contract between them.

Type 1 coordination (IEC 60947-4-1): after a short circuit the starter must harm no one — but contactor/OL may need replacement. Cheap, downtime. Type 2: after the same fault the starter must be fit for further use (light contact welding separable by a screwdriver allowed). Production-friendly. Type 2 is proven ONLY by the manufacturer's TESTED combination tables — never by arithmetic.

This is why panel builders pick fuse + contactor + OL as a published trio. An untested "equivalent" MCCB may let through enough I²t to weld the contactor solid — the fault is cleared, the machine is dead, and Type 2 was a fiction. Let-through energy, not rated amps, is the currency (A44's adiabatic idea again).

Sizing walk — 15 kW, 400 V, DOL
FLC = P / (√3·U·cosφ·η) = 15 000 / (1.732 × 400 × 0.85 × 0.90) ≈ 28.3 A Contactor: AC-3 ≥ 15 kW/400 V → 32 A AC-3 frame Overload: set 28 A (nameplate), Class 10 (normal start) SCPD: aM fuse 40–50 A or motor-rated MCCB from the tested Type 2 table Cable: ≥ 1.45 rule & derating per A44/A46 — and it must survive the SCPD's let-through

Two classic traps: sizing the SCPD to the cable instead of from the coordination table (it then trips on inrush, or worse, is oversized and never protects the contactor), and forgetting that star-delta panels size K1/K2 at 0.58 × FLC — their SCPD and OL positions shift accordingly (Page 6).

🧠 Quick check

After a cable fault, a "Type 2 coordinated" starter is found with its contactor welded shut and its OL destroyed. The panel schedule shows the specified fuse was swapped for a same-amp-rating MCB during maintenance. Why did same amps ≠ same protection?

Tap to reveal ↓ Type 2 was tested with the fuse's let-through characteristics — an aM fuse clips a big fault in a few ms with modest I²t and cut-off current. A same-rated MCB has a completely different let-through: at high prospective current its clearing I²t and peak can be several times the fuse's, exceeding what the contactor contacts and OL heaters were proven to survive. Coordination certifies a combination, not a current rating: change any member and the certificate is void. (Same logic as A46's tested-combination-only rule.)

Recap, Failure Modes & Final Quiz

Eight ideas, six ways it breaks in real plants, five questions.

The eight ideas
  • 1Standstill ≈ short circuit: 6–8× FLC until the rotor builds back-EMF. Every starter manages that first second.
  • 2The contactor separates worlds: a 3 W coil (A1-A2) commands kilowatts; ratings live in utilization categories, not the cover sticker.
  • 3Seal-in = memory: START ∥ K1:13-14, with every stop element in series — the 1-bit ancestor of PLC logic, and free restart protection via drop-out.
  • 4Overload = thermal model: dial at FLC, class matched to the start (10/20/30). It infers temperature; thermistors measure it.
  • 5Reversing = 2 phases swapped + 3 interlock layers: electrical, mechanical, button — because one welded contact is one event from a bolted short.
  • 6V² is the law: star-delta ÷3 both; autotransformer x² both; series impedance pays x for x² torque. Only frequency change (VFD) escapes.
  • 7Torque must beat load at every speed or the start plateaus and cooks — the star-delta fan and soft-started screw both fail this way.
  • 8Coordination is a tested trio: SCPD + contactor + OL prove Type 2 together; swap any member and the certificate is void.
How starters fail in the field
⚡ Chattering contactor

Low control voltage, loose control wiring, or an undersized control transformer sagging under coil inrush — the armature buzzes in and out, machine-gunning the contacts until they weld or the coil burns. Measure coil voltage during pull-in, not at rest.

🔥 Welded main contacts

Jogging on an AC-3 frame, or a fault beyond the tested combination. Motor won't stop from the button — only the upstream device. Mirror contacts + EDM (A35) exist to catch exactly this before the next cycle.

🕳 Single-phasing

One blown fuse or corroded terminal: the motor runs on, growling, with ~√3× current in the remaining lines. Bimetal differential levers and electronic unbalance detection trip it; without them the winding cooks in minutes.

🎛 The wound-up dial

OL set to 120% FLC "to stop nuisance trips" — the thermal model now lies, and next month's genuine jam takes the winding with it. Nuisance trips are data: wrong class, real overload, or unbalance.

🌟 Star-delta transition arc

Open transition into a drifted back-EMF, or K3/K2 interlock failure: the spike erodes contacts each start until one day the star bridge closes onto live delta. Check transition timing and interlocks at commissioning, not after.

🧲 Coil burnout

Wrong coil voltage (a 110 V coil on 230 V dies fast; 230 V coil on 110 V hums, draws inrush forever, dies slow), or an AC coil mechanically blocked from seating — inrush becomes continuous. Coils are consumables only when the application is wrong.

🧠 Final quiz 1

Why does a contactor dropping out on a voltage dip count as a safety feature, and which control scheme throws that feature away?

Tap to reveal ↓ Drop-out opens the seal-in, so when power returns the machine stays off until a deliberate re-start — undervoltage protection and restart protection in one (IEC 60204-1 requirement where restart is hazardous). 2-wire (maintained) control discards it: the held contact re-energises the coil the instant supply returns — acceptable for a remote pump, dangerous beside an operator.
🧠 Final quiz 2

Star-delta reduces current to ⅓ — why exactly ⅓ and not 1/√3?

Tap to reveal ↓ Two √3 factors stack. In star each winding sees U/√3 → winding current falls by √3. And in star the line current equals the winding current, whereas in delta the line is √3× the winding current. So the line-to-line comparison picks up both factors: (1/√3) × (1/√3) = 1/3. Torque follows voltage squared: (1/√3)² = 1/3 as well.
🧠 Final quiz 3

An OL relay's 95-96 contact and its 97-98 contact — where does each go and why?

Tap to reveal ↓ 95-96 (NC) goes in series with the contactor coil path — it opens on trip and must sit where no parallel branch can bypass it. 97-98 (NO) closes on trip and feeds the fault indication — lamp, PLC input (A07), alarm. Signal and trip are separated so the indication circuit can never hold the motor on.
🧠 Final quiz 4

A soft starter is set to current-limit 3.0×. The motor's LRC is 6.8× and LRT 1.9×. Estimate the available breakaway torque. Will a conveyor needing 0.5× rated torque start?

Tap to reveal ↓ Current limit 3.0× forces voltage down to V = 3.0/6.8 ≈ 0.44 pu → torque = 0.44² × 1.9 ≈ 0.37× rated. The conveyor needs 0.5× → it stalls at the limit. Options: raise the limit (~3.9× gives 0.44... recompute: V=3.9/6.8=0.57, T=0.57²×1.9=0.62× ✓), use kickstart, or unload the belt. The V² tax is merciless — always check torque at the chosen current limit.
🧠 Final quiz 5

Name the three interlock layers of a reversing starter and the failure each one covers.

Tap to reveal ↓ Electrical (cross-wired NC auxiliaries): covers normal operation — logic prevents both coils energising. Mechanical (pivoted lever): covers the electrical layer's failure — a welded aux or wiring error can't close both armatures. Button (NC element of each direction button breaks the other coil path): covers the human — pressing both gives break-before-make, nothing dramatic. Defence in depth, exactly as A35 prescribes.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems, solved in full.

WE 1 · Sizing a DOL branch

A 30 kW, 400 V pump motor (cosφ 0.85, η 0.90) starts DOL, normal 4 s start. Specify FLC, contactor, overload and SCPD.

FLC = P/(√3·U·cosφ·η) = 30 000/(1.732 × 400 × 0.85 × 0.90) = 30 000/530 ≈ 56.6 A Contactor: AC-3 rating ≥ 30 kW / 400 V → next standard frame, e.g. 65 A AC-3 Overload: dial = 56–57 A (nameplate FLC), Class 10 (4 s start ≪ 10 s) SCPD: aM fuse 63–80 A or motor-duty MCCB — from the manufacturer's Type 2 tested-combination table at the panel's fault level (A46: Icc)

Discussion: every number traces to a source — FLC from the nameplate physics, the contactor from the AC-3 kW table (never AC-1), the class from the measured start, the SCPD from a tested trio. The only "free" choice is Type 1 vs Type 2, and that's a downtime decision, not an electrical one.

WE 2 · Star-delta arithmetic, end to end

The same 30 kW motor (nameplate 400/690 V Δ/Y, LRC 7×, LRT 1.9×) is fitted with a star-delta starter for a lightly-loaded start (load torque 20% rated, roughly constant). Find the starting line current, starting torque margin, and the contactor/OL sizes.

DOL inrush would be 7 × 56.6 = 396 A Star start: line current = 396/3 ≈ 132 A (2.33 × FLC) Star torque: 1.9/3 = 0.63× rated vs load 0.20× → margin 3.2:1 ✓ accelerates Contactors: K1 (main) & K2 (delta) carry phase current = 0.58 × 56.6 ≈ 33 A → 40 A AC-3 K3 (star) carries ≈ 0.33 × 56.6 ≈ 19 A → 25 A AC-3 Overload: in the phase loop (after K1) → set 0.58 × FLC ≈ 33 A

Discussion: the 400/690 nameplate confirms the windings are Δ-rated for 400 V — star-delta is legal. The 0.58 factor appears twice (contactor size, OL setting); forgetting it in the OL position is the classic error that leaves a motor running 73% over-protected-against, i.e. unprotected.

WE 3 · Choosing a trip class from the curve law

An 11 kW ID fan (FLC 21 A) accelerates in 14 s drawing an average 6.5× FLC. Will Class 10 ride through? Which class is right? (Use the I²t scaling ttrip ≈ class × (7.2/x)².)

Class 10 at 6.5×: t ≈ 10 × (7.2/6.5)² = 10 × 1.23 = 12.3 s < 14 s → trips ✗ Class 20 at 6.5×: t ≈ 20 × 1.23 = 24.5 s > 14 s, margin 1.75 ✓ Class 30 at 6.5×: t ≈ 36.9 s — legal but blunts protection unnecessarily

Discussion: the (7.2/x)² scaling comes straight from the I²t thermal image — halve the current, quadruple the time. Class 20 with the dial at 21 A protects the motor and respects the start. Note the OL also enforces the motor's starts-per-hour budget: two consecutive 14 s hot starts may trip legitimately — that's the model working, not failing.

WE 4 · Autotransformer tap selection

A 75 kW mill motor: LRC 6.0×, LRT 1.7×. The utility caps starting current at 3.0× FLC; the load needs ≥ 0.5× rated torque to break away. Choose a tap (50 / 65 / 80%).

Tap x: line current = x²·6.0 torque = x²·1.7 80%: I = 0.64 × 6.0 = 3.84× ✗ exceeds 3.0 cap 65%: I = 0.42 × 6.0 = 2.54× ✓ T = 0.42 × 1.7 = 0.72× ✓ (margin 1.4) 50%: I = 0.25 × 6.0 = 1.50× ✓ T = 0.25 × 1.7 = 0.43× ✗ below 0.5 → stalls Answer: 65% tap. Korndorfer transition keeps it closed — no spike at changeover.

Discussion: notice the autotransformer's fairness — torque fraction equals line-current fraction (both x²). A star-delta here would give I = 2.0× ✓ but T = 0.57× — only a 1.13 margin over breakaway; workable but thin. The tap structure is exactly what star-delta lacks: a choice.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Work each before revealing — the reveal is the marking scheme.

Problem 1

Estimate FLC for 7.5 kW / 400 V (cosφ 0.83, η 0.88), and pick the contactor AC-3 frame.

Tap to reveal ↓FLC = 7500/(1.732×400×0.83×0.88) = 7500/506 ≈ 14.8 A. Contactor: 18 A AC-3 frame (7.5 kW class). Sanity check: ≈2 A/kW rule gives 15 A ✓.
Problem 2

Draw (mentally or on paper) the DOL coil path and mark which elements are in series and which in parallel. Where does a door safety switch go?

Tap to reveal ↓Series: STOP (NC) → OL 95-96 (NC) → coil. Parallel: START (NO) ∥ K1 13-14 (seal-in). The door switch is a stop function → series, upstream of the whole latch, using its NC (positively-opened) contact — via a safety relay if the risk assessment demands monitoring (A35).
Problem 3

A star-delta starter's OL is mounted in the line (before K1) and dialled to 0.58×FLC by habit. What happens?

Tap to reveal ↓In the line position the OL sees full line current = FLC, but it's set to 0.58×FLC → it trips at 58% of rated load: relentless nuisance trips around ~60% load. (The mirrored error — phase-loop OL dialled to full FLC — protects nothing until 173% overload.) Position and setting must be decided together: line → FLC; phase loop → 0.58×FLC.
Problem 4

Why does an AC contactor coil draw 6–10× its holding VA at the moment of energisation, and what does this do to control-transformer sizing?

Tap to reveal ↓With the armature open the magnetic circuit has a large air gap → low inductance → high current; as it seats, inductance rises and current falls to holding level. The control transformer must supply the largest simultaneous inrush (e.g. star-delta transition energises K2 while K1 holds) without sagging below ~85% coil voltage, or contactors chatter — hence A46's inrush-based VA sizing, not steady-state sums.
Problem 5

A motor nameplate reads "230/400 V Δ/Y". Can you star-delta start it on a 400 V network?

Tap to reveal ↓No. On 400 V this motor must run in star (each winding rated 230 V = 400/√3). "Starting in star" is its normal connection — there is no reduced step available, and connecting it in delta on 400 V puts 400 V across 230 V windings (1.73× flux → saturation → smoke). Star-delta needs "400/690 Δ/Y" on a 400 V network.
Problem 6

A soft starter set to 3.5× current limit starts a machine fine in summer but stalls it on cold mornings. Physics?

Tap to reveal ↓Cold gearbox oil multiplies breakaway and low-speed load torque (viscosity — A47's ν(T) sensitivity). Available torque at the limit is fixed (V = 3.5/LRC, T ∝ V²); the load curve rises above it → plateau-and-trip. Fixes: kickstart pulse, winter limit profile (many units have dual parameter sets), or address the oil (heater/synthetic). The starter isn't faulty — the load moved.
Problem 7

Rank DOL, star-delta, autotransformer (65%), reactor (65%) and soft start (3× limit) by starting torque per line-amp for the same motor.

Tap to reveal ↓Torque/line-current ratio: DOL = 1 (definition), autotransformer = 1 (both x²), star-delta = 1 (both ⅓) — these three are "fair". Reactor = x (0.65): pays x current for x² torque. Soft start ≈ V (=I/LRC ≈ 0.5 here): same physics as the reactor, continuously variable. So: DOL = auto = Y-Δ > reactor > soft-start — which is why autotransformers persist on torque-hungry weak-supply starts, and why soft starters need kickstart features.
Problem 8

Design intent: conveyor B may only run while conveyor A runs; both share one E-stop. Sketch the coil logic.

Tap to reveal ↓E-stop chain feeds the control supply to both circuits (series master). KA (conveyor A): standard 3-wire latch. KB: its coil path runs through KA's NO aux 13-14 in addition to its own STOP/START/seal-in — B can't start unless A is proven running, and A stopping drops B instantly. Add an off-delay on the KA aux if B must run on briefly to clear product. Start order: A then B; stop order: B then A (or all via E-stop).
Problem 9

What is a mirror contact (IEC 60947-4-1 Annex F), and why can't an ordinary NC auxiliary do its job in a safety circuit?

Tap to reveal ↓A mirror contact is an NC auxiliary with a guaranteed mechanical relationship to the main poles: it cannot be closed while any main contact is welded closed. An ordinary aux shares no such guarantee — its linkage can be closed while a welded main pole stays conducting, so a safety relay's feedback loop (EDM, A35/A46) would falsely report "contactor open". Mirror contacts make the feedback trustworthy; that's what the Annex F certification means.
Problem 10

A 5.5 kW machine jogs ~200 times per shift through a contactor sized "5.5 kW AC-3". Contacts last 6 weeks. Compute nothing — argue the fix.

Tap to reveal ↓Jogging breaks 6–8× current every cycle — that's AC-4 duty, where this frame's rating might be only ~40% of its AC-3 value and its electrical-life curve is perhaps 10× shorter per operation. Fixes in preference order: (1) move jogging to a VFD (silicon doesn't arc), (2) select a contactor from the AC-4 kW column with the electrical-life curve matched to 200 ops/shift × life target, (3) reduce jog frequency by fixturing. Replacing like-for-like every 6 weeks is a subscription, not maintenance.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations worth owning, the symbols, and the seven classic traps.

Derivation 1 · The star-delta ⅓, properly
Let each winding have impedance Z at standstill, supply line voltage U. Delta: winding voltage = U → Iw,Δ = U/Z line current IL,Δ = √3·Iw,Δ = √3·U/Z (vector sum at each node) Star: winding voltage = U/√3 → Iw,Y = U/(√3·Z) line current IL,Y = Iw,Y = U/(√3·Z) (line IS the winding) Ratio: IL,Y/IL,Δ = [U/(√3Z)] / [√3U/Z] = 1/3 Torque: air-gap torque ∝ Φ·Irotor, both ∝ V → T ∝ V² → TY/TΔ = (1/√3)² = 1/3 ∎

Exam habit: state both √3 factors explicitly (winding voltage ÷√3; line=winding in star vs line=√3×winding in delta). Most dropped marks hide in asserting "⅓" without separating them.

Derivation 2 · Why trip curves are inverse-square (the thermal image)
Adiabatic heating of the OL element (and motor copper): m·c·dθ/dt = I²·R For a trip at fixed temperature rise Δθtrip (ignoring cooling): Δθtrip = I²·R·t/(m·c) → I² · t = constant So ttrip(x) = ttrip(7.2) · (7.2/x)² — the scaling used in WE 3. Class definition: ttrip(7.2×Ie, from cold) ≤ class seconds (10/20/30), no trip within 2 h at 1.05×, trip within 2 h at 1.2× (IEC 60947-4-1).

Near 1.05–1.2× the adiabatic assumption fails (cooling matters — that's why those limits are 2-hour statements, not I²t points). Electronic relays add a cooling time-constant and thermal memory: a hot start begins with the integrator already charged — the "trips on second start" behaviour is the model being honest.

Derivation 3 · Autotransformer x² line current
Tap fraction x: motor voltage Vm = x·U → motor current Im = x·ILR Ideal transformer power balance (magnetising neglected): U·Iline = Vm·Im = (x·U)(x·ILR) → Iline = x²·ILR Torque T = x²·TLR (V² law) → T/Iline = TLR/ILR — same as DOL: "fair" Series reactor for contrast: Iline = Im = x·ILR (no transformer action) → T/I ratio = x.

Seven exam & field traps
  • 1AC-1 amps ≠ AC-3 amps. The big number on the contactor is the resistive rating; motors live in the AC-3 kW table, jogging in AC-4.
  • 2The 0.58 factor. Star-delta OLs and contactors in the phase loop see I/√3 — position and setting travel together.
  • 3Dial = FLC, class = start. Fix nuisance trips by changing class (or finding the real overload), never by winding the dial past nameplate.
  • 42-wire control restarts. Any maintained signal (float, thermostat, PLC output held TRUE) restarts after an outage — a risk-assessment decision, not a convenience.
  • 5Same amps ≠ same let-through. Swapping the tested SCPD for "an equivalent" voids Type 2 coordination — the certificate belongs to the trio.
  • 6T ∝ V² has no exceptions at fixed frequency. Any current reduction you buy with voltage costs torque squared; check the load curve before choosing the starter.
  • 7"230/400 Δ/Y" cannot star-delta on 400 V. Check the nameplate delta voltage equals the line voltage — the six terminals alone prove nothing.
