Why Bearings Exist — The Friction Problem

Precise Definition

A bearing is a machine element that constrains relative motion to a desired type (usually rotation) while transmitting load between members and minimising friction and wear at the interface.

Two surfaces in relative motion can interact in three ways. A plain (sliding) bearing lets them slide on a film of oil or low-friction polymer. A rolling-element bearing inserts hardened balls or rollers so the surfaces roll instead of slide. A fluid-film / magnetic bearing removes solid contact entirely. The micro card showed the headline number — and it is dramatic:

μ(sliding steel-on-steel) ≈ 0.15 – 0.30 μ(rolling-element bearing) ≈ 0.001 – 0.005 → ~50–100× less friction μ(hydrodynamic film) ≈ 0.001 – 0.01 (speed-dependent)

That gap is why almost every motor, gearbox, pump, turbine and wheel on Earth runs on rolling elements. But the trade is subtle: rolling contact concentrates the entire load onto a microscopically tiny contact patch, generating enormous local stress. The whole discipline of bearing engineering is about managing that stress so the element survives long enough.

Carry load

Radial, axial (thrust), or a combination — without seizing.

Guide motion

Keep the shaft centred and concentric; control runout.

Minimise friction

Cut drag torque, heat and energy waste.

Last predictably

Deliver a calculable, statistical service life.

The engineer's mindset

A bearing is never selected by “it fits the shaft.” It is selected by load, speed, life, environment and stiffness — and verified with the L10 equation on Page 5. Treat the rest of this deep dive as the toolkit that turns “it spins” into “it spins for 40,000 hours at 90% confidence.”

Page 2 · Anatomy

Inside a Bearing — Parts & Kinematics

The four functional parts

A rolling-element bearing has only four parts, but each does a precise job. The races (rings) provide the hardened raceways; the rolling elements carry load through point/line contact; the cage (separator/retainer) keeps the elements evenly spaced so they never touch each other; and the seals/shields retain lubricant and exclude contamination.

Deep-groove ball bearing. Outer race fixed in the housing; inner race + shaft rotate; balls orbit at roughly half shaft speed (the cage frequency).
Kinematics — the elements move slower than you think

The cage (and therefore each ball's orbit) rotates at the fundamental train frequency (FTF), typically ~0.4 × shaft speed. Each contact point on a race is struck at the ball-pass frequency. These frequencies are not trivia — they are exactly the signatures a vibration analyst looks for to detect a defect on a specific component (see Page 9).

FTF ≈ (n/2)·(1 − (d/D)·cosα) cage rotation BPFO = (Z/2)·(n)·(1 − (d/D)·cosα) outer-race defect BPFI = (Z/2)·(n)·(1 + (d/D)·cosα) inner-race defect n=shaft Hz · Z=ball count · d=ball Ø · D=pitch Ø · α=contact angle

Two reading rules: balls = point contact = lower friction & higher speed but lower capacity; rollers = line contact = higher capacity but more friction. And contact angle decides axial capacity — a deep-groove ball (α≈0°) takes a little thrust either way; an angular-contact or tapered roller (α=15–30°) takes heavy thrust one direction, so it is mounted in opposed pairs.

Contact geometry sets everything: a ball touches at a point, a cylinder along a line, and a taper's cone apex meets the shaft axis so radial and axial load share one element.
Page 4 · Stress

Loads & Hertzian Contact Stress

Equivalent dynamic load P

Real bearings rarely see pure radial or pure axial load. The standards combine them into a single equivalent dynamic load P using the X (radial) and Y (axial) factors from the catalogue:

P = X·Fr + Y·Fa equivalent dynamic load (N) Fr = radial force · Fa = axial force X,Y depend on the Fa/Fr ratio vs the limit e

This single number P is what feeds the life equation on the next page. Getting X and Y wrong — especially ignoring a thrust component — is the most common life-calculation error.

Hertzian contact — why bearings need hardened steel

Because a ball touches the race at almost a point, the contact patch is tiny (often <1 mm²) and the local pressure is colossal — routinely 1,500–3,000 MPa, near the yield strength of the steel itself. Hertz contact theory describes this elastic contact: load spreads over an elliptical patch, and the maximum shear stress sits just below the surface, ~0.3–0.5× the contact width deep.

The orange lobe is where peak shear stress lives — below the surface. Spalling fatigue cracks start there and work up, which is why surface inspection alone misses early damage.

That subsurface shear stress, applied millions of times as elements roll past, is the root cause of fatigue spalling. It is also why bearing steel (52100 chrome or case-hardened) is run at ~60 HRC with controlled cleanliness — inclusions become crack seeds.

Page 5 · Life Theory

L10 Bearing Life — The Master Equation

Basic rating life (ISO 281)

Bearing fatigue is statistical, not deterministic. Identical bearings under identical load fail at scattered times, so we quote L10: the life that 90% of a population will reach or exceed (i.e. 10% fail by then). The basic rating life equation is:

L10 = ( C / P )^p [ millions of revolutions ] C = basic dynamic load rating (from catalogue, N) P = equivalent dynamic load (Page 4, N) p = 3 for ball bearings (point contact) p = 10/3 ≈ 3.33 for roller bearings (line contact)

In operating hours at constant speed:

L10h = (10^6 / (60·n)) · (C/P)^p [ hours ] · n = rpm

The exponent is the headline insight: because life goes as the cube of the load ratio, halving the load multiplies life by 8×; doubling the load cuts life to ⅛. A small reduction in load — or a small overload — has a huge, non-linear effect. This is why oversizing slightly is cheap insurance and why a misalignment that adds edge load is so destructive.

The modified life — what reality adds

ISO 281 also defines the modified rating life that catalogues actually use for design:

Lnm = a1 · aISO · L10 modified rating life a1 = reliability factor (e.g. 0.21 for 99% reliability) aISO = life modifier: lubrication (κ), contamination (eC), fatigue limit

The lesson: clean oil and the right film thickness can multiply life many-fold; dirt and starvation can cut it below the basic L10. Lubrication (Page 7) is not maintenance trivia — it is a first-order term in the life equation.

Page 7 · Lubrication

Lubrication Regimes & the Stribeck Curve

A rolling bearing does not run metal-on-metal — it runs on a microscopic elastohydrodynamic (EHL) film, often just 0.1–1 µm thick, that the rolling motion drags into the contact and that the pressure stiffens almost solid. Whether that film fully separates the surfaces is captured by the film parameter λ (lambda):

λ = hmin / σ film thickness ÷ combined surface roughness λ > 3 full-film → minimal wear, max life λ ≈ 1–3 mixed → some asperity contact λ < 1 boundary → metal contact, rapid wear
The Stribeck curve: friction is high at low speed (boundary), drops to a minimum, then rises slowly as the full film thickens. Bearings are designed to run just past the minimum, in full-film.

Grease (~90% of bearings)

Oil held in a soap thickener. Simple, sealed-for-life, re-greasable. Limited by speed & temperature (NLGI grade, base-oil viscosity).

Oil (bath / mist / circulating)

For high speed or high heat — carries heat away and filters out debris. Needs a system but enables the longest life.

Two practical truths follow. First, grease ages and bleeds — over-greasing is as harmful as under-greasing (it churns, overheats, blows seals). Second, the right base-oil viscosity is the one that gives λ > 2 at operating temperature; pick it from the speed factor (n·dm) and temperature, not by habit.

Reading the ISO Designation

A bearing's part number is a compact spec sheet. The micro card hinted at it (“6204 = 20 mm bore”). Here is the full decode — the same system across SKF, FAG, NSK, NTN and Timken because it is standardised.

6205 → type 6 (deep-groove ball), series 2 (medium envelope), bore code 05 × 5 = 25 mm. So a 6205 = 25 mm bore, 52 mm OD, 15 mm wide — identical from any maker.

⚠️ The bore-code trap

The “×5” rule only starts at code 04. Codes 00–03 are the exceptions every junior engineer gets wrong: 00=10 mm, 01=12 mm, 02=15 mm, 03=17 mm. Memorise those four; everything above is simply ×5.

Suffixes carry the engineering nuance. C3 internal clearance, for example, is chosen when an interference fit or thermal growth would otherwise pinch the bearing — directly relevant to the shaft/housing fits you specify with GD&T (A21).

Page 9 · Forensics

Failure Analysis — Reading a Dead Bearing

Most bearings do not die of old age. Studies attribute the majority of premature failures to lubrication, contamination, mounting and misalignment — not to reaching L10. A failed bearing is a forensic record: the damage pattern names the root cause. Fix the cause, not just the bearing.

🕳️ Fatigue spalling

Flaked, pitted raceway. The expected end-of-life mode (subsurface Hertz fatigue). If it appears early → overload or misalignment edge-loading.

🏜️ Contamination / debris

Dents, bruising, dull matte tracks. Dirt or hard particles rolled into the raceway. Cause: bad seals, dirty grease, poor handling.

🔥 Lubrication failure

Discolouration (blue/brown), smearing, welded patches. Starvation or wrong/over lubricant. λ dropped below 1.

⚡ Electrical erosion (fluting)

Washboard ripples & frosting from current passing through the bearing — classic on VFD-driven motors. Fix with insulated bearings or shaft grounding rings.

📐 Misalignment

Wear path skewed across the raceway (not centred). Cause: bent shaft, soft foot, cocked housing. Edge load slashes L10.

🔩 False brinelling / fretting

Evenly spaced marks at ball pitch from vibration while stationary (transport, idle standby). Cause: micro-movement, no rotation to refresh film.

Condition monitoring — catch it before it lets go

Because each defect rings at a calculable frequency (the BPFO/BPFI/FTF of Page 2), vibration analysis and acoustic/ultrasound detect a flaw weeks before failure. This is the heartbeat of predictive maintenance and digital twins (A31): a spectrum peak at BPFO is a named, located, actionable fault — not a vague “it sounds bad.”

🔧 Maintenance reality (links TPM)

The discipline of catching these early — clean lube, correct mounting (induction heat the inner race, never hammer the balls), sealing and trend monitoring — is Total Productive Maintenance applied to the most replaced component in the plant.

Quick Check 1

Q: You halve the load on a deep-groove ball bearing (same speed). What happens to L10?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Life increases 8×. With p = 3, L10 ∝ (C/P)³, so halving P multiplies (C/P) by 2 and life by 2³ = 8. The cube law is the single most important number in bearing sizing.
Quick Check 2

Q: A motor on a VFD shows evenly spaced “washboard” ripples across the raceway. Cause and fix?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Electrical fluting — common-mode current from the PWM inverter discharges through the bearing, eroding the raceway. Fix: insulated/ceramic-hybrid bearings, a shaft grounding ring, or an output filter on the drive. (Ties to A08 VFD.)
Quick Check 3

Q: Decode bearing 7208 C3.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Type 7 = angular-contact ball (takes one-directional thrust, used in preloaded pairs), series 2, bore 08 × 5 = 40 mm, C3 = larger-than-normal internal clearance (for thermal growth / interference fit). A spindle-grade choice.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals, solved step by step.

Worked Example 1 — Full L10 life calculation

Given: a 6208 deep-groove ball bearing (C = 32.5 kN) carries a pure radial load of 4.0 kN at 1450 rpm. Find L10 in million revolutions and hours.

P = F_r = 4.0 kN (no axial component → equivalent load = radial) L10 = (C/P)³ = (32.5/4.0)³ = 8.125³ = 536 million rev L10h = 10⁶/(60·n) × L10 = 10⁶/(60×1450) × 536 = 11.49 × 536 ≈ 6 160 h

Discussion: 6 160 h is ~8.5 months of 24/7 duty — with 10% of bearings statistically failing sooner. If this is a critical pump, that is not a comfortable number: options are a larger bearing (C↑), load reduction (P↓, cubed leverage!), or accepting the maintenance interval and putting the point on the vibration route (→ A42). C/P = 8.1 sits in the healthy design band; the issue here is simply speed × hours, not misuse.

Worked Example 2 — Equivalent dynamic load (X·Fr + Y·Fa)

Given: the same 6208 now carries F_r = 3.0 kN and F_a = 1.5 kN. With Fa/Fr = 0.5 above e, tables give X = 0.56, Y = 1.55. Find P and the life penalty vs pure radial 3.0 kN.

P = X·F_r + Y·F_a = 0.56×3.0 + 1.55×1.5 = 1.68 + 2.33 = 4.0 kN life ratio vs P = 3.0 kN: (4.0/3.0)³ = 2.4× SHORTER

Discussion: a "small" axial load — half the radial — cost 58% of the bearing's life, because deep-groove bearings tolerate thrust poorly (the Y factor punishes it) and the cube amplifies everything. This is why axial load paths deserve explicit design attention: locate/non-locate arrangements, angular-contact pairs for real thrust, and correct mounting so thermal growth doesn't convert into hidden preload — the axial load nobody drew on the free-body diagram.

Worked Example 3 — Speed limit & lubrication selection via n·dm

Given: a 6208 (bore 40, OD 80 mm) must run at 9 000 rpm. Check the grease speed factor and choose lubrication.

dm = (d + D)/2 = (40 + 80)/2 = 60 mm n·dm = 9000 × 60 = 540 000 mm·rpm guide limits: grease ≈ 300–500k · oil bath ≈ 600k · circulating/jet ≫ ⇒ 540k exceeds comfortable grease range → oil lubrication (or high-speed grease + verified thermal test — marginal)

Discussion: n·dm is a churning/heat proxy: cage speed scales with dm×n, and grease shear-heats beyond its bleed capability. Past the limit, grease life collapses exponentially (rule of thumb: halve grease life for every 15 °C above 70 °C) — the bearing then dies of lubrication long before fatigue. The A42 link: an over-greased or overheated bearing announces itself in the ultrasonic band well before any defect frequency appears.

Worked Example 4 — Reliability & contamination adjusted life (ISO 281)

Given: the Example 1 bearing (L10 = 6 160 h) must reach 99% reliability. Compare clean-oil vs contaminated operation (a_ISO ≈ 4 vs 0.3 for this κ and cleanliness).

L_nm = a₁ × a_ISO × L10 a₁(99%) = 0.25 clean, good film: L = 0.25 × 4 × 6160 ≈ 6 160 h contaminated: L = 0.25 × 0.3 × 6160 ≈ 460 h — 13× worse

Discussion: moving from 90% to 99% reliability alone costs 4× (a₁ = 0.25); dirt costs another order of magnitude — while clean EHL conditions can give it all back. The practical reading: sealing and oil cleanliness (ISO 4406 → A43 p5) are life multipliers as powerful as bearing size, and far cheaper. The modified-life machinery is not academic: it is the catalogue's way of pricing contamination and lubrication decisions in hours.

Problem Set — 10 Questions

Work each one before revealing — C/P and the exponents do most of the lifting.

Problem 1

Q: Ball vs cylindrical-roller bearing, both at C/P = 5. Compare L10 in million revolutions.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Ball (p=3): 5³ = 125 Mrev. Roller (p=10/3): 5^(10/3) ≈ 214 Mrev — line contact rewards the same load ratio with ~70% more life. The exponent is type-specific; using 3 for rollers understates them.
Problem 2

Q: Load on a ball bearing doubles. Life changes by…?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: (½)³ = life ÷ 8. The cube is the whole tragedy of overload — a 26% load increase alone halves life (1.26³ ≈ 2).
Problem 3

Q: Decode 22216 E and name its superpower.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Type 2… series 22 = spherical roller, dimension series 22, bore 16 × 5 = 80 mm, E = reinforced design. Superpower: self-aligning under misalignment (±1.5°+) while carrying heavy radial + moderate axial load — the workhorse of fans, crushers and conveyor pulleys.
Problem 4

Q: When is C3 (increased) clearance specified instead of normal?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: When operating conditions will REMOVE clearance: interference fit on the shaft (expands the inner ring), significant inner-ring temperature rise (motors!), or both. Mounted-and-hot, a C3 bearing lands near normal clearance; a normal-clearance bearing would land preloaded — running hot, loud and short-lived. Standard for electric motors.
Problem 5

Q: EHL film 0.30 µm; composite surface roughness 0.25 µm. Compute λ and interpret.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: λ = h/σ = 0.30/0.25 = 1.2 — mixed regime: asperities touch through the film. Expect surface distress unless AW/EP additives carry the contacts. Target λ ≥ 3 for full separation: raise viscosity, cool the oil, or improve finish. λ is the single number connecting lubrication to fatigue life (a_ISO's κ is its cousin).
Problem 6

Q: A 6210 (bore 50, OD 90) runs at 11 000 rpm on grease. Verdict via n·dm?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: dm = 70 mm → n·dm = 770 000 — far beyond grease (~300–500k) and past plain oil bath (~600k): needs circulating oil / oil-air lubrication, likely with hybrid (ceramic ball) options. Speed capability is a lubrication property as much as a bearing property.
Problem 7

Q: F_r = 2.0 kN, F_a = 2.0 kN on a deep-groove bearing (X = 0.56, Y = 1.6 in this range). P, and life vs pure-radial 2.0 kN?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: P = 0.56×2.0 + 1.6×2.0 = 4.3 kN; life ratio = (4.3/2.0)³ ≈ 10× shorter. Equal axial and radial load on a deep-groove is a design smell — an angular-contact pair or a four-point-contact bearing wants this job.
Problem 8

Q: A gearbox spec demands 98% reliability. What fraction of catalogue L10 may you claim (a₁: 95%→0.64, 96%→0.55, 98%→0.37, 99%→0.25)?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: L2 = a₁ × L10 = 0.37 × L10 — barely a third. Reliability upgrades are expensive in hours because the Weibull failure distribution is broad; this is why "L10 = design life" is only ever a 90%-survival statement, and why critical trains combine bigger bearings WITH condition monitoring rather than paper reliability.
Problem 9

Q: Why are angular-contact pairs mounted with preload — what two problems does preload solve?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: (1) Stiffness & position: preload removes internal clearance, so the shaft holds its axis under load — micrometre repeatability for spindles (→ A40 CNC). (2) Ball guidance: unloaded balls in a fast bearing skid instead of roll, smearing raceways; preload keeps every ball driven. The cost: preload is permanent load — higher friction, heat, and a life entry in the P you must actually compute.
Problem 10

Q: A VFD-fed motor's bearing shows grey frosted raceways with regular washboard fluting. Mechanism and the three standard fixes?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Electrical erosion: PWM common-mode voltage builds shaft potential that discharges through the EHL film — micro-EDM craters organising into fluting (ISO 15243 class 3.4). Fixes: shaft grounding ring, insulated or hybrid ceramic bearing (break the circuit), dU/dt or common-mode filter at the drive (remove the source). Confirm with A42's envelope spectrum: fluting shows defect tones with sidebands at the switching-related frequencies (→ A08 VFD).

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Where the cube, the film and the a-factors come from.

Derivation 1 — why life goes as (C/P)^p
Hertz point contact: peak stress σ ∝ F^(1/3) (contact area grows with load) contact-fatigue (Lundberg–Palmgren): survival S with ln(1/S) ∝ τ^c · N^e · V → life N ∝ σ^(−c/e) chain them: N ∝ (F^(1/3))^(−c/e) = F^(−c/(3e)) empirically c/(3e) ≈ 3 for balls → L ∝ (C/P)³ line contact (rollers): σ ∝ F^(1/2)-ish geometry → exponent 10/3

Why it matters: the cube is not a decree — it is Hertz geometry × fatigue statistics. That is why it changes with contact type (ball vs roller), and why surface-origin failures (dirt, λ < 1) break the law entirely: the model assumes subsurface-initiated fatigue under clean EHL. When the assumption dies, so does the exponent — the physics behind a_ISO.

Derivation 2 — the EHL film and λ
elastohydrodynamic film (Dowson–Higginson form): h_min ∝ (η₀·v)^0.7 · α^0.5 · R^0.43 · F^(−0.13) ⇒ film thickness barely cares about load (−0.13!) ⇒ it is built by VISCOSITY × SPEED at the contact λ = h_min / √(Rq₁² + Rq₂²) target ≥ 3 · mixed 1–3 · boundary < 1

Why it matters: the tiny load exponent is the deep insight — you cannot crush a healthy EHL film with load; you starve it with low speed, low viscosity or heat. Hence: slow heavily-loaded bearings need high-viscosity oil or EP additives; fast bearings make film easily but overheat the lubricant. Every lubrication chart in the catalogue is this equation rearranged.

Derivation 3 — combining the a-factors
L_nm = a₁ · a_ISO · (C/P)^p a₁ reliability (Weibull tail): 90%→1 · 95%→0.64 · 98%→0.37 · 99%→0.25 a_ISO = f(κ, contamination e_C, P/C): κ = ν/ν₁ (viscosity ratio ≈ λ proxy) spans ~0.1 (dirty, starved) to ~50 (clean, thick film, light load)

Why it matters: a_ISO's enormous range (500:1) says the environment outweighs the catalogue: cleanliness and lubrication move life more than any same-size bearing upgrade. The MSc-level reading of ISO 281: the bearing is a system — seal, oil, filter, fit and temperature are all terms in its life equation.

Seven exam & field traps
  • 1
    P is the equivalent load, not F_r. Skipping X·Fr + Y·Fa on combined loads inflates life by the cube of the error.
  • 2
    L10 is 90% survival — not average. Mean life is ~5× L10; quoting L10 as "expected life" double-counts conservatism, quoting it as a guarantee ignores the 10%.
  • 3
    C vs C₀: dynamic rating governs fatigue; static rating governs brinelling at standstill/shock. A slewing or oscillating bearing is a C₀ problem.
  • 4
    Wrong exponent for rollers (3 instead of 10/3) understates roller life ~30% at typical C/P — a free size reduction missed.
  • 5
    Clearance class ≠ precision class. C3 is looseness; P5 is running accuracy. "Precision C3" is a coherent, common motor spec — not a contradiction.
  • 6
    Grease has its own L10. Beyond ~70 °C halve grease life per 15 °C; the bearing often outlives its grease — relubrication intervals are life calculations too.
  • 7
    Calculated defect frequencies sit 1–2% high vs measured (slip). Search a window in the spectrum, and remember BPFO+BPFI = n·f_r as the sanity check (→ A42).
