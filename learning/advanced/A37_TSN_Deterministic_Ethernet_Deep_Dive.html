Ethernet Is Fast — But It Doesn't Promise Anything

A control network doesn't need speed. It needs a GUARANTEE. Those are different products.

Definition

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) is a set of IEEE 802.1 standards that add bounded latency, sub-microsecond time synchronisation and seamless redundancy to ordinary switched Ethernet — so that control traffic and office traffic can share one network without the control traffic ever being late.

Classic Ethernet is best-effort: switches queue frames and forward them as soon as they can. On an idle network that takes microseconds; under load, a frame can sit behind a full queue for milliseconds — and no mechanism even tells you it happened. Average latency is excellent; worst-case latency is unbounded. A PLC cycle, a servo loop or a power-grid protection message doesn't care about the average — it cares about the one frame that arrives after the deadline (→ the same hard/firm/soft logic as A36, page 5).

Industry's first answer (2000s) was to modify Ethernet per vendor: PROFINET IRT reserves a scheduled red phase (→ A02), EtherCAT processes frames on the fly through slave silicon (→ A03), SERCOS III and Powerlink did their own thing. All work — none interoperate. TSN is the vendor-neutral redo: put the determinism into the IEEE 802.1 bridge standard itself, so any compliant switch from any vendor can carry any real-time protocol, plus your video streams and backups, on the same wire.

Three networks become one
What "deterministic" actually promises

Bounded latency

A provable worst-case end-to-end delay for each critical stream — not a hope, a calculation.

Bounded jitter

Arrival-time variation small enough for control loops and synchronised drives (→ A09).

Zero congestion loss

Reserved streams never lose frames to a full queue — loss becomes a hardware fault, not a load symptom.

Determinism ≠ speed. A 10 ms bound that ALWAYS holds beats a 50 µs average that sometimes becomes 20 ms.

Anatomy of Latency — Where the Microseconds Go

You cannot bound what you cannot decompose. Four delays add up per hop; only one of them is the villain.

The four components, per switch hop

Three of the four delays are physics and silicon — computable once. Queuing is traffic-dependent. All of TSN's shaping machinery exists to bound that one red box.

The numbers
t_ser = frame_bits / link_rate 1518 B ⇒ 12.3 µs @ 1 Gbit/s · 123 µs @ 100 Mbit/s t_prop ≈ 5 ns per metre of copper/fibre (100 m ⇒ 0.5 µs) t_queue,worst (no TSN, priority only) ≈ one max frame already departing + all queued higher/equal-priority frames ahead of you ⇒ grows with load, unbounded in general

Store-and-forward switches must receive the whole frame before forwarding, so serialisation is paid again at every hop. That is why hop count dominates worst-case budgets, and why flat, shallow topologies (or cut-through silicon) matter for motion-class traffic. Even with strict priority queuing, a critical frame that arrives one bit after a 1518-byte best-effort frame started transmitting waits the full 12.3 µs — priority cannot recall a frame already on the wire. Fixing exactly that is page 5 (gates) and page 6 (preemption).

Per hop: own serialisation + 2 µs processing + propagation + ONE 1518 B blocking frame (the frame that just beat you to the port). Real congested queues are worse — this is the best worst case without TSN gates.

The TSN Toolbox — A Family, Not a Protocol

TSN is a menu of IEEE 802.1 amendments. Every industry profile picks a different subset — nobody deploys them all.

The four pillars

Remember it as T-S-R-R: Time, Shaping, Reliability, Resources. Every TSN feature you'll ever meet fits one pillar.

Key mental model: TSN standards define mechanisms in bridges, not an application protocol. Your process data still travels as PROFINET, OPC UA Pub/Sub, or plain UDP — TSN just decides when each frame is allowed to leave each port. That layering is why one converged network can carry several industrial protocols simultaneously (page 9).

802.1AS — One Clock to Rule Them All

Scheduled gates are meaningless if every switch disagrees about what time it is. Sync comes first, always.

How gPTP measures link delay and offset
link_delay = ((t₂−t₁) + (t₄−t₃)) / 2 ← peer-delay, assumes symmetric cable offset = (t₂−t₁) − link_delay ← slave corrects its clock by −offset each bridge adds its residence time to the correction field → errors don't snowball blindly

802.1AS is a tightened profile of IEEE 1588 PTP: peer-to-peer delay on every link (not end-to-end), hardware timestamping mandatory, one domain, and a Best Master Clock Algorithm (BMCA) that elects the grandmaster automatically — priority fields first, then clock quality, then MAC address as tie-break. Bridges are time-aware relays: they syntonise (match frequency) to the GM and pass corrected time downstream. Typical per-hop error is tens of nanoseconds; over a sane industrial topology you stay comfortably below 1 µs across 7+ hops — the figure every scheduled feature silently assumes.

Engineering consequences
  • 1Asymmetric paths poison sync. The ÷2 assumes both directions take equal time. Mismatched fibre pairs, media converters, or one direction through a non-PTP-aware device inject a constant, invisible offset.
  • 2Every device in the sync path must be gPTP-capable. One dumb switch = timestamps corrupted by its queuing jitter. (Same class of mistake as putting an office switch inside a PROFINET IRT domain → A02.)
  • 3Plan grandmaster failover. BMCA re-elects automatically, but the network free-runs on local oscillators during the handover — schedules survive on holdover only if drift is small. Watch clockClass in diagnostics.
  • 4Sync error budget eats schedule margin. Gate windows must be padded by the worst clock disagreement between any two devices — the tighter your sync, the more bandwidth your schedule can actually use (page 5).

802.1Qbv — The Time-Aware Shaper

A railway timetable for frames: each egress queue gets a gate, and the gates open and close on a synchronized, repeating schedule.

Gate control list on one egress port
What it costs

Qbv buys hard bounds at the price of engineering effort and bandwidth. Someone must compute a global schedule: which stream transmits in which window on which port at which phase — an NP-hard scheduling problem solved by the CNC's tooling (page 8), not by hand. Guard bands waste one max-frame-time per gate transition (~12.3 µs at 1 G) unless preemption shrinks them (page 6). And a schedule is brittle by design: add one new cyclic stream and the whole timetable may need recomputation — exactly like adding a train to a saturated railway.

usable_TT_bandwidth ≈ (TT_window − sync_error_pad) / cycle guard_band = t_ser(max_frame) = 12.3 µs @1G → with preemption ≈ t_ser(127 B fragment) ≈ 1.0 µs

Preemption & the Other Shapers

Gates aren't the only tool. Preemption shrinks guard bands; CBS and ATS bound latency without any global schedule.

CBS (credit-based shaper) — the AVB inheritance — meters each class with a credit counter: transmit while credit ≥ 0, earn credit while idle/blocked at idleSlope, spend at sendSlope. It smooths bursts into a steady drip, which bounds downstream queue build-up without any schedule. ATS (asynchronous traffic shaping, from urgency-based scheduling theory) gives per-hop bounds for traffic that isn't periodic at all. The professional pattern is layered: Qbv windows for the motion class, CBS/ATS for alarms and video, best effort for the rest — all on the same port.

802.1CB FRER — Losing a Frame Without Noticing

At 10⁻⁹ frame-error rates, a gigabit link still drops frames every few hours. Critical streams can't wait for a retransmission.

FRER's advantage over PRP/HSR is granularity: replication is configured per stream, so the network duplicates the 2% of traffic that is safety- or motion-critical instead of doubling everything. Its subtlety is state: elimination functions track sequence-number windows, and a talker reset or misconfigured recovery window can silently blackhole a stream (failure mode #6, page 10). 802.1Qci (PSFP) complements it at ingress — per-stream gates, meters and filters that stop a babbling device from flooding a reserved class (the network-level equivalent of a fuse).

802.1Qcc — Who Computes the Timetable?

Streams must be requested, admitted and configured. Three architectures exist; industry overwhelmingly picked the centralized one.

Fully centralized model (the industrial default)
The three Qcc models

Fully distributed

Talkers announce streams hop-by-hop (SRP-style, as in AVB). Simple, no controller — but can't compute Qbv timetables. Fine for CBS-class streams.

Centralized network / distributed user

Devices still request at the edge; a CNC computes paths & schedules. Transitional model.

Fully centralized

CUC + CNC know everything, compute everything, push everything. The only model that scales to scheduled motion traffic — and the one PROFINET over TSN and OPC UA FX use.

Under the hood the CNC speaks NETCONF/RESTCONF + YANG models to every bridge — the same management machinery as datacentre SDN, aimed at gate control lists instead of routing tables. The practical consequence for the engineer: a TSN domain is engineered offline, like a PROFINET IRT domain (→ A02), not plug-and-pray like an office LAN. Change management, config backup and topology documentation stop being optional hygiene and become part of the control system itself.

Deployment reality check
  • 1Hardware is the entry ticket. Qbv/CB/AS live in switch silicon and PHY timestamping — no firmware update turns an office switch into TSN. Endpoints need TSN-capable NICs/MACs (common in modern industrial SoCs).
  • 2Profiles tame the toolbox. IEC/IEEE 60802 defines which features an industrial-automation device must implement, so vendors converge on an interoperable subset. Match devices to the profile, not to the marketing word "TSN".
  • 3Start where determinism pays. Machine-to-machine motion coordination, line-level controller sync, converged cells with vision + control (→ A16). Best-effort MQTT telemetry does not need Qbv — don't schedule what a queue can absorb.
  • 45G URLLC and wireless TSN extend bounded latency to mobile robots and tools — the 3GPP bridge model presents the 5G system as one virtual TSN bridge with measured, bounded delay. Early, but real (→ A33).
  • 5Security rides along: a converged network erases the old air gap — IEC 62443 zoning, PSFP ingress policing and 802.1X device authentication belong in the same design review as the schedule (→ A33 page 8).

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

Determinism is designed, calculated and monitored — never assumed.

The whole module in six lines
  • 1Ethernet's problem is not speed but unbounded queuing delay — TSN exists to bound the red box.
  • 2802.1AS gives every node one clock (sub-µs); everything scheduled stands on it.
  • 3Qbv gates reserve exclusive time windows — queuing delay ≈ 0 by construction, paid for with engineering and guard bands.
  • 4Preemption (Qbu/802.3br) shrinks blocking from 1518 B to 127 B; CBS/ATS bound latency without a global schedule.
  • 5FRER duplicates critical streams over disjoint paths — zero-time recovery, per stream, with sequence-number state to respect.
  • 6Qcc CUC/CNC computes and pushes the timetable — a TSN domain is an engineered artefact, managed like the control system it is.
Six classic failure modes
⏰ Sync loss, gates keep running

GM fails or a non-PTP device corrupts sync; bridges free-run and gate schedules drift apart. Streams start missing windows sporadically — monitor clockClass and offset, alarm on holdover.

🚦 Schedule starves best effort

Over-generous TT windows leave diagnostics, backups and vision uploads crawling. Users respond with rogue switches and bypass cables — audit gate utilisation like line OEE (→ A22).

🔀 Non-TSN switch in the path

One unmanaged switch re-introduces unbounded queuing and destroys the whole chain's bound. The calculation is only as good as its weakest hop — enforce with topology discovery (LLDP).

📏 Asymmetric link breaks sync

Media converters or mismatched paths violate the symmetric-delay assumption; a constant hidden offset shifts every gate. Verify with round-trip measurements at commissioning.

💥 Babbling talker floods a class

A faulty device transmits far beyond its reservation, drowning its priority class. Without Qci ingress policing, one sensor takes down the reserved lane it shares.

👻 FRER sequence blackhole

Talker reboot resets sequence numbers outside the listener's recovery window — every frame gets "eliminated" as a stale duplicate. Symptom: perfect links, zero traffic. Reset the recovery function or age out state.

Quick check 1

Why can't strict priority alone bound latency for a critical frame?

Tap to revealPriority chooses the next frame from the queues, but it cannot interrupt a frame already on the wire — a 1518 B best-effort frame that started first blocks the port for its full serialisation time (12.3 µs @1G), and under sustained load queue depth ahead of you is unbounded. Gates (Qbv) or preemption (Qbu) are needed to bound it.
Quick check 2

In gPTP, how are link delay and offset computed from t₁…t₄ — and what assumption can silently break the result?

Tap to reveallink_delay = ((t₂−t₁)+(t₄−t₃))/2 and offset = (t₂−t₁) − link_delay. The division by two assumes the path is symmetric; asymmetric media (converters, mismatched fibres) injects a constant offset error that no averaging removes.
Quick check 3

What is a Qbv guard band for, and how does frame preemption shrink it?

Tap to revealThe guard band before a protected window stops any frame starting that couldn't finish before the gate closes — sized to one max frame (~12.3 µs @1G). With preemption, a preemptable frame can be cut at a 64 B fragment boundary, so the worst blocking falls to a 127 B fragment (~1 µs), letting schedules pack ~11 µs tighter per transition.
Quick check 4

A stream needs zero-loss behaviour through a switch failure. Compare MRP, PRP and FRER for this job.

Tap to revealMRP is a ring that reconfigures in tens–hundreds of ms — frames are lost during the hole. PRP duplicates ALL traffic across two separate LANs with zero recovery time but doubles the whole network. FRER duplicates per-stream over disjoint paths inside one TSN network — seamless like PRP, but only for the streams that need it.
Quick check 5

Why does the fully-distributed Qcc model fundamentally not work for scheduled (Qbv) traffic?

Tap to revealA Qbv timetable is a global optimisation: every gate on every port along every path must be phase-coordinated. Hop-by-hop reservation only knows local state — it can admit bandwidth (CBS-style) but cannot compute a coherent network-wide schedule. That requires the CNC's complete topology + stream view.
Quick check 6

Your Qbv line works for months, then one device's frames start arriving late a few times per hour. Name three suspects, in test order.

Tap to reveal(1) Clock sync degradation — check gPTP offset/clockClass logs first, it's the base of everything. (2) A topology change or replaced switch that isn't running the pushed schedule (config drift vs CNC). (3) A new or babbling talker sharing the class without a reservation — check Qci counters/port statistics for policing drops and unexpected load.
