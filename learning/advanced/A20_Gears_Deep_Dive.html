Why Gears Exist — Power, Speed & Torque

Precise Definition

A gear is a toothed wheel that transmits rotary motion and torque between shafts by positive (form) engagement, holding an exact, slip-free velocity ratio set by the tooth counts.

The micro card stated the core trade. Let us make it exact. Mechanical power is the product of torque and angular velocity, P = T·ω. A gear pair conserves power (minus a few percent friction), so if it lowers speed it must raise torque by the same factor — and vice versa. That is the whole reason gears exist: motors are efficient at high speed and low torque, but the world (wheels, drums, conveyors, robot joints) needs low speed and high torque.

P = T·ω power = torque × angular velocity (W = N·m × rad/s) P_in ≈ P_out (η ≈ 0.97–0.99 per spur stage) ∴ T_out / T_in = ω_in / ω_out = i (the gear ratio)

Unlike a belt or friction drive, a gear cannot slip: every tooth that goes in must come out, so the ratio is exact and the timing is preserved. That positive engagement is why gears run camshafts, robot axes and machine-tool indexers where a fraction of a degree matters.

Multiply torque

Reduce speed → amplify torque to drive heavy loads.

Exact ratio

Tooth counts are integers → no slip, no drift, perfect timing.

Change direction

Reverse rotation, or turn a corner (bevel), or convert rotary↔linear (rack).

Match inertia

A reducer scales reflected load inertia by 1/i² — key for servos (A09).

The engineer's mindset

A gear pair is never just “two wheels that mesh.” It is selected by ratio, torque, speed, life, noise and precision — and verified against tooth bending and surface fatigue (Page 8). The chapters ahead turn “it turns” into “it transmits 12 kW for 20,000 hours at grade-8 quietness.”

Page 2 · The Law of Gearing

Why Teeth Are Involute — Conjugate Action

The fundamental law of gearing

For two gears to transmit motion at a constant velocity ratio, the common normal to the tooth surfaces at the point of contact must always pass through a fixed point on the line of centres — the pitch point. This is the law of conjugate action. Almost any random tooth shape would make the driven gear speed up and slow down within each tooth, producing vibration and noise.

Exactly one practical curve satisfies the law for all positions: the involute of a circle — the path traced by the end of a string unwound from a cylinder (the base circle). Because the involute’s normal is always tangent to the base circle, contact stays on a single straight line of action inclined at the pressure angle. The result is smooth, constant-ratio motion that is insensitive to centre-distance error — a uniquely forgiving property no other profile shares.

Two involute gears mesh. Contact slides along the straight blue line of action, always passing through the green pitch point. The angle φ between the line of action and the common tangent is the pressure angle.
Pressure angle (φ) — the standard 20°

The pressure angle sets the slope of the line of action. It splits the tooth force into a useful tangential component and a wasteful radial (separating) one. The world standardised on 20° as the best compromise: stronger tooth roots and fewer undercut problems than the old 14.5°, while 25° gives even more strength at the cost of higher separating force and noise. Two gears must share the same module and pressure angle to mesh.

💡 The forgiving involute

Because the involute depends only on the base circle, pulling the gears slightly apart just shrinks the contact ratio and changes the operating pressure angle — the velocity ratio stays exactly constant. This tolerance to centre-distance error is why involute gears dominate; cycloidal teeth (still used in clocks and some precision reducers) do not share it.

Page 3 · The Language

Module, Pitch & Tooth Geometry

Gears have their own vocabulary, and every term is one number you can measure or look up. The single most important is the module (m) — the size of the tooth. Two gears mesh only if their modules match.

m = d / z module = pitch diameter ÷ number of teeth (mm) p = π·m circular pitch (mm along the pitch circle) d = m·z pitch diameter d_a = m·(z + 2) addendum (outside) diameter d_f = m·(z − 2.5) dedendum (root) diameter C = m·(z1 + z2)/2 centre distance of a mating pair

In imperial practice the equivalent term is diametral pitch (Pd = z/d in teeth-per-inch); note that bigger Pd means smaller teeth, the opposite sense of module. The handy bridge is m = 25.4 / Pd.

Anatomy of involute teeth. The addendum sticks out one module above the pitch line; the dedendum reaches 1.25 m below it, leaving root clearance. Tooth thickness ≈ half the circular pitch.
Contact ratio & minimum teeth

The contact ratio (mp) is the average number of tooth pairs in mesh at once; it must exceed 1.0 (or motion would stop) and is ideally 1.4–2.0 so load shares across teeth and runs quietly. Pinions with too few teeth suffer undercutting — the cutter gouges the root and weakens it. For a standard 20° full-depth tooth the practical minimum is 17 teeth (or fewer with profile shift / addendum modification).

The Gear Family — Geometry Sets the Job

The micro card showed four; here is the full working set, organised by shaft relationship. Parallel-shaft gears (spur, helical) are the efficient workhorses; intersecting-shaft gears (bevel) turn corners; non-intersecting / crossed gears (worm, hypoid) give huge ratios or offset axes at a cost in efficiency.

Two reading rules. First, helix angle buys quietness but costs axial thrust that the bearings (A19) must absorb — which is why a herringbone exists. Second, worm self-locking is a feature, not a bug: if the lead angle is below the friction angle the wheel cannot back-drive the worm, holding a lifted load with no brake — but efficiency falls and heat rises, so worms often need cooling.

Spur

Cheap, efficient, noisy. The default parallel-shaft gear.

Helical

Quiet & strong; pays in axial thrust. Most gearboxes.

Worm

50:1+ in one stage, self-locking, low efficiency, runs hot.

Page 5 · Ratios

Gear Ratios & Gear Trains

The ratio of a single pair

The micro card gave the core formula. The ratio depends only on tooth counts (or, equivalently, pitch diameters), because the modules are equal:

i = z_driven / z_driver = d_driven / d_driver = ω_driver / ω_driven T_out = T_in · i · η torque amplified by the ratio (× efficiency) ω_out = ω_in / i speed divided by the ratio

An idler gear between driver and driven does not change the ratio — it cancels out of the maths — but it reverses the output direction and bridges a centre distance. That is its only purpose.

Compound trains — multiplying stages

A single spur pair is limited to about 6:1 before the big gear becomes huge. To get more, you compound: stack stages on shared shafts. The overall ratio is the product of the stage ratios, and the train value is the product of driven/driver counts:

i_total = i₁ · i₂ · i₃ · … = (z2/z1) · (z4/z3) · (z6/z5) · … compound train value
Compound train: stage 1 = 48/12 = 4, stage 2 = 70/14 = 5. Overall i = 4 × 5 = 20:1. Stacking small drivers with big driven gears multiplies cleanly.
🔧 Reflected inertia — the servo link (A09)

A reducer of ratio i divides the reflected load inertia seen by the motor by i². That is why a servo joint with a 20:1 reducer feels 400× lighter to the motor — the single biggest reason precision robots use planetary reducers. Pairs straight into the inertia-ratio tuning of A09.

Page 6 · Epicyclic

Planetary (Epicyclic) Gearsets

The planetary gearset is the highest torque-density gear arrangement in common use, and the heart of nearly every precision robot reducer, automatic transmission and wind-turbine gearbox. It packs a sun gear in the centre, several planet gears orbiting it on a carrier, all enclosed by an internal ring (annulus) gear.

A 3-planet set. Three planets share the load (≈⅓ each), giving high torque in a compact, coaxial, balanced package. Input and output are on the same axis line.
The Willis equation — one formula for every mode

Because three members (sun, ring, carrier) can each be input, output or held, a single planetary set offers several ratios. They are all governed by the Willis fundamental equation, where N = z_ring / z_sun:

ω_sun − ω_carrier z_ring ───────────────── = − ────── = − N Willis equation ω_ring − ω_carrier z_sun Common modes (ring fixed, ω_ring = 0): i = ω_sun / ω_carrier = 1 + N = 1 + z_ring/z_sun sun→carrier reduction Carrier fixed: sun→ring is a simple −z_ring/z_sun reverse All three locked together: i = 1 (direct drive)

A worked case: sun z=24, ring z=72, so N=3. With the ring held and the sun driven, i = 1 + 3 = 4:1 — and the output (carrier) turns the same direction as the input. Stack two such stages and you get 16:1 in a cylinder barely larger than the motor: exactly the recipe inside a robot-joint reducer.

Page 7 · Forces

Tooth Forces — What the Bearings Feel

The torque a gear transmits is delivered as a force pushing along the line of action at the pitch radius. Resolving it gives the three components every gearbox designer must know — because they are exactly the loads the shaft and bearings (A19) must carry.

W_t = 2·T / d tangential (transmitted) force — does the work W_r = W_t · tan φ radial (separating) force — pushes gears apart W_a = W_t · tan ψ axial (thrust) force — helical only, ψ = helix angle W_normal = W_t / (cos φ · cos ψ) total force on the tooth

For a spur gear there is no axial term (ψ=0): just tangential and radial. For a helical gear the helix angle ψ produces a thrust W_a that must be absorbed by a thrust-capable bearing — the price of quiet running. This is the mechanical handshake between Pages 4 and A19: pick a helical gear for noise and you have just specified an angular-contact or tapered bearing to take the thrust.

The normal tooth force resolves into a tangential component W_t (which transmits the torque) and a radial component W_r = W_t·tan φ that simply tries to push the gears apart and loads the bearings.

Worked number: a gear of pitch diameter d = 100 mm transmitting T = 50 N·m sees W_t = 2·50 / 0.100 = 1000 N tangential, plus W_r = 1000·tan 20° ≈ 364 N radial. Those vectors, multiplied by the gear position, set the bearing reactions — and feed straight into the L10 life calc of A19.

Page 8 · Rating

Gear Strength — AGMA Bending & Pitting

A gear can fail two ways, and a designer must check both. The tooth can break off at the root (bending fatigue) or its surface can pit and flake (contact/Hertzian fatigue). The AGMA / ISO 6336 standards turn each into a stress you compare against an allowable.

Bending — the Lewis equation & AGMA refinement

The classic Lewis equation treats the tooth as a cantilever beam. AGMA wraps it in correction factors for overload, dynamics, size and load distribution:

σ = (W_t / (F·m·Y)) Lewis bending stress (Y = Lewis form factor) σ = (W_t·K_o·K_v·K_s / (F·m)) · (K_H·K_B / Y_J) AGMA bending K_o overload · K_v dynamic · K_s size · K_H load-dist · K_B rim

The intuition: bending stress falls as module and face width grow. Make the teeth bigger or wider and the root survives more cycles. The form factor Y rewards more teeth (a fatter root).

Pitting — contact (Hertzian) stress

Surface durability uses a Hertzian contact stress (the same physics as bearings, A19), scaled by an elastic coefficient C_p and a geometry factor I:

σ_c = C_p · √( W_t·K_o·K_v·K_s·K_H / (F·d·I) ) AGMA contact stress σ_c ≤ σ_c,allow / SF design is safe if below the allowable / safety factor

Bending limit

Root cracks → tooth snaps. Cured by bigger module, wider face, root fillet, shot-peening.

Pitting limit

Surface fatigue → spalling. Cured by harder surface (carburise to 58–62 HRC), better lubrication, lower load.

Quality grade — ties to GD&T (A21)

Real teeth are never perfect; AGMA quality (Q) or ISO accuracy grade quantifies the allowable profile, pitch and runout error. A higher grade (e.g. ISO grade 5–6 spindle gears vs grade 9–11 farm gears) runs quieter and carries dynamic load better, and is specified with the same tolerancing language as GD&T (A21). Quality, lubrication and surface hardness together set whether your AGMA numbers hold in service.

Page 9 · Forensics

Failure Analysis — Reading a Dead Gear

Like bearings, most gears do not die of designed-in fatigue — they die of a cause the damage pattern reveals. Fix the cause, not just the gear. The AGMA failure-mode catalogue groups damage into wear, surface fatigue, plastic flow and breakage.

🕳️ Pitting (surface fatigue)

Small craters on the flank, usually near the pitch line. The expected contact-fatigue end-of-life. Early/severe → overload, thin oil film, or too-soft surface.

🔥 Scoring / scuffing

Radial scratches, smeared metal, discolouration. Lubricant film broke down under heat & sliding (worst at tooth tip/root). Cause: wrong oil, overload, high speed.

💥 Tooth breakage (bending)

A tooth snaps at the root fillet. Fatigue (beach marks) = chronic overload/stress riser; sudden (rough fracture) = shock or jam. The catastrophic mode.

🏜️ Abrasive wear

Uniform loss of profile, matte flanks. Hard particles in the oil grind the teeth. Cause: dirty lubricant, no filtration, worn seals.

📐 Misalignment / edge loading

Wear/pitting concentrated at one end of the tooth face. Cause: non-parallel shafts, deflection, poor mounting. Slashes capacity.

🌊 Plastic flow / rippling

Cold-worked ridges or rolled-over tips on softer gears under heavy, slow load. Surface yields rather than fatigues.

Condition monitoring — the gear-mesh frequency

Every gearbox sings at its gear-mesh frequency (GMF = shaft speed × tooth count) plus sidebands. A growing sideband or a sub-harmonic at a specific GMF localises a damaged gear weeks before it lets go — the same vibration toolkit as bearings (A19), and the data stream behind predictive maintenance & digital twins (A31).

GMF = z · n gear-mesh frequency (Hz) — n = shaft Hz, z = teeth sidebands at GMF ± shaft frequency localise the faulty gear
🔧 Maintenance reality (links TPM & lubrication)

The discipline that keeps gears alive — correct oil grade & level, oil analysis for wear metals, alignment, and avoiding shock overload — is Total Productive Maintenance applied to the drivetrain. Most “gear failures” are really lubrication or alignment failures wearing a gear costume.

Selection workflow — the 6 questions
  • 1
    Ratio & direction: how much reduction, same or reversed? → single pair, idler, compound or planetary.
  • 2
    Shaft geometry: parallel, intersecting, offset or coaxial? → spur/helical, bevel, hypoid/worm, planetary.
  • 3
    Torque & life: check AGMA bending and pitting → set module, face width, hardness.
  • 4
    Noise & speed: quiet or cheap? → helical (quiet, thrust) vs spur (noisy, simple).
  • 5
    Bearings & thrust: resolve W_t, W_r, W_a → specify the bearing set (A19).
  • 6
    Precision & backlash: servo-grade? → high AGMA/ISO quality, preloaded or anti-backlash gears (A09).
Quick Check 1

Q: A 12-tooth pinion drives a 60-tooth gear. Motor delivers 4 N·m at 3000 rpm. Output speed and torque (ignore losses)?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Ratio i = 60/12 = 5:1. Output speed = 3000/5 = 600 rpm; output torque = 4 × 5 = 20 N·m. Power is unchanged: 4·314 ≈ 20·63 ≈ 1.26 kW. Speed traded for torque.
Quick Check 2

Q: Why is the involute curve used for gear teeth rather than any other shape?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: It satisfies the law of conjugate action — the common normal always passes through the pitch point, giving a constant velocity ratio. Uniquely, the involute is also tolerant of centre-distance error (the ratio stays exact even if the gears are pulled slightly apart), which makes it manufacturable and forgiving.
Quick Check 3

Q: A planetary set has sun z=20, ring z=80. Ring is held, sun is driven. What is the reduction to the carrier, and which way does it turn?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: N = z_ring/z_sun = 80/20 = 4. Ring-fixed reduction i = 1 + N = 5:1, and the carrier turns the same direction as the sun. Compact, coaxial, load shared over the planets — the classic robot-reducer recipe.
Quick Check 4

Q: You spec a helical gear for quietness. What new load have you just created, and who must absorb it?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: An axial thrust force W_a = W_t·tan ψ from the helix angle. It must be carried by a thrust-capable bearing (angular-contact or tapered, A19) — or cancelled by going double-helical/herringbone. Quietness is paid for in thrust.
