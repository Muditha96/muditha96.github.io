What a MOSFET Really Is

A silicon valve where a voltage — not a current — controls the flow.

Definition

A MOSFET — Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor — is a voltage-controlled switch: a voltage on the insulated gate creates an electric field that opens or closes a conducting channel between the drain and source. Because the gate sits behind a thin insulating oxide, it draws almost zero steady current — you control amperes with microamps.

Invented in concept by Lilienfeld (1925) and first built at Bell Labs by Atalla & Kahng (1959), the MOSFET became the most-manufactured device in human history. In power electronics it is the switch that chops, regulates and inverts energy in everything from a phone charger to your micro card's VFD and servo drive.

The key contrast with the older BJT (bipolar junction transistor): a BJT is current-controlled (base current sets collector current); a MOSFET is voltage-controlled (gate voltage sets the channel). That single difference makes MOSFETs fast, efficient and easy to drive from logic.

Device Physics — How the Channel Forms

The "field effect": an electric field reaches through an insulator to summon a channel of charge.

Structure of an N-Channel Enhancement MOSFET

Take a p-type silicon body. Diffuse two heavily-doped n+ wells — these become the source and drain. Grow a thin SiO₂ oxide over the gap and deposit a gate (metal or poly-silicon) on top. With zero gate voltage the two n-wells are isolated by the p-body — no current can flow.

When Vgs exceeds the threshold Vth, the field inverts the silicon surface into an electron channel — current can now flow drain↔source.
Threshold, Enhancement vs Depletion, N vs P

Vth — threshold voltage

The minimum Vgs that forms the channel. Logic-level FETs: ~1–2 V; standard: ~2–4 V.

Enhancement vs depletion

Enhancement (normally-off): needs Vgs to turn ON — 99% of power parts. Depletion: normally-on.

N-channel

Electrons carry current. Lower Rds(on), faster — the workhorse. Turn ON with +Vgs.

P-channel

Holes carry current. Easy high-side switching but higher Rds(on). Turn ON with −Vgs.

The I–V Curves — Three Regions

Cut-off, saturation (active) and triode (ohmic). Power switching lives in two of them.

The Output Characteristic

Plot drain current Id against drain-source voltage Vds for several gate voltages and you get the classic family of curves with three regions:

Left of the dashed line = triode (a voltage-controlled resistor — where a closed switch sits). Right = saturation (a current source — used for linear amps, and crossed briefly during every switching transition).
The Governing Equations
Cut-off (Vgs < Vth): Id = 0 → switch OFF Saturation (Vds ≥ Vgs−Vth): Id = ½·k·(Vgs−Vth)² → amplifier / transition Triode (Vds < Vgs−Vth): Id ≈ k·[(Vgs−Vth)·Vds] → acts as a resistor where k = μ·Cox·(W/L) (process & geometry constant) Transconductance gm = ∂Id/∂Vgs — the "gain" of the device in saturation.

The switching insight: a power MOSFET wants to spend its life in cut-off (fully OFF) or deep triode (fully ON, looking like a tiny resistor). The saturation region in between is where heat is made — so we cross it as fast as possible.

As a Switch — Rds(on) & Conduction Loss

Fully ON, a MOSFET is just a small resistor. That resistance sets your steady-state heat.

On-Resistance

When fully enhanced (high Vgs), the channel behaves as a fixed resistance Rds(on) — modern power parts hit single-digit milliohms. The power burned simply conducting load current is pure I²R:

Conduction loss: P_cond = I²d(rms) × Rds(on) × D (D = duty cycle the FET is ON) Rds(on) rises with temperature → ~+0.4%/°C (positive tempco) ✅ This is GOOD: it forces current sharing when paralleling FETs.

The positive temperature coefficient is a safety feature: if one paralleled FET hogs current and heats up, its Rds(on) rises and pushes current to its cooler siblings. (BJTs do the opposite and thermally run away.)

The Body Diode

Every power MOSFET has an intrinsic body diode from source to drain (a by-product of the p-body / n-drain junction). It conducts reverse current — essential as a freewheeling path in motor & inverter half-bridges — but it is slow with poor reverse recovery, a real source of switching loss and ringing in hard-switched bridges.

Gate Charge & Driving the Gate

The gate is a capacitor. Turning the FET ON means shoving charge into it — fast.

Why "Zero Gate Current" Is a Half-Truth

DC gate current is ~0, but the gate is a capacitor (Ciss = Cgs + Cgd). To switch, you must charge and discharge it every cycle. The total charge is Qg, and how fast you can move it sets the switching speed:

Gate-drive current: Ig = Qg × fsw (average drive current) Turn-on/off time: t ≈ Qg / I_driver Drive power: P_drive = Qg × Vgs × fsw Caps: Ciss = Cgs+Cgd (input) · Crss = Cgd (reverse, the troublemaker) · Coss = Cds+Cgd (output)
The Miller Plateau

During switching, the gate voltage stalls at a flat "Miller plateau." Here all the driver's charge is diverted into the Cgd (Miller) capacitance as Vds swings, instead of raising Vgs. The plateau is exactly the slow, lossy part of the transition — minimising Cgd (Crss) and using a stiff, low-impedance gate driver is how you shorten it.

The flat region is the Miller plateau — the gate driver is busy charging Cgd while Vds collapses. This interval is where most switching energy is lost.
Gate-Driver Essentials

Stiff & low-Z

A dedicated driver IC sources/sinks amps to move Qg in nanoseconds.

Rg — gate resistor

Trades speed for control: smaller Rg = faster but more ringing/EMI.

Negative/clamp off

Pull gate firmly to 0 V (or below) to defeat Miller-induced false turn-on.

Switching Loss — Where the Heat Hides

The answer to your micro card's quiz: why 100% duty is cool but PWM gets hot.

Two Kinds of Loss

Total MOSFET loss splits into conduction (steady I²R while ON) and switching (energy burned during each ON↔OFF transition, while both voltage and current are momentarily high). Switching loss scales directly with frequency:

P_total = P_cond + P_sw P_cond = I²·Rds(on)·D P_sw = ½ · Vds · Id · (t_on + t_off) · fsw ← grows with frequency! Plus: gate loss (Qg·Vgs·fsw) + Coss & body-diode recovery losses

The trade-off that defines power design: raise the switching frequency and your magnetics & capacitors shrink (good) — but P_sw climbs linearly (bad). The art is picking fsw where total loss + size is optimal.

The Micro-Card Quiz, Answered Rigorously

"Why almost no heat at 100% duty but lots at 50%?" At 100% duty the FET is continuously ON — it only ever burns small conduction loss (I²·Rds(on)) and never transitions, so P_sw ≈ 0. At 50% PWM it transitions thousands of times per second, and each crossing of the lossy saturation region dumps ½·V·I·t of energy — multiplied by fsw. Hence: switching, not conduction, is what cooks a hard-switched FET.

The Half-Bridge — Building Block of Drives

Two FETs stacked across the DC bus. Six of these = the inverter in your VFD.

Topology

A half-bridge places a high-side and a low-side FET in series across the DC bus, with the load tapped at the midpoint. Toggling them alternately swings the output between +V and 0, and PWM sets the average. Three half-bridges in parallel make the 3-phase inverter — exactly the 6-switch stage your micro card cites in a VFD.

One leg of an inverter. Q1 high, Q2 low → output ≈ +V; reverse → output ≈ 0. Never both ON together.
Dead-Time & Shoot-Through

If Q1 and Q2 are ever ON simultaneously, they form a direct short across the bus — shoot-through — and explode. The fix is dead-time: a deliberate few-hundred-nanosecond gap where both are OFF during the cross-over, letting one fully turn off before the other turns on. Too little dead-time = shoot-through; too much = output distortion. The body diode freewheels the inductive load current during the gap.

⚡ Shoot-through

Both FETs on → bus short → catastrophic. Dead-time prevents it.

🔋 Bootstrap supply

The high-side gate floats — a bootstrap cap/diode (or isolated supply) powers its driver above the bus.

SOA & the Thermal Chain

Silicon lives or dies by junction temperature. Master the thermal resistor ladder.

The Thermal Equation — Ohm's Law for Heat

Heat flows from the silicon junction to ambient through a series of thermal resistances, exactly like current through resistors. Power is the "current," temperature rise is the "voltage":

Tj = Ta + P_loss × (Rθjc + Rθcs + Rθsa) Tj = junction temperature (keep below Tj(max), usually 150–175°C) Rθjc = junction→case · Rθcs = case→heatsink (TIM) · Rθsa = heatsink→air Analogy: P ↔ current, ΔT ↔ voltage, Rθ ↔ resistance, Tj ↔ node voltage

Design loop: estimate P_loss (pages 4 & 6) → add the Rθ ladder → check Tj stays under limit with margin. If not: lower Rds(on), drop fsw, add heatsink/airflow, or parallel devices. This ties straight back to your VFD thermal sizing.

Safe Operating Area (SOA)

The SOA is a log-log chart of allowable Vds vs Id, bounded by four limits: Rds(on) (current), package power, thermal, and at high voltage the dreaded thermal instability line. Stay inside it for the relevant pulse time and the part survives; a hard-switched inductive turn-off that punches outside the SOA is a classic failure.

Inside the polygon = safe. Short pulses extend the area (dashed); DC operation shrinks it. Inductive switching transients must be snubbed/clamped to stay inside.

Picking the right switch: the four technologies that share the power-electronics map.

Why MOSFET vs IGBT Is the Classic Decision

An IGBT is a hybrid: a MOSFET gate driving a BJT-like output. It has a near-constant ~1.5–2 V on-state voltage drop (Vce(sat)) instead of a resistance, so at high voltage and high current it loses less than a MOSFET would — but it switches slower and has a turn-off "tail current." Rule of thumb: high-V + high-I + lower-frequency ⇒ IGBT (the VFD inverter); lower-V or high-frequency ⇒ MOSFET.

SiC — wide bandgap

Blocks higher voltage in less silicon, runs hotter, switches faster → smaller, cooler EV inverters. Higher cost.

GaN — high electron mobility

Lateral HEMT, no body diode, blistering speed at <650 V → the shrinking phone charger. Sensitive gate.

This is the same hierarchy your micro card's BJT/MOSFET/IGBT trio introduced — now with the wide-bandgap newcomers that are displacing silicon at the high-performance edge.

Failure Modes, Recap & Quiz

How MOSFETs die — and the one-line truths to carry away.

How Power MOSFETs Fail
💥 Overvoltage / avalanche

Vds spike past the rating (inductive turn-off, no snubber) punches through. Clamp & snub.

🔥 Thermal overstress

Tj exceeds limit — undersized heatsink or runaway switching loss. Cooks the die.

⚡ Gate-oxide rupture

Vgs over ±20 V (ESD or ringing) destroys the thin oxide. Use a Zener clamp & handle with care.

🔌 Shoot-through

Insufficient dead-time → both bridge FETs on → bus short → instant death.

🌀 dV/dt false turn-on

Fast Vds edge couples through Cgd, momentarily turning the FET on. Stiff, low-Z (or negative) gate off.

↩️ Body-diode recovery

Slow body diode + hard reverse recovery → huge current spike & ringing in bridges. Add SiC/Schottky.

One-Page Recap
  • 1A MOSFET is a voltage-controlled switch — a gate field opens an inversion channel from drain to source.
  • 2It lives in cut-off (OFF) or deep triode (ON as a tiny Rds(on)); the saturation region in between makes heat.
  • 3Loss = conduction (I²·Rds(on)) + switching (½·V·I·t·fsw). Switching loss grows with frequency.
  • 4The gate is a capacitor: Qg & the Miller plateau set switching speed — drive it stiff and fast.
  • 5Half-bridges with dead-time build inverters; survival is governed by SOA and the Rθ thermal chain.
Quick Check — Tap to Reveal

Q1: A MOSFET stays cool at 100% duty but overheats at 50% PWM. Why?

Tap to reveal ↓A: At 100% it never transitions → only small conduction loss. At 50% it switches thousands of times/sec, and each crossing of the saturation region dumps ½·V·I·t of energy × fsw — switching loss dominates.

Q2: Why does the positive temperature coefficient of Rds(on) make MOSFETs easy to parallel?

Tap to reveal ↓A: If one FET hogs current and heats up, its Rds(on) rises (~+0.4%/°C), which pushes current to the cooler parallel devices — automatic current sharing. BJTs do the opposite and thermally run away.

Q3: What is the Miller plateau and why does it matter?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The flat region of Vgs-vs-Qg where all the driver charge feeds Cgd (Miller cap) as Vds swings. It's the slow, lossy part of every transition — minimise Crss and use a stiff driver to shorten it.

Q4: Why is dead-time mandatory in a half-bridge, and what's the risk of too much?

Tap to reveal ↓A: To prevent shoot-through (both FETs on = bus short). Too much dead-time distorts the output waveform and lets the body diode conduct longer (extra loss). It's a tuned compromise.

Q5: When would you choose an IGBT over a MOSFET for a motor drive?

Tap to reveal ↓A: At high voltage + high current + moderate frequency (e.g. a 480 V VFD inverter). The IGBT's fixed ~2 V drop beats a MOSFET's I²·Rds(on) there. For low voltage or high frequency, the MOSFET wins.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals — loss budget, gate drive, thermal chain, dead-time.

WE1 — Full Loss Budget of a Buck-Converter Switch
Buck 48 V → 12 V, Iout = 10 A, fsw = 100 kHz, D = 12/48 = 0.25.
FET: Rds(on) = 8 mΩ @ 25 °C (×1.5 hot → 12 mΩ), tr+tf = 40 ns.

Conduction: Pcond = I²·Rds(hot)·D = 10² × 0.012 × 0.25 = 0.30 W
Switching: Psw = ½·V·I·(tr+tf)·fsw = ½ × 48 × 10 × 40n × 100k = 0.96 W
Total ≈ 1.26 W — switching is 3× conduction even at a modest 100 kHz.

Discussion: the hot-resistance correction (×1.4–1.6 at Tj ≈ 100–125 °C) is the single most-forgotten step in student loss budgets — datasheet Rds(on) is a 25 °C number. Note also what happens if fsw rises to 500 kHz: Psw → 4.8 W while Pcond is unchanged; that is the entire argument for GaN (page 9) at high frequency.

WE2 — Gate Drive: Current, Time and Driver Power
FET: Qg(tot) = 60 nC at Vgs = 12 V, of which Qgd (Miller) = 25 nC.
Driver: 2 A peak source/sink.

Miller-plateau crossing time: t ≈ Qgd/Ig = 25n/2 = 12.5 ns — this sets the V-I crossover speed.
Total charge time: t ≈ Qg/Ig = 60n/2 = 30 ns.
Driver average power: P = Qg·Vgs·fsw = 60n × 12 × 100k = 72 mW (dissipated in Rg + driver, not the FET).

Discussion: gate power is tiny but gate current is what buys fast edges — the plateau is crossed at constant current, so halving Rg (or doubling driver current) halves switching time and thus Psw. The limit is ringing and dV/dt-induced turn-on (page 7): speed is traded against EMI and shoot-through immunity, which is why real designs use separate turn-on/turn-off gate resistors.

WE3 — The Thermal Chain: Will It Survive?
P = 12 W in a TO-247. RθJC = 0.9 K/W, insulating pad RθCS = 0.5 K/W, heatsink RθSA = 2.4 K/W. Ambient 45 °C.

Tj = Ta + P·(RθJC + RθCS + RθSA) = 45 + 12 × 3.8 = 45 + 45.6 = 90.6 °C ✓ (limit 150–175 °C)

Largest allowed heatsink for Tj ≤ 125 °C:  RθSA ≤ (125 − 45)/12 − 0.9 − 0.5 = 5.27 K/W

Discussion: the chain is series resistance arithmetic (→ A41 thermal imaging sees exactly these gradients). Note the design habit: solve backwards from the Tj you'll accept (with derating — running at 90 °C instead of 150 °C roughly squares the lifetime multiplier) to the heatsink you must buy. The pad's 0.5 K/W matters: a cheap dry mounting can add 1–2 K/W and silently eat the whole margin.

WE4 — Dead-Time & Body-Diode Loss in a Half-Bridge
Synchronous buck, I = 10 A, fsw = 100 kHz, dead-time td = 100 ns per edge (2 edges/cycle).
Body diode Vf = 0.8 V.

Pdt = Vf · I · (2·td) · fsw = 0.8 × 10 × 200n × 100k = 0.16 W
…plus reverse-recovery loss each time the diode is force-commutated: Prr = Qrr·Vbus·fsw = 150n × 48 × 100k = 0.72 W

Discussion: the recovery term dwarfs the conduction term — which is why bridges either add a parallel Schottky, choose FETs with fast body diodes, or move to SiC (Qrr ≈ 0). Dead-time itself is a two-sided tax: too little risks shoot-through, too much grows this loss and distorts inverter output (→ A08 VFD, A38 EV inverter). Adaptive dead-time control exists precisely to walk this line.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Attempt first — the reveal is the marking scheme.

Problems 1–10

P1: A FET with Rds(on) = 5 mΩ @25 °C runs at Tj = 125 °C (tempco ≈ +0.4%/°C). Compute the hot resistance and the conduction-loss increase.

Tap to reveal ↓A: ΔT = 100 °C → factor ≈ 1 + 0.004×100 = 1.4 (datasheets show ~1.4–1.6 from the normalized curve). Rhot ≈ 7 mΩ; conduction loss rises 40% at the same current. Always read the normalized Rds(on)–Tj curve, not just the 25 °C headline.

P2: Two identical FETs share 40 A in parallel. One runs 15 °C hotter. Explain quantitatively why the system is stable anyway.

Tap to reveal ↓A: The hotter FET's Rds(on) is ≈ 6% higher (0.4%/°C × 15), so its current share falls until heating equalizes — negative feedback via the positive tempco. Caveat: this balances conduction, not switching; dynamic sharing needs symmetric gate loops and layout.

P3: Estimate the dV/dt-induced gate bump: Cgd = 50 pF, Cgs = 1.2 nF, bus edge 20 V/ns, gate held low through Rg = 4.7 Ω. Does a 3 V-threshold FET false-turn-on?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Injected current ≈ Cgd·dV/dt = 50p × 20G = 1 A; bump ≈ I·Rg = 4.7 V > 3 V threshold → yes, it can conduct briefly (shoot-through stress). Fixes: lower Rg for turn-off path, negative gate rail, Miller-clamp driver pin, or slower edge. The capacitive divider Cgd/(Cgd+Cgs) alone gives 20 V×(50/1250) ≈ 0.8 V — the resistive path, not the divider, is the danger.

P4: A datasheet SOA shows a DC line at (100 V, 2 A) for Tc = 25 °C. Why can you NOT run 100 V·2 A continuously at Tc = 80 °C — and what's the derating method?

Tap to reveal ↓A: SOA curves are drawn for 25 °C case; the thermal-limit segments scale with available ΔT = Tj(max)−Tc. At 80 °C the allowed power falls by (150−80)/(150−25) ≈ 56% → ~1.1 A at 100 V. Some modern FETs also derate the DC line further for Spirito instability (hot-spotting in linear mode) — check the note under the SOA graph.

P5: Avalanche: a 60 V FET switches off 8 A through 50 µH of stray+load inductance with no clamp. Compute Eav and compare to a 15 mJ single-pulse rating.

Tap to reveal ↓A: E = ½LI² = 0.5 × 50µ × 64 = 1.6 mJ — inside the 15 mJ single-pulse rating, so a rare event survives. But repetitive avalanche at fsw would dump 1.6 mJ × fsw = 160 W at 100 kHz — instantly fatal. Rule: avalanche ratings buy fault tolerance, never a working clamp; add the snubber/TVS.

P6: Synchronous rectification: replacing a Schottky (Vf = 0.45 V) with a 4 mΩ FET at 20 A. Compute both losses and the crossover current where the diode wins.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Diode: 0.45 × 20 = 9 W. FET: 20² × 0.004 = 1.6 W — 5.6× better. Crossover where I·Vf = I²·R → I = Vf/R = 0.45/0.004 = 112 A; below that the FET always wins (at 4 mΩ). This linear-vs-quadratic contest is the whole case for synchronous rectification at low voltage.

P7: Bootstrap supply: high-side gate needs Qg = 80 nC per cycle plus 0.1 mA leakage; allowed droop 0.5 V; max ON-time 20 µs. Size the bootstrap capacitor.

Tap to reveal ↓A: ΔQ = Qg + I·t = 80n + 0.1m×20µ = 82 nC. C ≥ ΔQ/ΔV = 82n/0.5 = 164 nF → with ×5–10 practical margin, use ≈ 1 µF. Also check the 100% duty trap: the bootstrap only recharges when the low side conducts — a topology fact, not a capacitor fact (page 7).

P8: Why do designs use a small series gate resistor split into Ron (e.g. 10 Ω via diode) and Roff (e.g. 2 Ω)?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Slow turn-ON limits dV/dt, EMI and recovery stress on the opposing body diode; fast turn-OFF holds the gate hard low against Miller injection (P3) and shrinks turn-off loss. Asymmetric needs → asymmetric resistance, implemented with an anti-parallel diode across part of Rg.

P9: A 650 V SiC MOSFET replaces a 650 V Si IGBT in a 20 kHz, 400 V-bus welder inverter. Name the two loss mechanisms that shrink and the one drive requirement that tightens.

Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Switching loss: no IGBT tail current → per-cycle Esw drops several-fold; (2) low-load conduction: I²R beats the IGBT's ~1–2 V knee at partial current. Tightened: gate drive — SiC wants ~+15/−4 V with tight ringing control and strong Miller immunity; a recycled 0/+15 V Si driver gives marginal enhancement and dV/dt vulnerability.

P10: Design check: a motor-drive FET sees 8 A continuous, 35 A stall for 2 s, 48 V bus with 15% ringing overshoot. Choose minimum voltage class and justify the current rating you'd actually buy.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Voltage: 48 × 1.15 ≈ 55 V transient → with the standard 80% derating rule choose ≥ 80 V (60 V is one ring away from avalanche). Current: the "100 A" headline is a 25 °C silicon limit; buy for thermals — check the 2 s stall against the transient thermal-impedance curve Zθ(2 s) and the package's continuous rating at your real Tc, typically landing 3–5× the continuous current, e.g. a 40–60 A-class device with the heatsink from WE3.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Where the formulas come from — and the seven datasheet traps examiners love.

Derivation 1 — Switching Loss ½·V·I·t·f
During a linearized transition of duration ts, voltage falls V→0 while current rises 0→I (or vice-versa):
v(τ) = V(1 − τ/ts),   i(τ) = I·τ/ts

E = ∫₀t_s v·i dτ = V·I ∫₀t_s (τ/ts − τ²/ts²) dτ = V·I·ts·(½ − ⅓) = V·I·ts/6

Engineering practice keeps the cruder bound E ≈ ½·V·I·ts per edge (triangle overlap) —
conservative, and honest about non-linear real waveforms. Then Psw = Eon+off · fsw.

Exam point: know that the ⅙ comes from the double-ramp overlap integral and the ½ from the single-ramp triangle; quote whichever the question's waveform implies, and state the assumption. Hard-switching loss is overlap area × frequency — every soft-switching topology (ZVS/ZCS, → A28) is an attack on that overlap.

Derivation 2 — Square Law, and Where Rds(on) Comes From
Channel charge per area: Q = Cox(Vgs − Vth − v(x)); drift current: I = µ·Q·W·dv/dx.
Integrating x: 0→L, v: 0→Vds:

ID = µCox(W/L)·[(Vgs−Vth)Vds − Vds²/2]   (triode)
Saturation at Vds = Vgs−Vth:   ID = ½µCox(W/L)(Vgs−Vth)²   (square law)

Deep triode (Vds→0):   Rds(on) = 1/[µCox(W/L)(Vgs−Vth)] + Rdrift
In high-voltage FETs Rdrift dominates and scales ≈ VBR2.5 (silicon limit) — the reason SiC/GaN exist.

Exam point: the channel term explains why more Vgs lowers Rds(on) with diminishing returns, while the drift term explains why a 650 V Si FET can't match a 60 V FET's resistance no matter the gate drive. The VBR2.5 scaling ("silicon limit") versus SiC's 10× critical field is the one-line justification for wide-bandgap devices (page 9).

Derivation 3 — The Miller Plateau from Charge Balance
At turn-on, once ID reaches its final value, Vds must fall. Falling Vds pulls charge
through Cgd: iCgd = Cgd·dVds/dt. KCL at the gate node:

Ig = Cgs·dVgs/dt + Cgd·d(Vgs−Vds)/dt

If all of Ig is consumed by the Cgd term, dVgs/dt = 0 → Vgs plateaus at the level
just sustaining ID (from the square law), while   dVds/dt = −Ig/Cgd.

Plateau duration: t = Qgd/Ig — gate current sets slew rate; Cgd (Crss) is the enemy.

Exam point: the plateau isn't a mystery region — it's the gate driver funding the drain-voltage swing through the feedback capacitor. Everything practical follows: driver current ∝ speed, Crss minimization in device selection, Miller-clamp pins, and the dV/dt immunity problem of P3 (same capacitor, opposite direction).

  • 1Rds(on) at 25 °C. Multiply by the normalized-vs-Tj curve (~1.4–1.6× at 125 °C) before any loss claim.
  • 2Vgs(th) is a µA spec. A "2 V threshold" FET may need 8–10 V for full enhancement — read the ID–Vgs transfer curve, and match logic-level parts to 5 V/3.3 V drives.
  • 3The headline current is silicon-limited fantasy. "195 A" TO-220 parts are bond-wire and thermally limited to a fraction of that; size from P = I²R and the thermal chain (WE3).
  • 4Qg vs Ciss. Switching time budgets use charge (Qg, Qgd), not small-signal capacitance — Ciss varies wildly with voltage; Qg integrates that honestly.
  • 5SOA lines are 25 °C, single-pulse, and (for linear mode) Spirito-derated. Repetitive pulses use Zθ(t); linear-mode use needs the DC line, not the 10 ms line.
  • 6Body diode ≠ free Schottky. Its Vf is higher and Qrr can dominate bridge loss (WE4) — check trr class before trusting synchronous topologies at high frequency.
  • 7±20 V absolute gate rating means ±20 V including ringing. A 15 V drive with 40% overshoot is already dead; check with a probe at the pin, tip-and-barrel grounding.

Hands-On Ladder
  • 1Take any TO-220 FET datasheet and extract: Rds(on) hot, Qg/Qgd, SOA DC line at your bus voltage, Zθ at 2 s. Write the five numbers from memory afterwards.
  • 2Rebuild WE1's loss budget for your own bus voltage and frequency in a spreadsheet; add a fsw slider column and find where Psw = Pcond.
  • 3Simulate a gate-drive edge in SPICE (Rashid's book, material folder): watch the Miller plateau appear; halve Rg and measure the dV/dt change.
  • 4Breadboard a low-side switch (logic-level FET + 24 V lamp/solenoid + flyback diode, → A36's output-stage circuit); scope Vgs and Vds on a real edge.
  • 5Deliberately under-drive it (3.3 V on a standard FET) and thermal-image the result (→ A41) — feel "partially enhanced" as watts.
  • 6Build/simulate a half-bridge with adjustable dead-time; find the shoot-through edge with a current probe, then measure body-diode conduction as you over-lengthen it.
  • 7Do WE3 backwards on a real heatsink: measure its RθSA from a known power and two temperatures.
  • 8Compare an Si and (datasheet-level) SiC design for the same 3 kW inverter leg: loss table, driver spec, cost — write the one-page recommendation an employer would actually read.
