The One-Second Specification That Was Never Possible

AN APPAREL PLANT, KATUNAYAKE — ENERGY MONITORING ON ONE RS-485 LOOP

Forty-two three-phase energy meters and six VFDs, all Modbus RTU, all daisy-chained — on paper — onto a single serial port of a data-logging gateway at 9600 baud, 8 data bits, no parity, one stop bit. The tender said “one-second logging of all electrical parameters”. Nobody, on either side, did the arithmetic before signing.

The system was commissioned and it worked, in the sense that numbers appeared. Then the energy team tried to use it to catch the compressor's peak demand, and discovered that consecutive log rows were between fourteen and nineteen seconds apart, that six of the forty-eight devices were never in the same row twice, and that the total plant kWh from the system disagreed with the utility bill by 11 %.

Fault 1 — the specification was arithmetically impossible on day one. The gateway polled each device three times: a voltage/current block, a power block, an energy block, eight holding registers each. That is 48 × 3 = 144 transactions per cycle, and a Modbus RTU transaction is strictly serial — the bus is half-duplex, one conversation at a time, no exceptions.

character time t_c = (1 start + 8 data + 0 parity + 1 stop + 1 idle) / 9600 = 11 bits / 9600 bit/s = 1.1458 ms per byte FC03 request = 8 bytes → 8 × 1.1458 = 9.167 ms FC03 response = 3 + 2×8 + 2 = 21 bytes → 21 × 1.1458 = 24.06 ms slave turnaround (meter thinking) → measured 35 ms inter-frame silence 3.5 t_c → 4.01 ms ------------------ T_txn = 72.24 ms T_cycle = 144 × 72.24 ms = 10.40 s against a 1 s specification

And here is the part that decides how you fix it. Of that 10.40 s, the wire is carrying data for 4,176 characters × 11 bits = 45,936 bits, which at 9600 bit/s is 4.79 s — 46 % bus utilisation. The other 54 % is the bus sitting silent: 144 × 35 ms = 5.04 s of slave turnaround plus 0.58 s of mandatory silence. Turnaround is a property of the devices. No baud rate on earth reduces it.

Fault 2 — the energy totals were decoded wrong. The meter manual listed 40100 for “Active Energy Import, 32-bit, kWh”. The engineer typed 40100 into a gateway that expects protocol addresses, so the request went out for PDU address 40100 — a register 40,000 places past the one intended. On this particular meter that address existed (it was inside a large diagnostic block) and returned data, with a valid CRC, with no exception. Two of the six VFDs, configured the same way, returned exception 02 and were logged as “offline” for eight months. Where the address was right, the 32-bit values were still wrong: the meter transmits the high-order word first, the gateway's default was word-swapped, so a true reading of 123,456 kWh came through as 3,795,845,121.

true value 123456 = 0x0001E240 meter transmits (big-endian word order) reg[0]=0x0001 reg[1]=0xE240 → 0x0001E240 = 123,456 gateway assembles (word-swapped) reg[1],reg[0] → 0xE2400001 = 3,795,845,121 ratio = 30,747×. Not a plausible-looking error — which is the only reason anybody noticed. The dangerous word-order errors are the ones that land inside the expected range.

Fault 3 — the bus was wired as a star, and the timeout hid it. Forty-eight devices, but not a daisy chain: the panel had a terminal strip from which fifteen spurs radiated, the longest 22 m. No termination at either end, no failsafe bias, screen bonded to earth in every cabinet. The result was a 4 % CRC failure rate concentrated on the electrically distant devices. That alone would be survivable. What was not survivable was the gateway's response timeout, left at its factory default of 1000 ms with one retry — against transactions whose worst legitimate response takes 72 ms.

failed transactions per cycle = 4 % × 144 = 5.76 added time = 5.76 × 1000 ms retry timeout = +5.76 s observed cycle = 10.40 + 5.76 = 16.2 s (matches the 14–19 s log spacing: the error rate wanders with load) a timeout set to 4 × T_txn = 250 ms would have cost +1.44 s instead, and would have made the physical fault visible as a retry counter instead of invisible as “the system is a bit slow”.

Three faults, three layers: an application-layer budget nobody computed, a data-representation convention nobody agreed, and a physical layer built by an electrician who had never been told that RS-485 is a transmission line. Every one of them is standard, documented, and forty years old. All three are diagnosed in the pages that follow and repaired, with numbers, in the table on page 14.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Ignore the wiring and the decoding for a moment. Why was the one-second specification impossible from the day it was written?

B. A Modbus RTU transaction has two costs: a byte cost that scales as 1/baud, and a per-transaction cost — the slave's turnaround time plus the mandatory silent interval — that does not scale with baud at all. At 9600 the two happen to be roughly equal; above about 9,100 baud for a transaction of this shape, the fixed cost dominates and keeps growing as a fraction. Push the line to 115,200 and the cycle falls from 10.40 s only to 5.69 s, because 5.04 s of it is 144 devices thinking. This is the single most useful fact in the module: on Modbus you buy speed by removing transactions, not by adding baud.

A is the reflex answer and it is quantitatively wrong. Twelve times the baud rate bought a 1.8× improvement. The derivation on page 18 shows the hard floor: as baud → ∞, T_cycle → n × t_turnaround. Here that floor is 5.04 s, five times the specification.

C confuses two different limits. Cable length limits the maximum baud rate (roughly, baud × length ≤ 108) and it limits reliability, but signal propagation at ~5 ns/m contributes about 0.1 ms to a 1,200 m bus — utterly negligible next to a 35 ms turnaround. Page 8 separates these properly.

D is almost never true and is almost always blamed. Half of every Modbus cycle is the master doing nothing. If your master is CPU-bound on 144 transactions per second you have a software defect, not a capacity limit.

Four Tables, One Conversation at a Time

DEFINITION

Modbus is an application-layer request/response protocol in which a single client (“master”) addresses commands to numbered servers (“slaves”) and each command reads or writes ranges of numbered bits or 16-bit words in one of four abstract data tables. It defines a message and nothing else: no device model, no units, no data types beyond the 16-bit word, no discovery, no time, no quality, no security.

That last sentence is the whole design. Modbus is not a small protocol because its authors ran out of time; it is small because in 1979 the Modicon 984 needed to move register images between a programmable controller and a handful of drops on a serial line, and everything that was not that was left out. The specification — Modbus Application Protocol V1.1b3 plus Modbus over Serial Line V1.02, both free from the Modbus Organization — is about eighty pages, and you can read the whole of it in an evening. You should.

Three vocabulary points that trip up examiners and interviewers. First, the 2020 revisions of the specification use client/server for what everyone in the field still calls master/slave; both terms appear here, they mean the same thing. Second, “Modbus” on its own names the application protocol; RTU, ASCII and TCP are three different ways of wrapping the same request onto a wire. Third, RS-485 is not Modbus — it is the electrical standard (TIA/EIA-485-A) that Modbus RTU usually rides on, and it carries plenty of other protocols. Confusing a protocol problem with a copper problem, in either direction, is the commonest diagnostic failure on serial networks and page 12 is built around telling them apart.

WHY IT SURVIVED
  • 1
    Royalty-free and unambiguous. No consortium fee, no certification, no stack licence. A competent engineer can write a working RTU slave in about 300 lines of C.
  • 2
    Tiny footprint. It runs on an 8-bit microcontroller with 2 kB of flash, which is why it appears on temperature controllers, weighing indicators, gas analysers and inverters that could never host a real fieldbus stack.
  • 3
    Everything speaks it. Every PLC, every SCADA package, every gateway, every historian. It is the lowest common denominator, and a lowest common denominator that is genuinely universal is worth more than a better protocol that is not.
  • 4
    It is diagnosable with cheap tools. A 20 US$ USB–RS-485 dongle and free software will show you every byte on the bus. Try that with a proprietary fieldbus.
WHAT MODBUS IS NOT
  • 1
    Not deterministic. There is no guaranteed cycle time, no scheduling, no priority. Cycle time is whatever your budget arithmetic makes it (page 9). For hard real-time, see A03 and A37.
  • 2
    Not self-describing. Register 40031 is a number. Whether it is a temperature in tenths of a degree or a fault bitfield lives in a PDF, and when the PDF is lost the plant is undocumented. This is precisely the gap OPC UA exists to close.
  • 3
    Not secure — at all. No authentication, no integrity beyond an error-detecting CRC, no confidentiality. Anything that can reach the bus can write any coil. Page 13 and A54.
  • 4
    Not safe. Modbus carries no safety integrity. It must never be the sole path for an emergency stop or a protective function — see A52.
  • 5
    Not event-driven. A slave cannot report a change. Latency to detect an event equals your poll cycle, and that is a design input, not an accident.

ASCII exists because early modems and some radio links could not be trusted with arbitrary binary, and because a human with a terminal can read it. Its price is exactly double the airtime and a weaker error check — an 8-bit LRC catches far less than a 16-bit CRC (page 5). ⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A supplier offers to convert your 9600-baud RTU network to Modbus ASCII because “the noisy environment corrupts binary”. What actually happens to reliability?

C. Noise corrupts bits on the wire, and ASCII puts twice as many bits on the wire for the same payload. If the bit error rate is p and a frame is n bits, the probability the frame is hit is 1 − (1−p)n ≈ np for small p — so doubling n roughly doubles the corruption rate. Then, when corruption does occur, an 8-bit LRC leaves about 1 in 256 corrupted frames undetected against roughly 1 in 65,536 for CRC-16, and the LRC is a simple sum which is blind to whole classes of error including any two errors that cancel. You have made corruption twice as likely and detection 256 times worse.

D contains the one true thing in the offer. ASCII's explicit : … CRLF framing genuinely is more robust than RTU's timing-based framing when a device or a USB converter cannot honour inter-character gaps — and that, not noise, is the legitimate reason to pick ASCII. Page 7 explains why non-real-time operating systems and USB adapters break the 1.5T rule.

The real answer to a noisy environment is on page 8: proper twisted pair with the correct impedance, termination at exactly two points, failsafe bias, single-point screen bonding, segregation from VFD motor cables, and if the environment is genuinely hostile, fibre or a repeater with galvanic isolation. Changing the character encoding is fixing a copper problem in software.
A SHORT, USEFUL HISTORY

1979 — Modicon publishes Modbus for the 984 PLC family. 1990s — it becomes the de-facto serial protocol for third-party devices, largely because Modicon never charged for it. 1999 — Schneider publishes Modbus TCP; the transport changes, the PDU does not. 2004 — Schneider transfers the specification to the independent Modbus Organization, which still maintains it. 2018 — Modbus/TCP Security (TLS on port 802) is published, and largely ignored. 2020–present — the specification's terminology moves to client/server, and the protocol acquires an unexpected second life as the southbound interface of tens of thousands of IIoT gateways feeding MQTT and OPC UA northbound (pages 11 and 13).

The lesson worth carrying out of that timeline is not nostalgia. It is that a protocol's install base is a function of its cost of adoption, not its technical merit, and that as an engineer you will spend far more of your career making excellent use of an inadequate standard than you will spend choosing a better one.

Four Tables, Two Numbering Systems, One Permanent Confusion

THE DATA MODEL

Modbus defines exactly four tables. Two hold single bits, two hold 16-bit words; one of each pair is read-only, the other read/write. That is the entire type system. Everything else — floats, 32-bit counters, text, timestamps, enumerations — is a convention layered on top of consecutive 16-bit registers by the device manufacturer, and page 10 is entirely about surviving those conventions.

Crucially, the specification does not say whether these are four separate memory areas or four views of the same memory. Both implementations exist and both are legal. Some devices give you holding register 40001 and input register 30001 as physically different values; others map both onto the same word, so a value you can read two ways. Never assume; test it on the bench with a Modbus poller before you write the register map into a document. This is the first of many places where the specification's silence is the hazard, not its content.

ADDRESSING — THE 40001 PROBLEM CORE

On the wire, a Modbus PDU carries a 16-bit starting address, zero-based, from 0x0000 to 0xFFFF, and the function code selects the table. There is no “40001” anywhere in a Modbus frame. The five-digit numbers are a documentation convention inherited from Modicon's original PLC memory layout, in which the leading digit names the table and the remaining digits are a one-based index.

traditional reference PDU address (on the wire) 00001 – 09999 → 0x0000 – 0x270E (coils) 10001 – 19999 → 0x0000 – 0x270E (discrete inputs) 30001 – 39999 → 0x0000 – 0x270E (input registers) 40001 – 49999 → 0x0000 – 0x270E (holding registers) PDU address = (traditional reference mod 10000) − 1 Holding register 40100 → 100 − 1 = 99 = 0x0063 Holding register 40001 → 1 − 1 = 0 = 0x0000

Two further wrinkles make this worse than it looks. First, the 4xxxx form only reaches 9,999 registers, so devices with larger maps use a six-digit form: 440100, or 4x00100, or just “register 100”. Second — and this is the one that costs days — software tools disagree about which convention their address field expects. Some ask for the traditional reference and subtract for you. Some ask for the PDU address. Some ask for a one-based PDU address, which is a third convention that exists only to cause harm. A gateway, a SCADA driver and a handheld tester can each be right by their own documentation and disagree with each other by one.

The professional habit is simple and non-negotiable: every register you document gets both numbers and a test result. “Motor current, 4x 40007 = PDU 6 = 0x0006, FC03, 1 register, INT16, scale 0.1 A, verified 12.3 A on 2026-03-14 against a clamp meter.” Write that line and the next engineer — who will be you, in two years, at 23:00 — loses no time at all.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You request FC03, start 0x0063, quantity 2 from a meter and get a valid response containing 0x0000 0x0000 instead of the expected energy total. The CRC is good and there is no exception. What is the most likely cause?

C. You received a well-formed, CRC-valid response from the device you addressed, so the physical layer, the framing, the CRC and the slave address are all provably correct. The device believes it answered your question. Therefore the question was wrong: either an off-by-one or off-by-40001 in the address, or the right address in the wrong table. Reading holding register 99 when the value is at input register 99 gives exactly this symptom on a device that implements both tables with separate memory — a plausible, valid, useless answer.

A and B are eliminated by the good CRC. This is worth internalising as a diagnostic rule: a valid CRC removes the entire physical layer and the entire framing layer from suspicion for that frame. Engineers waste enormous amounts of time re-terminating cables to fix problems that the CRC has already proven are not in the cable.

D is eliminated by getting a reply at all — on RTU, only the addressed slave responds, and the response's address byte tells you who answered. Check it: on rare occasions two devices share an address, and then you have a genuinely entertaining fault (page 12).

The fastest confirmation is a sweep: read 20 registers either side of the suspect address, in both tables, and look for the value you expect. Page 12 gives the procedure.

GAPS, HOLES AND THE COST OF A SPARSE MAP

A single FC03 reads a contiguous range. If the four values you need sit at PDU 0, 40, 41 and 300, you have three choices: three transactions; one transaction of 301 registers (illegal — over 125); or two transactions covering 0–41 and 300. On a device that tolerates reads across undefined addresses, over-reading is nearly free — you pay 2 bytes per unwanted register, which at 9600 baud is 2.3 ms, against roughly 40 ms for an extra transaction. The break-even gap is g* = (13·t_c + t_TA + t_gap) / (2·t_c), which at 9,600 baud with a 35 ms turnaround is 23.5 registers — and rises to 70 at 38,400 and 199 at 115,200. The faster the line, the more it pays to over-read. Derived on page 18 and applied in WE2.

The trap is that some devices raise exception 02 for a range that includes an unimplemented address, even if both endpoints are valid. There is no way to know but to try. Test it, record the answer in the device profile, and never let a colleague discover it during a night shift.

The Frame, Byte by Byte

PDU AND ADU

The PDU (Protocol Data Unit) is one function-code byte plus up to 252 data bytes. It is identical on serial and on Ethernet, and it is the only part of Modbus that is actually the Modbus protocol. The ADU (Application Data Unit) is the PDU plus whatever the transport needs to deliver and validate it — on RTU that is an address byte in front and a CRC behind; on TCP it is a six-byte MBAP header and nothing behind.

RTU ADU = 1 (address) + 253 (PDU) + 2 (CRC) = 256 bytes maximum → PDU max = 256 − 1 − 2 = 253 bytes → data max = 253 − 1 (function code) = 252 bytes TCP ADU = 7 (MBAP incl. unit id) + 253 (PDU) = 260 bytes maximum

The 256-byte figure is the historical constraint that generates every quantity limit in the protocol. It originates in the buffer size of the original Modicon implementation, and it has propagated, unchanged, into forty-six years of devices. When you see “125 registers” you are looking at a 1979 memory decision.

Byte order on the wire is big-endian throughout the protocol itself. Addresses, quantities, byte counts and each individual register are transmitted most-significant byte first. This is fixed and unambiguous, and it is important to state clearly because it is not the thing people mean when they complain about Modbus endianness. The protocol's byte order within a register is defined; what is undefined is the order of registers when a manufacturer spreads a 32-bit value across two of them. That is page 10, and it is a documentation problem, not a protocol problem.

READING A FRAME OFF A SCOPE OR A SNIFFER CORE

This is a skill, it takes about twenty minutes to acquire, and it will separate you permanently from engineers who can only read a driver's status field. Given a capture like 01 03 00 6B 00 03 74 17, decode left to right:

01 slave address 1 03 function 03, read holding registers 00 6B starting PDU address 0x006B = 107 → traditional 40108 00 03 quantity = 3 registers 74 17 CRC, low byte first → CRC value 0x1774 (verify it with the calculator on page 5) expected response length = 1 + 1 + 1 + 2×3 + 2 = 11 bytes an exception response would be 01 83 xx crc crc = 5 bytes

Three habits make this fast. Count the bytes before you decode them — length alone identifies the function class and immediately reveals a truncated frame. Read the second byte first: if its top bit is set (0x83, 0x84, 0x90…) it is an exception and the third byte is the reason, which you will know by heart after page 6. And always check the CRC by machine, never by eye; a plausible-looking CRC on a corrupted frame is exactly the failure mode the CRC exists to catch.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You capture 01 03 14 … as the start of a response and expected 10 registers. Is this response the right size?

B. The byte-count field in a register-read response counts bytes of data, not registers, so ten registers give 0x14 = 20. The full ADU is address (1) + function (1) + byte count (1) + data (20) + CRC (2) = 25 bytes. Being able to predict that number instantly is what lets you spot a truncated frame the moment it appears on a sniffer.

A confuses the two units, which is the most frequent slip when people first write their own parsers — and the resulting bug is nasty, because reading 10 bytes instead of 20 leaves half a response in the buffer that then desynchronises everything after it.

C is a real thing, but in the wrong place. Byte counts are one byte because the maximum is 250 and fits; quantities in requests are two bytes. Bit-read responses (FC01/02) use ceil(bits/8) for the same field, so 10 coils give a byte count of 2 — a different arithmetic that catches people out separately.

D confuses validity with structure. The CRC tells you whether the bytes are intact; the byte count tells you what they mean. You need both, and they answer different questions.

CRC-16: Sixteen Bits Between You and a Wrong Number

WHAT A CRC ACTUALLY IS STRETCH

A cyclic redundancy check treats the message as the coefficients of a polynomial over GF(2) — arithmetic where addition is XOR and there are no carries — and transmits the remainder after dividing by a fixed generator polynomial. The receiver repeats the division including the remainder; a correct message divides exactly, leaving zero.

Modbus generator (normal form) x¹⁶ + x¹⁵ + x² + 1 = 0x8005 Modbus uses the reflected form = 0xA001 initial value = 0xFFFF final XOR = 0x0000 (none) bit order = reflected in and out transmission = low byte first

Why reflected? Because UARTs transmit the least significant bit of each byte first. Processing bits in the order they physically arrive lets an implementation shift right and avoids reversing every byte, which mattered enormously on a 1979 microprocessor and matters not at all today — but the format is frozen. The consequence for you is that the Modbus CRC is not the same as CRC-16/ARC, CRC-16/CCITT, or the CRC in a Xmodem library, and pasting the wrong one in produces a device that appears completely dead.

The generator 0x8005 is the ANSI/IBM “CRC-16” polynomial. It factorises as (x + 1)(x15 + x + 1), and that factor of (x + 1) is what guarantees detection of all odd numbers of bit errors: any polynomial with an odd number of terms is not divisible by (x + 1). The remaining factor is a primitive polynomial of degree 15, which gives the code a Hamming distance of 4 for messages up to 32,767 bits — comfortably longer than any Modbus frame.

undetected corrupt frames per day ≈ 500,000 / 65,536 = 7.6 per day — seven or eight times a day, a completely wrong value entered the energy database with a perfect CRC and no error flag. Restore the bus to a 0.05 % error rate and the same arithmetic gives 0.095 per day, i.e. about one every eleven days — and at that point the retry counter, not the CRC, is your instrument.

That is the honest engineering statement: a CRC is an error-detection mechanism whose residual rate scales with your error rate. It is not a substitute for a bus that works. This argument — that error detection buys time, not correctness — is exactly why CAN pairs a 15-bit CRC with mandatory automatic retransmission and error counters, and why safety protocols add a second, independent CRC on top of the fieldbus (A52).

IMPLEMENTATION NOTES RESEARCH

The bitwise loop above costs eight iterations per byte. The classical optimisation is a 256-entry lookup table of 16-bit values, computed once, which reduces the per-byte cost to one table index, one XOR and one shift — roughly eight times faster, at a cost of 512 bytes of ROM. On an 8-bit microcontroller with 2 kB of flash that trade is not obvious, which is why a 16-entry nibble table (two lookups per byte, 32 bytes of ROM) is a common middle path in real slave firmware.

bitwise : 8 shifts + up to 8 XOR per byte ~50 cycles/byte on AVR nibble tbl : 2 lookups + 2 XOR + 2 shifts ~18 cycles/byte, 32 B ROM byte table : 1 lookup + 1 XOR + 1 shift ~8 cycles/byte, 512 B ROM At 115,200 baud a byte arrives every 86.8 µs. On a 16 MHz AVR that is 1,389 cycles — so even the bitwise version uses under 4 % of the budget. CRC computation is almost never the bottleneck. Measure before optimising.

One genuine subtlety: many slave implementations compute the CRC incrementally as bytes arrive, so that when the 3.5-character silence declares the frame complete the CRC is already finished. This is the correct architecture for a small device, because it removes any need to buffer and re-scan, and it makes the whole receive path O(1) in memory beyond the frame buffer itself.

A last trick worth knowing for testing: if you run the CRC over the entire received frame including its two CRC bytes, a valid frame yields 0x0000. That is one comparison instead of two, and it is the standard check inside real firmware.

THE LRC, FOR COMPLETENESS

Modbus ASCII uses a Longitudinal Redundancy Check: sum all the message bytes (not the ASCII characters) modulo 256, take the two's complement, transmit as two hex characters. It is four lines of code and it detects any single-byte error and any odd number of bit errors within a byte column — but it is blind to any set of errors that sums to zero, including the very common case of two bytes swapped, and it leaves 1 in 256 corrupted messages undetected. It exists because it can be computed by hand. Treat its presence in a specification as a signal that the specification is old.

A VFD returns exception 06 (Server Device Busy) to roughly one write in twenty. Your master's retry logic re-sends immediately, up to three times. What is the most likely consequence?

B. Exception 06 means “I am doing something long, ask me later”. An immediate retry arrives while the device is still doing that long thing, so it must be parsed and refused — consuming the very CPU the device needed to finish. Three immediate retries turn one refused write into four transactions, each costing a full turnaround out of your cycle, and on a device with a small command queue they can extend the busy state rather than wait it out. The correct handling is an exponential or at minimum a fixed back-off: retry after 100 ms, then 200, then 400, and count the events so the pattern becomes visible.

A is what the retry counter suggests and it hides the real cost. “It usually succeeds” is a statement about eventual correctness that says nothing about the 5 % of cycles that just grew by three transactions — which is precisely how a scan budget silently degrades over months as more devices are added.

C confuses two independent mechanisms. An exception is a perfectly formed, CRC-valid frame. Exceptions and CRC errors come from different layers and should be counted separately — a diagnostic discipline set out on page 12.

EXCEPTION vs SILENCE — A DIAGNOSTIC FORK

There are exactly three things that can happen after you transmit a request, and each points to a different half of the system.

  • 1
    A normal response. Everything below the application layer is proven good.
  • 2

  • 3

Framed by Silence: the 3.5T Rule

HOW AN RTU RECEIVER KNOWS WHERE A FRAME ENDS CORE

Modbus RTU has no start byte, no end byte and no length field. A frame is delimited entirely by time: a gap of at least 3.5 character times means the previous frame is complete, and a gap of more than 1.5 character times inside a frame means the frame is broken and must be discarded whole. This is elegant, it costs zero bytes, and it is the source of an entire family of faults that look like nothing else.

character time t_c = (bits per character) / baud 8N1 = 1 start + 8 data + 1 stop = 10 bits 8E1 / 8O1 = 1 + 8 + 1 parity + 1 stop = 11 bits 8N2 = 1 + 8 + 2 stop = 11 bits The Modbus serial specification defines the character as 11 bits and requires 8E1 as the default; 8N1 with 11 bits assumed is the near-universal practice, and it errs on the safe side. at 9600 baud: t_c = 11/9600 = 1.1458 ms 3.5 t_c = 4.01 ms 1.5 t_c = 1.72 ms

Above 19,200 baud the specification stops scaling the intervals and fixes them at 1.750 ms for the 3.5-character delimiter and 750 µs for the 1.5-character limit. The reason is practical: at 115,200 baud, 3.5 characters is 334 µs, and hardware and software that must detect a 334 µs gap reliably was, in 2002, expensive. Fixing a floor made high-speed Modbus implementable on ordinary UARTs. The cost is that at 115,200 baud your inter-frame overhead is five times longer than the standard would otherwise require — 1.75 ms against 334 µs — and page 9 will show that this fixed floor becomes a visible fraction of a fast bus's cycle.

Notice also what the 3.5T rule does not do. It does not protect against a slave that starts talking too early; it does not detect a lost final byte if the CRC happens to pass on the truncated frame (impossible in practice but not in principle); and it provides no way for a listener that joins mid-stream to know whether it is looking at the middle of a frame or the start of one. A sniffer solves that by waiting for a gap. Your code must too.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A bus runs perfectly with a hardware master. You replace it with a Python script on a laptop through a USB–RS-485 dongle and now roughly one response in ten is missed — but a sniffer shows the slaves replying correctly every time. What is happening?

C. The sniffer sees the wire; your master sees a USB endpoint. A full-speed USB serial adapter polls at 1 ms intervals and typically holds bytes until a latency timer — commonly 16 ms by default on FTDI parts — expires or the buffer fills. Add the operating system's scheduling jitter and a response that was transmitted as a clean unbroken 24 ms burst can be delivered to your code as two chunks separated by 16 ms. A master that enforces the 1.5T rule on received data will declare that frame invalid, and it will be right by the letter of the specification and wrong about the world.

The three real fixes, in order: reduce the adapter's latency timer (1–2 ms; on Windows it is a driver property, on Linux it is /sys/bus/usb-serial/devices/…/latency_timer); use a library that frames on silence plus expected length rather than strict 1.5T, which is what every practical library including pymodbus and libmodbus actually does; or move the master onto a device with a real UART — a Raspberry Pi's on-board UART or an industrial gateway (A36).

A is the expensive guess and buying a second dongle will reproduce the fault exactly. B is nearly always wrong — the work per frame is microseconds of parsing. D would show as CRC errors or as garbage on the sniffer, and the sniffer is clean.

This is also the legitimate case for Modbus ASCII mentioned on page 2: its explicit :…CRLF framing is immune to gap jitter entirely.

RS-485 Is a Transmission Line, Not a Pair of Wires

THE ELECTRICAL STANDARD CORE

TIA/EIA-485-A specifies a differential, multipoint, half-duplex line. A driver puts a voltage difference between two conductors, A and B; a receiver decides the bit from the difference, not from either wire's voltage to ground. Any interference that couples equally into both conductors — which, on a twisted pair, is nearly all of it — cancels at the receiver. That cancellation is the entire reason RS-485 works next to a VFD and RS-232 does not.

FAILSAFE BIASING

When no driver is enabled — which on a half-duplex Modbus bus is most of the time — the line floats. A floating line drifts to whatever noise is nearby, and a receiver seeing less than its 200 mV threshold produces random output that looks like start bits. The symptom is framing errors and garbage characters between valid frames.

The cure is a pull-up on A and a pull-down on B, sized to hold at least 200 mV (in practice 250–300 mV) across the terminated line. Fit it once, at one point, usually at the master. Many transceivers now include internal failsafe; if yours do, external bias is unnecessary and can even reduce noise margin.

Everything in this module converges here. A Modbus RTU master is a strictly sequential machine: it may have exactly one transaction outstanding on a segment, so the cycle time is the plain sum of the transactions, and every transaction carries a fixed overhead that baud rate cannot reduce.

A working 20-device bus at 19,200 baud has a 1.2 s cycle. You add one device whose turnaround is 250 ms and poll it twice per cycle like the rest. What happens to the cycle?

B. Cycle time is a sum over transactions, weighted by each device's own turnaround — not an average over devices. This is the classic mechanism by which a Modbus network that worked for years becomes unusable after a small addition, and why the maintenance answer “we only added one sensor” is not a defence.

JITTER, AND THE HONEST ANSWER TO “IS IT REAL TIME?”

A Modbus cycle is not periodic. Its duration varies with error rate, with which devices happen to be busy, with the master's own housekeeping, and with any writes injected on demand. In a plant like the cold open, cycles ranged from 14 s to 19 s — a ±15 % jitter that made the logged timestamps almost useless for computing a demand profile.

Three consequences follow, and you should state all three in any specification you write:

  • 1
    The age of a value is up to one full cycle. Not half, not the average: the worst case. Any control decision or alarm built on polled data inherits that latency, and the operator interface must be able to show it (A14).
  • 2
    Two values in the same record were not measured at the same instant. Computing instantaneous power from a voltage and a current polled 4 s apart is arithmetic without physics. If simultaneity matters, read them in one transaction, or use a device that computes the derived value internally.
  • 3
    Modbus timestamps the arrival, not the measurement. There is no time in the protocol. Every timestamp in your historian is a master-side clock reading, offset from reality by an unknown fraction of a cycle. For sequence-of-events work you need a device that timestamps internally, or a different protocol entirely — IEC 60870-5 or IEC 61850 (A14).

Thirty-Two Bits in a Sixteen-Bit World

WHY THIS PROBLEM EXISTS AT ALL

Modbus defines the byte order within a register — big-endian, high byte first — and says nothing whatsoever about how to combine registers. The moment a manufacturer needed a 32-bit counter or a float, they picked an order, and they all picked differently. This is not ambiguity in the standard; it is the absence of any standard at all.

A float usually announces a swap by producing a denormal or an astronomical number; a 32-bit counter with both words populated usually does not, because the swapped value is another plausible reading. The four permutations get called ABCD / CDAB / BADC / DCBA, or big-endian / little-endian / big-endian byte-swap / little-endian byte-swap, or “normal / swapped / swapped bytes / swapped both”, depending on whose software you are using. Learn the four-letter form; it is unambiguous and it maps directly onto what you type into a gateway. Extend it to 64-bit values (eight letters, eight permutations that anyone actually uses being far fewer) and you have covered every case you will meet.

A related trap: bit numbering inside a register. Some manufacturers number bit 0 as the least significant, others as the most significant, and some number from 1. A status word documented as “bit 3 = overload” may mean the value 8 or the value 4096. There is only one defence, and it is the same one as for everything else on this page: force the bit and observe.

A flow meter's 32-bit totaliser is read once per second. Occasionally the logged value jumps backwards by a large amount and then recovers on the next reading. The bus has no CRC errors. What is happening?

C. Modbus guarantees that a multi-register read returns the registers you asked for; it guarantees nothing about their mutual consistency in time. A device that updates its totaliser asynchronously can increment the low word from 0xFFFF to 0x0000 and carry into the high word between the two words being copied into the response buffer, and you receive a value that never existed. On a 32-bit counter incrementing at a few counts per second the event is rare — roughly once per 65,536 counts — which is exactly why it survives commissioning and then confuses somebody a year later.

Three defences, in order of preference. Read the value twice and accept it only if unchanged or plausibly incremented — cheap and effective. Prefer a device that snapshots its registers on the first byte of a read, which good instruments document and poor ones do not. Or reconstruct from the low word alone and count rollovers in the master, which is what a well-written driver does with a counter it polls faster than the rollover period.

B produces a constant error, not an intermittent one — a useful discriminator. A is the conclusion reached in the field, generally after replacing the meter and reproducing the fault. D is essentially ruled out by rate: at a clean bus's residual rate you would see this once in decades, not once a week.

The general principle is worth carrying to every protocol: atomicity of a multi-word read is a property of the device, never of the bus.

DESIGNING A REGISTER MAP THAT WILL STILL MAKE SENSE IN TEN YEARS STRETCH
  • 1
    Group by poll rate, then by function. The map's physical layout should match the way it will be read. Fast-changing values contiguous, slow values contiguous, configuration elsewhere. This single decision is worth a factor of two or three in scan time and costs nothing at design time.
  • 2
    Leave gaps between blocks, not inside them. Reserve 16 registers at the end of each block for the next three years of additions. Inserting a register in the middle of a shipped map breaks every installation in the field.
  • 3
    Never move a register. Ever. Deprecate, do not relocate. This is the same discipline as versioning an API, and Modbus register maps are APIs with a twenty-year life and no version negotiation.
  • 4
    Put an identity block at a fixed address — device type, firmware version, map version — ideally at PDU 0. A master that can read the map version can adapt; one that cannot, cannot.
  • 5
    Separate read-only measurements from writable setpoints, by using input registers for the former where possible, and at minimum by address range. Then a gateway can be configured read-only for a whole range, which is the cheapest security control available (page 13).
  • 6
    Document units, scale, type, order and range for every register, in a machine-readable file. A CSV is enough. The map you can import into your gateway is worth ten times the PDF that must be retyped.

Everything on that list is an attempt to recover, by discipline, some of what a self-describing protocol gives you for free. That is the honest comparison to make with OPC UA: not that Modbus is bad, but that its information model lives in your documentation instead of in the device, and documentation is a thing organisations lose.

The Gateway: Where Three Clients Queue for One Serial Line

MODBUS TCP IN ONE PARAGRAPH CORE

Take the PDU, drop the address byte and the CRC, prepend a six-byte MBAP header, and send it over a TCP connection to port 502. That is the whole of Modbus TCP. TCP already provides ordering, retransmission and a checksum, so the CRC is redundant and was removed; the Length field replaces timing-based framing; and the Transaction ID permits many requests in flight at once — the single genuine capability that serial Modbus does not have.

Three practical points that separate a working TCP deployment from a fragile one. First, keep the connection open: opening a TCP connection per poll costs a three-way handshake, and a device with a four-connection limit will refuse the fifth while three sit in TIME_WAIT. Second, match the Transaction ID on the response — if you assume responses arrive in order you will eventually mis-attribute one, and the bug will be intermittent and awful. Third, set TCP keepalive or an application-level poll, because a silently dropped connection on a device with no keepalive can leave your client waiting indefinitely for a reply that will never come; this is a genuine difference from serial, where a timeout always fires.

THE SERIALISATION TRAP

Nearly every “Modbus TCP” installation in a factory is actually Modbus RTU behind a gateway. And the gateway is where the two worlds' assumptions collide: the TCP side can accept a dozen concurrent requests, and the serial side can perform exactly one transaction at a time. The gateway's queue is the only thing between them, and it is typically eight deep.

three clients, each polling 16 devices at their own rate, one shared RS-485 segment, 72 ms per transaction: offered load = 16×1 Hz + 16×0.2 Hz + 16×0.5 Hz = 27.2 transactions/s serial capacity = 1 / 0.072 s = 13.9 transactions/s offered load is 1.96× capacity → the queue fills, then the gateway either drops requests or returns exception 0B, and every client blames the network. Worse: the three clients are unsynchronised, so their requests ARRIVE in bursts. Even at 60 % average utilisation, a burst of ten simultaneous requests puts the tenth 720 ms behind — longer than most clients' timeouts.

The structural fix is the one drawn in the illustration: exactly one entity owns each serial segment. That poller reads on a schedule it controls, caches, and serves everyone else — from its own cache over Modbus TCP, or northbound over MQTT/Sparkplug or OPC UA. Every modern edge gateway does this, and it is the single most valuable thing they do. Note the trade: the cached value carries an age, and any client that treats it as live is deceived. A well-built gateway exposes the acquisition timestamp alongside the value; if yours cannot, publish the poll cycle time next to it and make the age visible on the HMI (A14).

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You replace a 9,600-baud serial link between a PLC and a remote panel of 12 meters with a Modbus TCP gateway on a gigabit fibre link. The meters are unchanged. What happens to the poll cycle?

B. The gateway converts the transport of the last few metres between the PLC and the gateway. Behind it, the same twelve devices sit on the same RS-485 segment at the same baud rate with the same turnaround, and the cycle is the same sum it always was. What the fibre genuinely bought you is distance, galvanic isolation, immunity, and the ability to have more than one client — all real and often worth the money — but not throughput.

This matters because the upgrade is regularly sold, and bought, as a performance improvement. The only way a gateway improves cycle time is by having several serial ports and splitting the devices across them, which converts a sequential sum into a parallel maximum. A four-port gateway with three meters per port turns 12 × T into 3 × T — a real 4×, for a few thousand rupees, and it is the correct thing to specify.

C is wrong in magnitude but right in spirit for one case: if you now point three clients at the gateway, the offered load can exceed the serial capacity and the effective cycle genuinely does get worse. A is the belief being sold. Recognising it is one of the more commercially useful skills in this module.

MODBUS RTU OVER TCP — THE THIRD THING RESEARCH

You will meet a variant that sends the full RTU frame — address, PDU and CRC — inside a TCP stream, with no MBAP header. It is not in any specification. It exists because it is the laziest possible way to tunnel a serial port over Ethernet, and it is what most cheap “serial device servers” do.

It works, mostly, and it has two real defects. There is no Length field, so the receiver must guess frame boundaries from timing — over TCP, which explicitly does not preserve timing — or from the expected response length. And there is no Transaction ID, so concurrency is impossible.

If you meet it: it is usually on port 502 as well, so a client configured for Modbus TCP will connect happily and then fail to parse anything. Recognising the symptom — connection succeeds, every response is rejected — and switching the client to “RTU over TCP” mode saves an afternoon.

THE FOUR INSTRUMENTS, AND WHAT EACH IS FOR
🔌 USB–RS-485 dongle + polling software

Under 20 US$. Lets you talk to one device in isolation, sweep an address range, and confirm a register map without the plant's master involved. Buy two — one to poll, one to listen. This is the highest-value tool in the list by an enormous margin.

📈 Two-channel oscilloscope

The only instrument that shows you the waveform: reflections from stubs, the differential amplitude, the idle-state voltage that tells you whether bias is present, and the turnaround gap. Set it to trigger on A–B and look at the shape of the edges, not just the bits.

🔍 Protocol analyser / passive tap

A dongle in listen-only mode with software that decodes frames, timestamps them and counts CRC errors per address. This is what turns “it is unreliable” into “slaves 31 to 38 have a 6 % error rate and the rest have none” — which is a repair instruction, not a complaint.

📏 A multimeter, used correctly

With the bus idle: A–B should read a few hundred millivolts if bias is present, near zero if not. A–common and B–common should be within the common-mode range. Resistance across A–B with everything powered down should be about 60 Ω for two terminators — 120 Ω means one, 40 Ω means three, and open means none. That one measurement finds a large fraction of all termination faults in thirty seconds.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A bus works all night and fails every morning between 07:40 and 08:10, then recovers. CRC errors spike during the failure. What should you investigate first?

B. A fault with a reproducible time signature is a gift, and the correct first move costs nothing: find out what else happens in that window. A morning start-up is a burst of large motors, contactors and inverters energising, which is precisely when conducted and radiated interference peaks and when the earth reference between panels moves the most. Thirty minutes with the production log will usually name the culprit, and then the hardware investigation is targeted instead of exploratory.

A is where most people start, and the cable is genuinely part of the story — but as the susceptible element, not the cause. You will re-route or re-screen it eventually; you want to know what you are protecting against first, because the fix differs. Coupling from a nearby VFD motor cable is solved by separation and screening; a ground-potential shift between panels is solved by isolation and is made worse by bonding the screen at both ends.

C misreads “time-related”. Software faults with a clock signature exist — a nightly database job, a daylight-saving edge — but they do not raise the CRC error rate, because CRC errors happen on the wire before any software sees them.

D treats the messenger. The devices showing errors are the ones with the worst signal margin, which tells you where on the bus the margin is thin — useful information, thrown away by replacing them.
RETRIES, TIMEOUTS AND THE ARITHMETIC OF GIVING UP STRETCH

Three parameters govern how a master handles trouble, and all three are usually left at a default chosen by someone who had never seen your plant.

Response timeout t_to correct value = 3 to 4 × the worst measured transaction time for the cold-open plant: 4 × 72 ms ≈ 300 ms, not 1000 ms too short → you abandon slow-but-healthy devices too long → every failure costs a fortune in cycle time Retry count k 2 is almost always right. The purpose of a retry is to ride out a transient; a device that fails 3 times in a row has a real fault. worst-case cost of a dead device per cycle = (k+1) × t_to with k = 3, t_to = 1 s → 4 s per cycle, for one dead device Dead-device back-off — the parameter that is usually absent after k failures, stop polling that device for T_off, then probe once. With T_off = 60 s, a dead device costs (k+1)·t_to once a minute instead of once a cycle: at a 5 s cycle and 4 s of timeout: 80 % of the bus recovered

That last mechanism is the difference between a network that degrades gracefully and one that collapses. A plant with three failed meters and no back-off spends most of its cycle waiting for devices that are physically absent, so the healthy devices update slowly, and the operator concludes the whole system is broken. Implement back-off, expose the “devices currently in back-off” count on the HMI, and the failure becomes visible, bounded and actionable.

MULTI-MASTER: DON'T, AND IF YOU MUST… RESEARCH

Modbus RTU has no arbitration. There is no CSMA, no token, no collision detection — nothing corresponding to CAN's bitwise arbitration (A04). Two masters on one segment will eventually transmit simultaneously, producing garbage that both interpret as a bus error, and the failure rate rises with load in a way that looks exactly like a cabling fault.

People do it anyway, usually because a portable diagnostic tool gets plugged into a live bus. It works because the second master is used briefly and the errors are absorbed by retries. Know that this is what you are doing, do it deliberately and briefly, and never leave a second master permanently connected. Where two systems genuinely need the data, the answer is the gateway pattern from page 11: one owner, everyone else reads the cache.

There Is No Security. Design Around That.

THE THREAT MODEL, STATED PLAINLY

Modbus has no authentication, no authorisation, no integrity protection against a deliberate attacker, no encryption and no logging. Any entity that can put frames on the bus, or open a TCP connection to port 502, has complete read and write control of every device. There is no configuration that changes this, because there is nothing in the protocol to configure.

Say the operational consequence out loud, because it focuses the mind: a single well-formed 8-byte frame can write a coil that starts a motor, or write a register that changes a drive's speed reference, or a setpoint, or a protection threshold. The CRC is an error-detecting code — it detects accidents, and an attacker computes it correctly in one line of Python. Treating a CRC as a security control is a category error, and one that appears in real risk assessments.

This is not a criticism of a 1979 protocol designed for a cabinet. It becomes a criticism the moment that cabinet is connected, through a gateway, through a plant LAN, to anything with a route to the internet — which is the standard architecture of every IIoT retrofit being sold today. Internet-wide scans routinely find tens of thousands of devices answering on port 502 with no protection at all, and a meaningful number of them are in South Asia.

MODBUS/TCP SECURITY (2018)

The Modbus Organization published a genuine solution: Modbus over TLS on port 802, with X.509 certificates for mutual authentication and an optional role-based authorisation extension carried in the client certificate. It is technically sound and it solves the problem properly.

Adoption is negligible. Almost no field device supports it, because the devices Modbus runs on are the ones that cannot host a TLS stack — the same 2 kB microcontrollers that made the protocol universal. Where you can use it, it is between gateways and servers, not to end devices, and at that point OPC UA with its own security model is usually the better-supported choice.

Know it exists, ask for it in tenders so that suppliers start to feel demand, and design as though you will not get it.

THE SAFETY BOUNDARY — NON-NEGOTIABLE

Modbus carries no safety integrity and must never implement a protective function. Not an emergency stop, not a guard interlock, not an overpressure trip, not a light-curtain response. There is no black-channel safety layer for Modbus as there is for PROFIsafe or FSoE (A03, A52, A35).

Modbus may report the state of a safety system — that is monitoring, and it is useful. It may not be one. The distinction is between a signal an operator looks at and a signal that stops a machine, and if you are ever asked to blur it, the answer is a written refusal with the standard cited. That refusal is part of the job.

Recap, Failure Modes and Quiz

THE EIGHT IDEAS WORTH KEEPING
  • 1
    Cycle time is a sum of transactions, and every transaction has a fixed cost. T ≈ B·t_c + n·(t_TA + t_gap). You buy speed by removing transactions; baud rate has a floor it cannot cross.
  • 2
    Turnaround time is the number that decides your design, it varies by two orders of magnitude between devices, and it appears in no datasheet. Measure it.
  • 3
    A valid CRC clears the physical layer and the framing for that frame; an exception clears everything below the application layer. Both facts eliminate whole categories of suspicion instantly.
  • 4
    The address is 16-bit, zero-based, on the wire. 40001 is documentation. Write both numbers, every time.
  • 5
    Modbus defines byte order inside a register and nothing about combining them. Four word orders exist, all legal; scaled 16-bit integers avoid the whole problem.
  • 6
    RS-485 is a transmission line. Chain, two terminators, one bias point, stubs under 0.3 m, one screen bond, a signal common, isolation where earths differ.
  • 7
    A gateway does not make a serial bus faster — only more ports do. And concurrency arriving from Ethernet must be serialised by something; make it one owner with a cache.
  • 8
    There is no security in the protocol. Segment it, keep 502 inside, make the gateway read-only, and never let Modbus carry a safety function.
SIX FAILURE MODES — DETECT AND PREVENT
1 · The impossible specification

An update rate is promised that the transaction count and turnaround make unreachable, and nobody computes it until commissioning. Detect: compare measured cycle against n·t_TA — if you are near that floor, the design is wrong, not the tuning. Prevent: a submitted scan budget with 30 % margin, before installation.

2 · The default timeout

A 1,000 ms timeout against a 72 ms transaction turns a 4 % error rate into 36 % of the cycle, and hides the physical fault behind “a bit slow”. Detect: cycle time varying with error rate. Prevent: t_to = 3–4× worst transaction; retries 2; dead-device back-off.

3 · The star topology

Spurs off a terminal strip reflect, and errors cluster on the electrically distant devices. Detect: per-device FC08 error counters — a gradient along the bus is diagnostic. Prevent: daisy chain, stubs < 0.3 m, a repeater where the building forces a star.

4 · The word-order error that looks plausible

A swapped 32-bit float lands inside the expected range and is believed for months. Detect: reconcile against an independent measurement — a clamp meter, a utility bill, a calibrated gauge. Prevent: verify each 32-bit point against a known non-symmetric value at commissioning; prefer scaled integers.

5 · The non-atomic totaliser

A counter read across a low-word rollover produces a value that never existed, rarely enough to survive commissioning. Detect: a backwards jump in a monotonic value. Prevent: read twice and compare, or reconstruct rollovers in the master, or use a device that snapshots.

6 · The second master

A diagnostic laptop or a second SCADA left connected to a live segment collides with the real master; the error rate rises with load and looks exactly like a cable fault. Detect: errors that appear only when both systems are polling. Prevent: one owner per segment; everyone else reads a cache.

Q1. A 30-device bus at 19,200 baud reads 10 registers from each device once per cycle. Turnaround averages 20 ms. Compute the cycle time and state which term dominates.
Tap to reveal ↓A: t_c = 11/19200 = 0.5729 ms. Request 8 bytes = 4.583 ms; response 3+20+2 = 25 bytes = 14.323 ms; gap 3.5t_c = 2.005 ms; turnaround 20 ms. Of that, byte time is 18.91 ms (46 %) and the fixed per-transaction cost is 22.0 ms (54 %) — the fixed term already dominates, so doubling the baud rate to 38,400 would give only 30 × 31.2 ms = 0.936 s, a 24 % gain, while the hard floor is 30 × 20 ms = 0.6 s.
Q2. You send 01 03 00 00 00 0A C5 CD and receive 01 83 02 C0 F1. What exactly has the device told you, and what have you learned about the rest of the system?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Function 0x83 = 0x03 + 0x80, so it is an exception to your read; code 02 = Illegal Data Address. The device does not implement holding registers 0–9. You have simultaneously learned that the cabling, termination, baud rate, parity, framing, CRC, slave address and the device's firmware are all working — an exception is a fully formed, CRC-valid reply. Investigate only the address convention (are you off by 40001 or by one?), the table (should this be FC04?), and the quantity. Do not touch the cable.
Q3. Why does the Modbus specification stop scaling the 3.5-character silent interval above 19,200 baud, and what does that cost you at 115,200?
Tap to reveal ↓A: At high baud rates 3.5 characters becomes very short — 334 µs at 115,200 — and reliably detecting a gap that small was impractical for the hardware and software of the time, so the specification fixes the interval at 1.750 ms (and 1.5T at 750 µs) above 19,200. The cost is that inter-frame overhead is about 5.2× longer than the scaled value would be. In a fast, short-transaction network this becomes a visible share of the cycle: at 115,200 with an 8-byte request and a 21-byte response, the frames take 2.77 ms and the mandatory gap takes 1.75 ms — 39 % of the wire-related time is now silence.
Q4. An energy meter's 32-bit total reads 3,795,845,121 when the true value is 123,456. Diagnose it precisely, and say why the equivalent error on a temperature float would be more dangerous.
Tap to reveal ↓A: 123,456 = 0x0001E240. The observed value is 0xE2400001 — the two 16-bit registers combined in the opposite order. This is a CDAB / word-swap error: the meter transmits the high word first (ABCD) and the master is assembling low word first. Fix the master's word-order setting, or swap the registers in the mapping. It is not a byte-swap within registers, which would give 0x0100 0x40E2 = 16,793,826.

The equivalent error on a float is more dangerous because a wildly wrong integer is obvious, whereas IEEE-754 word-swapping can map a plausible value onto another plausible value — and a temperature reading of 30 °C that should be 24 °C passes every sanity check anyone will apply. The defence is to verify against a known asymmetric value at commissioning, not to rely on the number looking sensible.
Q5. Three SCADA clients poll one 16-device serial segment through a gateway. Each transaction takes 72 ms. What is the segment's capacity, and what happens when the offered load exceeds it?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Capacity = 1/0.072 = 13.9 transactions per second, and it is hard — the segment is half duplex with one transaction outstanding. Beyond it the gateway's queue (typically 8 deep) fills; then it either drops requests silently or returns exception 0B, Gateway Target Failed to Respond, and all three clients report an intermittent Ethernet problem that does not exist. Even below capacity, unsynchronised clients arrive in bursts: ten simultaneous requests put the tenth 720 ms behind, beyond most default timeouts. The fix is architectural — one owner polls the segment on its own schedule and serves the others from a cache, with the value's age exposed.
Q6. A supplier proposes putting a machine's emergency-stop status on Modbus so the SCADA can stop the line. What do you say, and why?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Reporting E-stop status to SCADA for information is fine and useful. Using Modbus to perform the stop is not acceptable and should be refused in writing. Modbus has no safety integrity: no black-channel safety layer, no guaranteed delivery time, no authentication, no detection of a stalled master, and a diagnostic default that silently holds the last value. A protective function needs a rated architecture — a hardwired safety relay or a safety fieldbus with a certified safety layer, designed to the required performance level under ISO 13849-1 or SIL under IEC 62061/61508 — see A52 and A35. The correct answer names the standard, offers the compliant alternative, and puts the refusal in the minutes.

WE2 — SHOULD I OVER-READ THE GAP?

Situation. A meter's register map has the four values you need at PDU 0–3 and another six at PDU 40–45, with registers 4–39 undefined but readable. 9,600 baud, turnaround 35 ms. Question: two transactions, or one over-reading the 36-register gap?

Option A — two transactions: T_A = 2 × [8·t_c + t_TA + (5 + 2r)·t_c + t_gap] = [9.167 + 35 + (5+8)(1.14583) + 4.010] + [9.167 + 35 + (5+12)(1.14583) + 4.010] = 63.07 + 67.66 = 130.73 ms Option B — one transaction of 46 registers (0 to 45): T_B = 9.167 + 35 + (5 + 92)(1.14583) + 4.010 = 159.32 ms Two transactions win, by 28.6 ms. General break-even — the gap size g at which they are equal: saving from merging = 13·t_c + t_TA + t_gap (one request + one response overhead + one turnaround + one gap) cost of merging = 2g·t_c g* = (13·t_c + t_TA + t_gap) / (2·t_c) at 9,600 / 35 ms: g* = (14.90 + 35 + 4.010)/2.2917 = 23.5 registers at 9,600 / 10 ms: g* = 12.6 at 38,400 / 35 ms: g* = 70.6 at 115,200 / 35 ms: g* = 198.9

Answer: split, because the 36-register gap exceeds g* = 23.5 at this baud rate. The general result is more useful than the specific one, and it is counter-intuitive in a productive way: the faster the line, the more it pays to over-read. At 115,200 baud you should merge across gaps of up to two hundred registers, because bytes have become almost free while the turnaround has not moved at all. This is the same two-term structure as page 9, applied to a different decision, and it is derived properly on page 18.

WE3 — WHAT A BAD TIMEOUT COSTS, IN SECONDS AND IN TRUTH

Situation. The cold-open plant: 144 transactions per cycle, 72.24 ms each, frame error rate 4 %, retry timeout 1,000 ms, one retry. Question: quantify the cost of the timeout setting, the cost of the error rate, and the number of wrong values that pass the CRC undetected each day.

clean cycle = 144 × 72.24 ms = 10.40 s failed transactions = 0.04 × 144 = 5.76 per cycle retry penalty = 5.76 × 1.000 s = +5.76 s observed cycle = 16.16 s (55 % overhead from one config field) with t_to = 4 × T_txn = 289 ms, rounded to 250 ms: penalty = 5.76 × 0.250 = +1.44 s → 11.84 s after the physical repair (p = 0.03 %): penalty = 144 × 0.0003 × 0.250 = +0.011 s → 10.41 s undetected corruption, BEFORE repair: cycles per day = 86,400 / 16.16 = 5,347 corrupted frames = 5,347 × 5.76 = 30,799 per day undetected at 2⁻¹⁶ = 30,799 / 65,536 = 0.47 per day undetected corruption, AFTER repair: = 86,400/10.41 × 144 × 0.0003 / 65,536 = 0.0055 per day (one per 182 days)

Reading the result. Three things are worth saying to a client from this arithmetic. The timeout alone accounted for 4.32 s of a 16.16 s cycle — 27 % — and cost nothing to fix. The physical repair mattered for a reason nobody had raised: it took the rate of silently wrong values from about one every two days to about one every six months. And the CRC did not prevent those; it merely made them rare. Error detection converts a certainty into a probability, and you own the probability.

A last point of professional honesty: 0.47 undetected wrong values per day in an energy billing system is not obviously acceptable, and it is not obviously unacceptable either — it depends on whether a single wrong sample can move a total. Here it could, because the wrong sample was a cumulative counter, and a corrupted 32-bit counter reading can inject an enormous step. The mitigation is at the application layer: reject any counter reading that decreases or increases by more than physically possible. Two lines of code that make the CRC's residual irrelevant.

cycle time 16.16 s, jittering 14–19 s samples per 15-minute demand window = 900 / 16.16 = 55.7 — and the sample instants are not evenly spaced, because the cycle length depends on that cycle's error count. wrong: E = Σ P_i · Δt_i with Δt unknown and P aliased right: E = kWh_register(t₂) − kWh_register(t₁) — poll cost of the right answer: one transaction per meter per minute = 48/60 = 0.8 transactions/s × 58.5 ms = under 5 % of the bus. — and it removes the jitter, the aliasing and the integration error completely, not approximately.

P2 — FRAME CONSTRUCTION AND CRC [8 marks]

Construct the complete RTU request to read 2 holding registers starting at traditional reference 40101 from slave 10, give the CRC bytes in transmission order, state the expected response length, and state what running the CRC over the whole received frame should produce.

P3 — THE PLAUSIBLE WORD SWAP [8 marks]

A 32-bit energy counter is read as registers 0x000A and 0x0005 and interpreted as UINT32 ABCD. (a) What value does the master report? (b) What would a CDAB master report? (c) Explain why this class of error is more dangerous than a swapped IEEE-754 float, and (d) give a commissioning test that detects it in one reading.

Tap for the solution ↓(a) 0x000A0005 = 655,365. (b) 0x0005000A = 327,690.

(c) Both are entirely plausible kWh readings for an industrial site, so no range check, no operator and no sanity test will reject either. The error reveals itself only as a wrong rate of increase, which requires an independent reference to notice — and the factor is not constant, so it cannot be calibrated away. Contrast a swapped float: 24.7 °C is 0x41C5999A, and word-swapping gives 0x999A41C5 = −1.59×10−23, because the exponent field is taken from the wrong half. Almost every float swap yields a denormal or an astronomical value and is caught within minutes. The dangerous errors are the ones that produce values inside the expected range, and for 32-bit integers with both words populated, all four permutations usually do.

(d) Read a register pair whose true value is known and asymmetric in its two halves, then compare all four permutations. P4 — TIMING AND THE 1.5T RULE [8 marks]

At 115,200 baud: (a) give t_c, the 3.5T and 1.5T intervals as the specification defines them, and the values they would take if the intervals still scaled. Tap for the solution ↓(a) t_c = 11/115,200 = 95.5 µs. Scaled values would be 3.5t_c = 334 µs and 1.5t_c = 143 µs. (b) A 6 ms gap inside a frame exceeds the 750 µs intra-frame limit by 8×, so a strict receiver declares the frame invalid and discards it entirely, including the part that arrived correctly; the master then times out and retries, and the sniffer on the wire shows a perfect response, because the fault is between the wire and your code. P5 — RESIDUAL ERROR AND WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT [9 marks]

A bus runs 144 transactions per 10.4 s cycle with a 2 % frame error rate. (a) How many corrupted frames per day? (b) How many pass the CRC undetected? Tap for the solution ↓(a) Cycles per day = 86,400/10.4 = 8,308. Corrupted frames = 8,308 × 144 × 0.02 = 23,926 per day.

(Credit for noting this is an upper bound: single, double, odd-count and ≤16-bit burst errors are detected with certainty, so the true figure is lower and depends on the error mechanism — a genuinely bursty noise source is closer to this bound than random single-bit errors, which are never missed.)

(c) Risk. A corrupted 32-bit counter is not a small error; flipping one bit of the high word changes the value by up to 231. One undetected corruption every three days in a billing feed is a material financial and reputational exposure, and it will be blamed on the meter.

P6 — RS-485 SEGMENT DESIGN [9 marks]

Design a segment for 40 devices spread over three buildings, maximum 620 m between the furthest two, 38,400 baud required. Devices use 1/4-unit-load transceivers. State the topology, termination, biasing, screening, isolation and the maximum stub length, and justify each with a number where one exists.

Tap for the solution ↓Feasibility first. Rate × length = 38,400 × 620 = 2.4×107, against the 108 guideline — 4× margin, feasible. Loading: 40 × 0.25 = 10 unit loads of the 32 permitted (a 1/4-UL bus supports 128 nodes), so node count is not the constraint. Propagation across 620 m = 3.1 µs, irrelevant against any turnaround.

Topology. Daisy chain along the trunk, in and out at every device. Three buildings means three earth systems, so this is the decisive point: place an isolated repeater at each building boundary, giving three independently terminated segments. This is not optional in Sri Lanka — inter-building earth-potential differences during a lightning event routinely exceed the −7 to +12 V common-mode window, which destroys transceivers rather than merely corrupting data. The repeaters also restore the 32-UL budget and the length budget per segment, and they localise a fault to one building.

Per segment: 120 Ω termination at the two physical ends only (verify: 60 Ω across A–B with power off); failsafe bias at exactly one point per segment, ~470 Ω pull-up/pull-down from 5 V giving about 300 mV idle; screened 120 Ω twisted pair with A and B in the same pair; a third conductor as signal common; screen bonded at one end of each segment.
Stubs: with a slew-limited driver (t_rise ≈ 200 ns) the rule 2L/v < t_rise/5 gives L < 4 m, but specify ≤ 0.3 m so that no future driver choice or contractor shortcut can break it.
Also specify: surge protection at each building entry, segregation of 300 mm from motor cables, and an FC08 error-counter acceptance test per segment. Marks: 2 feasibility numbers, 3 isolation/repeater reasoning, 2 termination and bias, 2 stubs, screen and cable.
P7 — THE GATEWAY THAT COULD NOT KEEP UP [9 marks]

Twenty devices behind one gateway serial port, 9,600 baud, 10 registers per read, 20 ms turnaround. (c) The gateway's queue (typically 8) fills within about half a second. P8 — DIAGNOSE: “IT WORKS UNTIL WE START THE EXTRUDER” [10 marks]

A 15-device bus at 19,200 baud runs cleanly. Whenever a 55 kW extruder drive starts, three devices at the far end of the bus report CRC errors for about four seconds, then recover. The drive has been in service for years; the Modbus network was added last month. Give five ranked hypotheses, the discriminating test for each, and the fix for the most likely.

Tap for the solution ↓Framing observation first: the fault is correlated with a known event, affects only the electrically distant nodes, and is transient — that combination points to coupled interference plus thin signal margin, not to a fault in any device.

1. Capacitive/inductive coupling from the VFD motor cable running parallel to the Modbus cable. Test: measure the separation and parallel run length; temporarily re-route a section or run a spare cable along a different path and repeat the start. Fix: 300 mm separation, cross at right angles, screened motor cable bonded 360 ° at both ends at the drive and motor (a motor cable's screen is bonded both ends — the opposite of the signal-cable rule, and for the opposite reason: it is carrying the return current, not avoiding a loop). See A08.
2. Common-mode transient / earth-potential shift on start. Test: scope A–common at the far end during a start; look for excursions beyond ±7 V. Fix: isolated repeater between the drive panel and the rest of the bus.
3. Thin margin at the far end from a missing terminator or bias — the interference is merely revealing it. Test: 60 Ω check with power off; idle A–B voltage. Fix: correct termination and bias. This is the cheapest test and should be done first even though it ranks third as a cause.
4. Screen bonded at both ends, so the start-up inrush drives current through the screen. Test: clamp meter on the screen during a start. Fix: single-point bond.
5. Shared, inadequate 24 V supply sagging on inrush and browning out the far transceivers. Test: scope the 24 V rail at the far panel during a start. Fix: separate supply or local decoupling.

Most likely: 1, aggravated by 3. The fault appeared when the Modbus network was added, which tells you the drive is unchanged and the new cable was routed badly — the commonest single cause of this exact symptom. Expected fix: re-route with separation and correct the termination and bias, then prove it with an FC08 error count across ten starts. Marks: 5 for ranked hypotheses, 3 for discriminating tests, 2 for the fix and its verification.
P9 — DESIGN A RURAL WATER SCHEME [10 marks]

Reason: 4G round-trip is 60–300 ms and unreliable, and polling eight sites sequentially across it re-creates the cold-open problem at a larger scale (this is exactly the argument in A14). What can and cannot be said about time. Can: the acquisition timestamp from the gateway (NTP-disciplined, good to tens of milliseconds), the value's age at the SCADA, and the data-quality flag. Cannot: any claim of simultaneity between sites finer than the gateway clock synchronisation; any sequence-of-events ordering between sites, because Modbus carries no time and the gateway timestamps arrival, not measurement; any assertion that a value is live rather than cached. The SCADA display must therefore show an age or a staleness indication per site, and alarms must be derived at the gateway, where the data is fresh, not centrally on possibly stale values. Marks: 3 cycle computation, 4 architecture including store-and-forward and local autonomy, 3 the honest time statement.
P10 — SYNTHESIS: THE REQUEST YOU SHOULD REFUSE [9 marks]

A client asks you to deliver, on one existing RS-485 segment: 120 devices, all parameters at 1 s, an operator write path from a cloud dashboard, and the machine interlock chain moved onto Modbus “to save wiring”. Turnaround averages 30 ms. Respond as an engineer: what you will do, what you will not, and why — with numbers.

Tap for the solution ↓1. The 1-second requirement on one segment is impossible, and the proof is one line. The turnaround floor alone is 120 × 30 ms = 3.6 s for a single transaction per device, before a single byte is transmitted. At a realistic 70 % target utilisation that needs at least 6 segments, and more if any device needs two transactions. What I will do: deliver 1 s on 6–8 segments via a multi-port gateway, or deliver a classified scheme on fewer segments — 1 s for the values that change, 10 s and 60 s for those that do not — and show the client the classification so the trade is theirs, made with numbers.

2. The cloud write path: yes, with conditions, or no. A write path from outside the OT network to devices with no authentication is an unauthenticated remote-control interface (page 13). What I will do: writes permitted only through a gateway with a whitelist of specific registers and value ranges, over a VPN with MFA, with every write logged with user, time, register and value, and with the device-side comms-loss behaviour tested and documented. What I will not do: forward port 502, or allow arbitrary register writes from a cloud service.

3. The interlock chain: refused, in writing. Modbus has no safety integrity, no bounded delivery time, no black-channel safety layer and no detection of a stalled master; a fault that leaves the last value latched is indistinguishable from a healthy interlock. Moving a protective function onto it would breach ISO 12100 / ISO 13849-1 and IEC 62061 and would be indefensible after an incident (A52, A35). What I offer instead: keep the interlock hardwired or on a certified safety bus, and put its status on Modbus for monitoring — which delivers the visibility they actually wanted at no risk. Quantify the saving they are chasing so the refusal is not merely a refusal: the wiring saved is typically a few hundred metres of cable against a machine that cannot be legally operated.

Marks: 3 for the floor calculation and the segment count, 3 for a proportionate, conditional yes on writes, 3 for a refusal that names the standards and offers a compliant alternative. An answer that refuses everything scores as poorly as one that accepts everything.

Four Derivations

D1 — THE TWO-TERM BUDGET LAW, ITS FLOOR AND ITS CROSSOVER CORE

This is the result the whole module rests on. Start from one transaction and be careful about what depends on the baud rate and what does not.

Let B = baud rate (bit/s) m = bits per character (11 for Modbus serial) t_c = m/B character time N_i = total bytes in transaction i (request + response) t_TA,i = slave i turnaround time t_g(B) = inter-frame silence = max(3.5·m/B , 1.75 ms) One transaction: T_i = N_i·t_c + t_TA,i + t_g(B) (1) A cycle of n transactions on ONE half-duplex segment is a plain sum, because exactly one transaction may be outstanding: T_cycle = Σᵢ T_i = (Σ N_i)·(m/B) + Σ t_TA,i + n·t_g(B) Write N_tot = Σ N_i and let t̄_TA be the mean turnaround: T_cycle = N_tot·m/B + n·(t̄_TA + t_g(B)) (2) ← term A → ←——— term B ———→ Term A is inversely proportional to B. Term B is independent of B once B > 19,200, where t_g is fixed at 1.75 ms; below that it carries a small 1/B component through t_g. The floor. Take the limit: lim (B → ∞) T_cycle = n·(t̄_TA + 1.75 ms) (3) — a hard lower bound set by the DEVICES and the TRANSACTION COUNT. No serial speed, no better master, no cable improves it. The crossover. The two terms are equal when N_tot·m/B = n·(t̄_TA + t_g) → B* = N_tot·m / [ n·(t̄_TA + t_g) ] (4) For a uniform network, N_tot = n·N, so B* = N·m/(t̄_TA + t_g): the crossover does not depend on the number of devices at all. N = 29 bytes, m = 11, t_TA = 35 ms, t_g = 4.01 ms: B* = 319 / 0.03901 = 8,177 baud ignoring t_g (the usual quick estimate): 319/0.035 = 9,114 baud

Three consequences, and they are the examinable ones. First, equation (3) means that a specification is either achievable or not before you choose any hardware: compute n·t̄TA and compare it with the requirement. Second, equation (4) says the crossover baud is a property of the transaction shape and the devices, not of the network size — so a two-device network and a hundred-device network built from the same instruments have the same answer to “will more baud help?”. Third, since term B is proportional to n, halving the transaction count is always at least as good as doubling the baud rate, and is better whenever B > B*. That inequality is the module's memory hook in algebraic form.

Adding errors is straightforward and worth doing, because it shows where a bad timeout sits in the structure. With frame error probability p, retry limit k and timeout tto, each transaction is attempted 1 + p + p2 + … times, and each failed attempt costs a full timeout rather than a full transaction:

T_cycle(p) ≈ T_cycle(0) · 1/(1−p) + n·p·t_to (5) for small p the first correction is negligible and the second is not: p = 0.04, n = 144, t_to = 1.0 s → +5.76 s p = 0.04, n = 144, t_to = 0.25 s → +1.44 s The timeout enters LINEARLY and multiplied by n. It is the highest- leverage single number in a Modbus configuration, and it is the one most often left at a vendor default.
D2 — EVERY QUANTITY LIMIT FROM ONE BUFFER SIZE STRETCH

Modbus's apparently arbitrary limits — 125 registers, 2000 coils, 123, 1968 — all follow from a single number, and being able to reconstruct them shows you understand the frame rather than having memorised a table.

Axiom: the serial ADU shall not exceed 256 bytes. ADU_RTU = 1 (address) + PDU + 2 (CRC) → PDU_max = 256 − 3 = 253 bytes → data after the function code = 253 − 1 = 252 bytes FC03 / FC04 read registers. Response PDU = FC(1) + byte count(1) + 2n 1 + 1 + 2n ≤ 253 → 2n ≤ 251 → n ≤ 125.5 → n = 125 FC01 / FC02 read bits. Response PDU = FC(1) + byte count(1) + ceil(n/8) ceil(n/8) ≤ 251 → n ≤ 2008 the specification rounds to a decimal figure → n = 2000 FC16 write multiple registers. Request PDU = FC(1) + addr(2) + qty(2) + byte count(1) + 2n 6 + 2n ≤ 253 → 2n ≤ 247 → n ≤ 123.5 → n = 123 FC15 write multiple coils. 6 + ceil(n/8) ≤ 253 → ceil(n/8) ≤ 247 → n ≤ 1976, specified as 1968 (= 246×8, one byte of margin) FC23 read/write. Request = FC(1) + 4 read fields(8) + 4 write fields(8) … wait: read addr(2)+read qty(2)+write addr(2)+write qty(2) + byte count(1) + 2n_w → 10 + 2n_w ≤ 253 → n_w = 121 Response = FC(1) + byte count(1) + 2n_r ≤ 253 → n_r = 125 Modbus TCP. ADU = 7 (MBAP) + PDU ≤ 260, PDU unchanged at 253 → every quantity limit is identical on TCP. The limits are a property of the PDU, and the PDU is the part that never changes.

The 256-byte axiom itself has no protocol justification — it is the receive-buffer size of the 1979 Modicon implementation. It is a good example of a phenomenon worth recognising generally: in a long-lived standard, the constants are usually archaeology. Understanding that they are archaeology rather than physics tells you which constraints a future revision might relax and which are structural.

D3 — WHY CRC-16 DETECTS WHAT IT DETECTS STRETCH

Treat the message bits as coefficients of a polynomial M(x) over GF(2). Multiply by x16 to make room, divide by the generator G(x), and transmit the remainder R(x) alongside. The received polynomial is T(x) = M(x)·x16 + R(x), which is divisible by G(x) by construction.

received: T'(x) = T(x) + E(x) E(x) = the error polynomial T'(x) mod G(x) = [T(x) mod G] + [E(x) mod G] = 0 + E(x) mod G An error is UNDETECTED if and only if G(x) divides E(x). (6) Everything else follows from (6) and from the factorisation G(x) = x¹⁶ + x¹⁵ + x² + 1 = (x + 1)(x¹⁵ + x + 1) (7) Single-bit errors. E(x) = xᵢ. G has more than one term, so it cannot divide a monomial. → always detected. Odd numbers of errors. If E has an odd number of terms then E(1) = 1. But (x + 1) | G, so any multiple of G satisfies E(1) = 0. Contradiction → all odd-weight errors detected. This is exactly what the (x + 1) factor buys, and it is why generators are chosen to have it. Two-bit errors. E(x) = xᵢ + xʲ = xʲ(xᵢ⁻ʲ + 1), i > j. G divides this only if the primitive factor x¹⁵ + x + 1 divides xᵎ + 1 where d = i − j. A primitive polynomial of degree 15 has order 2¹⁵ − 1 = 32,767, so the smallest such d is 32,767 bits — far longer than any Modbus frame (256 bytes = 2,048 bits). → all double-bit errors detected. Burst errors. A burst of length L is E(x) = xʲ·b(x) with deg b = L−1. G cannot divide xʲ, so it must divide b(x); impossible if L − 1 < 16. → every burst of 16 bits or fewer is detected. L = 17 exactly: fraction undetected = 2⁻¹⁵ = 1 / 32,768 L > 17: fraction undetected = 2⁻¹⁶ = 1 / 65,536 Hamming distance 4 for frames under 32,767 bits: so 1, 2 and 3-bit errors are always caught, and some 4-bit patterns are not.

Two practical corollaries. First, the whole-frame check: because T(x) is divisible by G(x), running the same CRC over the received message including its two CRC bytes yields zero for a valid frame. That is one comparison instead of extracting and comparing two bytes, and it is what real firmware does. Second, and more important operationally: the residual undetected rate is a rate, not a guarantee. Multiply 2−16 by your corrupted-frame rate to get wrong values per day (WE3). If that number is unacceptable for the data in question — and for a cumulative counter feeding a bill, it usually is — the answer is an application-layer plausibility check, because no error-detecting code will do it for you.

Finally, note what a CRC provably does not do: it offers no protection against a deliberate attacker, who simply recomputes it. CRC is a code against noise; a MAC is a code against people. Confusing the two is the single commonest error in industrial-protocol risk assessments (page 13, A54).

D4 — OPTIMAL BLOCKING OF A SPARSE REGISTER MAP STRETCH

You need registers scattered across a map. Merging two reads across a gap costs the extra data bytes but saves a whole transaction overhead. Where is the break-even, and how do you block an arbitrary map optimally?

Two adjacent blocks of r₁ and r₂ registers separated by a gap of g. Split: T_split = [8t_c + t_TA + (5 + 2r₁)t_c + t_g] + [8t_c + t_TA + (5 + 2r₂)t_c + t_g] Merged: T_merge = 8t_c + t_TA + (5 + 2(r₁+g+r₂))t_c + t_g Subtract — the r₁ and r₂ data terms cancel: T_split − T_merge = (8 + 5)t_c + t_TA + t_g − 2g·t_c Merging wins when this is positive: g < g* = (13·t_c + t_TA + t_g) / (2·t_c) (8) Substituting t_c = m/B and t_g = max(3.5m/B, 1.75 ms): g* = 6.5 + (t_TA + t_g)·B / (2m) (9) 9,600 baud, t_TA = 35 ms: g* = 6.5 + 0.03901×9600/22 = 23.5 9,600 baud, t_TA = 10 ms: g* = 6.5 + 0.01401×9600/22 = 12.6 38,400 baud, t_TA = 35 ms: g* = 6.5 + 0.03675×38400/22 = 70.6 115,200 baud, t_TA = 35 ms: g* = 6.5 + 0.03675×115200/22 = 198.9 g* grows linearly with baud rate. The faster the line, the more aggressively you should over-read. This is the same structure as D1 seen from the other side: as bytes get cheap, transactions do not. Blocking an arbitrary map. Sort the required addresses; walk the sorted list merging any two consecutive points whose separation is less than g*; then split any resulting block longer than the device's maximum quantity R_max. This greedy pass is optimal for the cost model above, because the cost is additive over blocks and the merge decision for each gap is independent of every other gap — merging across a gap never changes the sign of the decision at any other gap. Complexity O(k log k) for k points, dominated by the sort. Caveats that the model does not contain: • a device may reject a range spanning an unimplemented address (exc. 02) • R_max may be far below the protocol's 125 • registers in one block share a poll rate — merging a 1 s value with a 60 s value forces the slow one to 1 s, which can COST more than the transaction it saved. Block by rate class first, then by address.

That last caveat is the one that separates a correct answer from a complete one. The optimisation above minimises the cost of one cycle at a fixed set of registers; the real objective is the long-run transaction rate across all rate classes, and there the right sequence is: classify by poll rate → block within each class using (8) → cap at Rmax. Applying the greedy merge before classification is a common and expensive mistake, because it silently promotes slow data into the fast class.

SEVEN TRAPS THAT CATCH GOOD ENGINEERS
#TrapThe correction
1“Raise the baud rate to fix the cycle time”Compute term A against term B first. Above B* ≈ 8–9 kbaud for a typical transaction, the fixed term dominates and the gain is bounded by the floor n·t̄TA.
2“A good CRC means the value is correct”It means the frame is intact with probability 1 − 2−16 per corrupted frame. Multiply by your error rate to get wrong values per day, then add an application-layer plausibility check.
3“The cable is too long, that is why it is slow”Length limits maximum baud rate and reliability. Propagation is 5 ns/m — 6 µs over 1,200 m, invisible against a 35 ms turnaround. Length and throughput are unrelated.
4“Modbus TCP is faster than Modbus RTU”Only if there is no serial segment behind the gateway. Otherwise the serial budget is unchanged, and added concurrency makes it worse. More ports make it faster; a faster transport does not.
5“Endianness is a Modbus ambiguity”The protocol's byte order is defined and big-endian. What is undefined is how a manufacturer spreads a value across registers — a documentation problem, solved by testing and by preferring scaled 16-bit integers.
6“A multi-register read is atomic”Atomicity is a device property and is usually absent. A counter can roll over mid-response. Read twice, or reconstruct rollovers, or use a device that snapshots.
7“Termination: more is safer”Exactly two, at the two physical ends. Three loads the driver below its guaranteed 1.5 V; one leaves a reflecting end. Verify with 60 Ω across A–B, powered down.
