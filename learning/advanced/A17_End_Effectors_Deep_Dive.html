Page 1 · The Problem

Why the gripper decides if automation works

Quick Recap → Going Deeper

The micro card said: "The Robot is the Arm — the Gripper is the Job." The MSc truth behind that line is that the robot arm (A15) only delivers a pose; everything that makes a task succeed — grasp, hold, orient, release — happens in the end-effector.

A six-axis arm with sub-0.05 mm repeatability is worthless if its gripper crushes the part, drops it under acceleration, or cannot reach into the fixture. This is why integrators repeat the rule of thumb the micro card gave: gripper selection accounts for roughly half of automation-project success. The arm is a commodity; the grasp is the engineering. The end-effector (EOAT — End-Of-Arm Tooling) is the only part of the cell that physically touches the product, so it carries the entire burden of part variation, surface finish, mass, and cycle-rate demands.

The arm provides position; the end-effector provides the grasp. Project failures cluster in the gripper, not the robot.
MSc framing: Think of EOAT as a force/torque transducer with geometry. Its job is to create a stable wrench (force + moment) on the part that resists gravity, inertia and process loads — without exceeding the part's allowable stress. Everything in this deep dive is a way to engineer that wrench.
Page 2 · Grasp Theory

Form-closure, force-closure & the friction cone

The physics of "holding"

A grasp is stable when the contacts can resist any external wrench. Two regimes are distinguished in grasp theory:

Form-closure

Geometry alone immobilises the part — contacts cage it so no motion is possible even with zero friction. Needs ≥ 7 frictionless contacts for a 3D rigid body. Example: a part nested in a matching V-pocket.

Force-closure

Friction at the contacts supplies the constraint. Fewer contacts (often 2) suffice, but only if the required forces stay inside the friction cone. This is what almost every industrial gripper relies on.

Force-closure: the contact force vector must stay within the friction cone (half-angle arctan µ) or the part slips.
The grip-force inequality

For a two-jaw grasp lifting a mass m with safety factor S, vertical robot acceleration a, friction coefficient µ and n friction surfaces, the required normal force per jaw is:

F_grip ≥ S · m · (g + a) / (µ · n) m = part mass µ = friction coefficient (steel-on-steel ≈ 0.1–0.2) g+a = gravity + dynamic n = number of gripping friction surfaces (2 for parallel) S = safety factor (typ. 2–4; up to 8 for rotating/heavy moves)
Why parts get dropped: Engineers size for static g but forget the dynamic term a. A part that needs 40 N at rest may need 200 N during a fast slew. Always size for the worst-case acceleration on the trajectory (links to motion profiles in A09).
Page 3 · Parallel Jaw

The workhorse — parallel & angular jaw mechanics

From the micro card's "🔧 Parallel" tab to the mechanism

The micro card listed parallel jaws as the rigid-part default. Mechanically, the jaws must stay parallel through their whole stroke so contact stays flat — achieved by a wedge-cam, rack-and-pinion, or linkage (toggle) drive. Angular grippers instead swing the fingers about a pivot — cheaper, but contact geometry changes through the arc.

Parallel jaws preserve flat contact through the stroke; angular jaws trade that for a wider, cheaper open swing.
Finger design — the part you actually engineer

The gripper body is bought; the fingers (jaws) are custom. Rules that separate a robust cell from a flaky one:

Locate, then grip. Fingers should self-centre the part into a known datum (V-grooves, prism pockets) so grasp pose is repeatable — this feeds back into GD&T datums and downstream placement accuracy.
Keep fingers short & stiff. Finger length is a moment arm; long fingers deflect and amplify positioning error. Deflection ∝ L³.
Match the contact to the surface. Hard tips for machined steel; urethane/HNBR pads for finished or fragile parts to raise µ and spread pressure.
Design for the worst part in tolerance. Size stroke for max material condition and min grip for least material condition.

The electric-gripper revolution: An electric gripper makes force a software variable — the same hardware can grip an egg at 5 N or a casting at 200 N, report grip width (part-present / part-size verification), and log force for every cycle. That telemetry is exactly the field data that feeds Digital Twins / Industry 4.0 (A31) and predictive maintenance.

Pneumatic grip force is simply F = P·A·η (pressure × effective piston area × mechanism efficiency). Doubling the bore quadruples nothing — it multiplies area linearly, so A = πd²/4 means force scales with the square of bore diameter. This is the same gas-law/area physics developed in the Pneumatics deep dive.

Page 5 · Vacuum

Vacuum grasping — flow, leakage & cup engineering

From "🌪️ Vacuum" tab to the suction physics

Vacuum is the most common gripper type in the world (think every cardboard, sheet-metal, and glass line). The holding force is deceptively simple but the flow behaviour is where designs fail.

F_hold = ΔP · A_eff · n_cups / S ΔP = vacuum level below atmosphere (max ≈ 101 kPa, real ≈ 60–85 kPa) A_eff = effective sealed cup area (NOT nominal cup diameter) n = number of cups S = safety factor (≥ 2; ≥ 4 if porous/shear loads)
Holding force = ΔP × sealed area × cups. Reality is governed by leakage flow vs the generator's flow capacity.

Page 6 · Magnetic & Specialty

Magnetic, Bernoulli, needle & cryo grippers

The micro card's "🧲 Magnetic" answer, generalised

The micro quiz answer was a magnetic gripper for a flat steel plate — correct because it needs no clearance and holds anywhere on the surface. Here is the full specialty family for parts that defeat jaws and vacuum.

🧲 Electro-permanent magnetic

Permanent magnet holds with zero power; a brief current pulse switches it on/off. Fails safe (holds during power loss). Only ferrous parts; force drops fast with air gap and on thin/curved sheets.

🌬️ Bernoulli (non-contact)

High-velocity air over the part creates low pressure (Bernoulli) that lifts it while air film prevents contact. Ideal for silicon wafers, fragile films, hot/sticky parts.

📌 Needle / pin

Fine needles penetrate slightly to grip textiles, foam, leather — limp materials no jaw or cup can hold.

❄️ Cryogenic / electrostatic

Freeze a thin water layer to grip soft food/fabric; or electrostatic charge to hold films & PCBs. Used where contamination must be near-zero.

Magnetic hold force collapses with air gap — so surface finish, paint, and sheet curvature directly degrade the grasp.
Selection insight: jaws grip the geometry, vacuum grips the surface, magnets grip the material, soft hands grip the uncertainty. Name what is constant about your part and the gripper class follows.
Page 7 · Soft Robotics

Soft & underactuated compliant grasping

From the tomato line to the theory of compliance

The micro card's real-world example — soft silicone grippers picking 80 tomatoes/min without bruising — is the headline application of soft robotics. The principle: instead of controlling force precisely, the gripper's mechanical compliance spreads contact and self-adapts to shape, so a single tool handles huge part variation.

Fluidic elastomer actuators

Air-filled silicone bellows bend around the object. Low pressure (≈ 1–2 bar) gives a gentle, conforming wrap. Pressure ∝ curvature.

Underactuated fingers

Many joints, few motors. Tendons + springs let fingers passively conform around irregular parts (adaptive/“Fin-Ray” effect) — the structure does the thinking.

Granular jamming

A membrane of granules (e.g. coffee) flows around the part, then vacuum jams it rigid — universal grip of arbitrary shapes.

Gecko / electroadhesion

Van-der-Waals micro-structures or electrostatic pads grip smooth, delicate, or flat items with near-zero pressure.

Soft grippers replace precise force control with passive compliance — the geometry adapts, lowering peak contact pressure.
Where it wins: food, agriculture, e-commerce piece-picking, and any line where part shape varies cycle-to-cycle. Brands the micro card named — Soft Robotics, OnRobot, SCHUNK — all ship compliant or adaptive lines today.

Page 9 · Wrist Budget

Tool changers, payload & the moment budget

"Quick-change tool plates allow multi-task robots"

The micro card's second fact — quick-change plates — is how one arm becomes many tools. An Automatic Tool Changer (ATC) is a master plate on the wrist and tool plates on each EOAT; pneumatics lock a ball mechanism and pass through air, power and signal. But every gram and millimetre you add to the wrist spends the robot's payload budget.

Effective payload must cover: m_part + m_gripper + m_changer + m_cabling and the wrist sees a MOMENT, not just a mass: M = m · g · L L = distance from wrist flange to the combined centre of gravity A 5 kg load at 300 mm offset = the same wrist torque as 15 kg at 100 mm.
The wrist feels mass × offset as a moment. Light, compact EOAT close to the flange protects speed and accuracy.
Integration trap: a perfectly sized gripper can still fail the cell if cabling/hoses snag through the robot's motion, or if the changer offset pushes the CoG past the wrist's allowable inertia. Always model the EOAT in the robot's payload tool before buying.
Page 10 · Value + Cheat Sheet

A selection framework & the Industry 4.0 payoff

Choosing a gripper — a 5-question framework
What is constant about the part? Rigid geometry → jaws; flat smooth surface → vacuum; ferrous material → magnet; shape varies → soft/adaptive.
What is the grip-force budget? Compute F_grip ≥ S·m·(g+a)/(µ·n) at worst-case acceleration, not at rest.
How much variation & how many SKUs? One rigid part → pneumatic; many parts or delicate → electric/soft for programmable, data-rich grasping.
What's the wrist budget? Part + gripper + changer + cabling mass and offset must fit the robot's payload and moment limits.
What data do you need? Grip-width, force, and cycle logging turn the gripper into an Industry 4.0 sensor feeding the twin.

Quick Check — MSc level

A 6 kg steel casting (µ ≈ 0.15) is carried by a 2-jaw parallel gripper through a fast slew with peak vertical acceleration of 12 m/s². Using safety factor S = 3, roughly what normal force must each jaw apply — and what single design change most cheaply halves it?

Tap to reveal answer ↓

F_grip ≥ S·m·(g+a)/(µ·n) = 3 × 6 × (9.81+12) / (0.15 × 2) ≈ 1308 N per jaw. That is a large pneumatic gripper. The cheapest force-halving change is to raise µ — bond a high-friction urethane/HNBR pad (µ ≈ 0.6–0.8) to the jaws. Going from µ = 0.15 to µ ≈ 0.6 cuts the required force ~4×. Slowing the trajectory (lower a) also helps but costs cycle time. This is why finger/pad design — not a bigger gripper — is the real lever.
