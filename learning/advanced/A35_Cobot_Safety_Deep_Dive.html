There Is No Such Thing as a "Safe Cobot"

Safety is a property of the APPLICATION — robot + tool + workpiece + task + layout — never of the robot arm alone.

Definition (ISO 10218)

A collaborative application is one where an operator and a robot system can occupy the same workspace at the same time, protected not by a fence but by a combination of safety-rated functions, separation monitoring, and biomechanical force limits.

The 2025 revision of ISO 10218 deliberately dropped the term "collaborative robot". A power-and-force-limited arm carrying a knife-edged gripper or a hot, sharp workpiece is a dangerous machine. Conversely, a big industrial robot running speed & separation monitoring with laser scanners can be part of a perfectly collaborative application. The arm supplies safety functions; the integrator's risk assessment turns them into a safe application.

Caged cell vs collaborative application

Left: risk removed by exclusion. Right: risk controlled by safety-rated monitoring — the fence is replaced by mathematics and certified electronics.

1996 · First cobot

Colgate & Peshkin patent the "cobot" — a passive, human-guided device at Northwestern University.

2011 · ISO 10218

First robot-safety standard with collaborative clauses; TS 15066 (2016) adds the force/pressure data.

2025 · ISO 10218 rev.

TS 15066 content absorbed into ISO 10218-2 annexes; "collaborative application" replaces "collaborative robot"; cybersecurity added.

Machinery safety is a pyramid — generic principles at the top, robot-specific rules at the bottom.

Type A / B / C hierarchy

The single most misunderstood point in industry: buying a certified PFL arm does not make your cell compliant. ISO 10218-2 compliance — the integrator's risk assessment and validation of the whole application — is what the CE mark on the cell actually claims.

Stop cat. 0

IEC 60204-1: immediate power removal — uncontrolled coast/brake stop.

Stop cat. 1

Controlled deceleration, then power removal — the usual robot protective stop.

Stop cat. 2

Controlled stop, drives stay energised & monitored — enables fast restart (SOS).

The Four Collaborative Methods

ISO 10218-2 / TS 15066 define exactly four ways a human and a robot may share space. Real cells usually combine two or more.

Method overview

Methods 1–3 prevent contact; method 4 makes contact tolerable. CWS = collaborative workspace.

Choosing a method
MethodContact?Typical robotThroughput costBest for
Monitored stopNo (robot frozen)Any, incl. big 6-axisHigh if human enters oftenOccasional load/unload, inspection visits
Hand guidingIntentional onlyAny with HG devicen/a (teaching / lift-assist)Teaching paths, heavy-part placement
SSMNo (distance kept)Standard industrial + scannersMedium — speed scales with distanceBig robots, fenceless cells, AMR handover
PFLYes, limitedForce-limited arm (UR, iiwa, TM…)Speed capped ~250–1500 mm/sSmall parts, assembly beside humans

Rule of thumb: SSM buys speed at the price of floor space (bigger zones), PFL buys closeness at the price of speed. Many cells run PFL and SSM: full speed when nobody is near, PFL-limited when a human is adjacent, monitored stop during hand-over.

Risk Assessment: The Legal Engine

Every safeguard decision must trace back to an ISO 12100 risk assessment — task by task, hazard by hazard.

The ISO 12100 loop

Order matters: you may not "fix it with a warning label" (step 3) if a design change (step 1) is feasible.

Robot-specific hazards to hunt for
  • 1
    Crushing & trapping — between arm and fixture, wall, or the part itself. Head/neck contact must be excluded by design, not just by limits: quasi-static (clamping) contact with the skull is not permitted at all.
  • 2
    Tool & workpiece hazards — sharp edges, hot surfaces, ejected chips, laser/UV. A PFL arm holding a screwdriver bit is a stabbing hazard the arm's force limit does not fix.
  • 3
    Unexpected motion — auto-restart after protective stop, program resume, drift on brake release, singularity speed spikes near joint limits (see A15).
  • 4
    Systematic & cyber — wrong safety configuration loaded, unverified parameter change, network attack altering safety zones (new in 10218:2025).

Output of the assessment: a list of safety functions (e.g. "SLS 250 mm/s when scanner zone 2 occupied") each with a required Performance Level PLr — typically PL d, Category 3 for cobot protective functions.

SSM: The Separation-Distance Math

Speed & separation monitoring is a live inequality: actual distance must always exceed the protective separation distance Sp.

The TS 15066 formula (from ISO 13855)

The robot must be able to stop before the human can reach it — Sp adds up everything that happens during detection and braking.

Sp(t0) = Sh + Sr + Ss + C + Zd + Zr Sh = vH·(Tr + Ts) human travel while system reacts & robot stops Sr = vR·Tr robot travel during reaction time Ss = vS·Ts robot travel during stopping C = intrusion distance (reach-through allowance, ISO 13855) Zd, Zr = sensor & robot position uncertainty vH = 1600 mm/s walking (2000 mm/s if approach ≤ 500 mm) Tr = detection + processing time · Ts = robot stopping time

Everything is worst-case: maximum human speed, maximum robot speed toward the human, longest stopping time at full payload and extension (from the robot's stopping-performance data in the manual, per ISO 10218-1 Annex).

Zone picture

Dynamic SSM: the controller recomputes Sp continuously from live robot speed — the zones "breathe" with the motion.

PFL: Biomechanics of Allowed Contact

Power & force limiting turns "how hard may a robot hit a person?" into published engineering numbers.

Two contact types

⚡ Transient contact

Free impact — the body part can recoil (< 0.5 s contact). Limits are 2× the quasi-static values because energy is shared with the moving body part.

🗜️ Quasi-static contact

Clamping — body part trapped between robot and a fixed surface. Full force persists; the stricter base limits apply. Never permitted for skull, face, throat.

The TS 15066 body model divides the body into regions, each with a maximum force, a maximum pressure, a spring constant k and an effective mass mH. Pressure often governs before force does — a blunt, padded contact surface is the cheapest compliance tool there is.

Transient limits = 2× these forces/pressures. Values are onset-of-pain thresholds from Univ. Mainz studies — not injury thresholds.

From force limit to speed limit

Model the impact as a spring: contact force depends on the relative speed and the reduced mass of the two colliding bodies.

reduced mass: μ = (1/mH + 1/mR)⁻¹ mR ≈ M/2 + mL energy → force: E = F²/(2k) and E = ½·μ·v² v_max = F_max / √(μ·k) ← the speed the safety system must enforce

mR is the effective moving mass: roughly half the arm's moving link mass M plus the full payload mL. This is why PFL arms feel "slow" — for a hand contact (k = 75 N/mm, transient F = 280 N) a 20 kg-effective robot must stay below ≈ 1 m/s.

Functional Safety: PL, Categories & Safe Motion

A safety function is only as good as the electronics executing it — ISO 13849-1 quantifies how much you can trust it.

Performance Level (PL) in one box
PLr from risk graph: S (severity) × F (frequency) × P (possibility of avoidance) S1/S2 · F1/F2 · P1/P2 → PLr = a … e PL ↔ probability of dangerous failure per hour (PFHd): PL c: 10⁻⁶ … 3·10⁻⁶ PL d: 10⁻⁷ … 10⁻⁶ PL e: 10⁻⁸ … 10⁻⁷ PL achieved = f(Category, MTTFd, DCavg, CCF)

Category 3 means: single-fault tolerant — two channels, a single fault does not lose the safety function and is detected where practicable. The standard cobot recipe is PL d, Cat 3: dual-channel joint torque/position sensing, cross-checked by a safety controller.

Dual-channel architecture (Cat 3)

MTTFd (component reliability), DC (diagnostic coverage) and CCF (common-cause failure score ≥ 65) decide whether this architecture actually reaches PL d.

Safe-motion functions (IEC 61800-5-2)

These certified drive functions are the building blocks; the four collaborative methods of page 3 are compositions of them (SSM = SLS + SLP + protective stop; PFL = SLT + SLS…).

Mounting math — ISO 13855
S = K·T + C S = min. distance safeguard → hazard zone K = 2000 mm/s (approach ≤ 500 mm) else 1600 mm/s T = total stop time (sensor + logic + robot) C = 8·(d − 14) for vertical curtains (d = resolution, mm) Example: 30 mm curtain, T = 0.55 s → S = 1600·0.55 + 8·(30−14) = 880 + 128 = 1008 mm

The same K·T logic drives the SSM formula on page 5 — ISO 13855 is the parent. Reach-over and reach-under paths need their own C values from the standard's tables; forgetting a crawl-under gap is a classic audit finding.

Muting

Temporary, automatic suspension of a safeguard while a pallet (not a person) passes — needs ≥ 2 independent muting sensors.

Blanking

Fixed/floating dead zones in a light curtain for material feed — resolution worsens; recalculate C.

Defeat prevention

ISO 14119: mount switches out of reach, use coded actuators — 46% of accidents involve defeated guards.

Application Engineering & Validation

Design the tool, cap the energy, then PROVE the numbers with a force-pressure measurement device.

Design levers before you touch the robot menu
  • 1
    Blunt everything. Edge radii ≥ 2 mm on tool & fixtures, padding on contact-likely surfaces — pressure limits are usually hit long before force limits.
  • 2
    Kill clamping geometry. Keep ≥ 500 mm clearance or ≤ 120 mm gaps (no body part fits); route motions so the arm never closes on a fixed edge at head height.
  • 3
    Exclude head & neck by layout — workpiece presentation below shoulder height, approach vectors away from the face. Skull contact cannot be "limited", only prevented.
  • 4
    Reduce effective mass — lighter tools and slower approach segments only where humans can be; full speed elsewhere via safety zones (SLP).
  • 5
    Plan the restart. Protective stop → restart only from outside the zone, deliberate reset action, no auto-restart with a person still inside (ISO 10218-2 reset rules).
Validation — measuring what you claimed

PFL limits are validated with a biofidelic force-pressure measurement device: a spring + damper gauge matching the body region's k, faced with pressure-mapping film.

Measure at the worst-case points found in the risk assessment — highest speed, stiffest backing, smallest contact area — and file the traces in the technical documentation.

Paper trail to CE / UKCA

The integrator compiles the technical file: risk assessment, safety-function list with PLr vs PL-achieved (SISTEMA calculation), scanner field drawings with 13855 math, PFL measurement reports, functional test protocol for every safety function, operator instructions and training records. Then a Declaration of Conformity under the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 (fully applying from Jan 2027, replacing the 2006/42/EC Directive) — and the cell, not the arm, gets its CE mark. Any later change to speed, tool, payload or layout reopens the assessment.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

The whole module in one card — then the ways real collaborative cells go wrong.

60-second recap
  • 1
    Safety = application, not robot. ISO 10218-1 certifies the arm, 10218-2 + your risk assessment certify the cell.
  • 2
    Four methods: monitored stop, hand guiding, SSM (keep distance ≥ Sp = vH·(Tr+Ts) + robot travel + C + Z) and PFL (keep contact below body-region force/pressure limits).
  • 3
    PFL math: v_max = F_max/√(μ·k), with μ the reduced mass — blunt tools and low effective mass buy you speed.
  • 4
    Functional safety: cobot protective functions are typically PL d Cat 3, built from certified drive functions (STO, SS1/SS2, SLS, SLP, SLT).
  • 5
    Validate: 13855 mounting math, force-pressure measurements, functional test of every safety function — documented in the technical file.
⚠️ How collaborative cells fail audits (and hurt people)
🔪 "Safe robot, deadly tool"

Certified PFL arm fitted with sharp gripper/workpiece — pressure limits blown by tiny contact area. Fix: blunt edges, padding, recalculate pressure, not just force.

📏 Stopping data ignored

Sp computed with marketing speed instead of measured stopping time at full payload/extension. Fix: use the 10218-1 stopping-performance tables, verify with test stops.

🗜️ Clamping point missed

Arm can pin a hand between tool and fixture — quasi-static limits apply, not transient; head-height clamping is outright forbidden. Fix: layout clearances ≥ 500 mm or gaps ≤ 120 mm.

🔁 Auto-restart trap

Robot resumes the instant the scanner clears while the operator is still leaning in from an unmonitored side. Fix: full-coverage zoning + manual reset located with view of the cell.

🧾 Paper PL

"PL d" claimed but e-stop wired through a standard PLC output, no cross-monitoring, CCF ignored. Fix: SISTEMA calc per function, dual-channel wiring, proof-test schedule.

🛠️ Change without reassessment

Heavier gripper or +30% speed tweak after commissioning silently invalidates PFL measurements and Sp. Fix: management-of-change gate on any safety-relevant parameter.

🧠 Quick check 1

Why did ISO 10218:2025 drop the term "collaborative robot"?

Tap to revealBecause safety is a property of the whole APPLICATION — robot + tool + workpiece + layout + task. A force-limited arm with a sharp tool is dangerous; a big robot with proper SSM can be collaborative. Only the application, after risk assessment, can be called safe.
🧠 Quick check 2

An SSM cell has Tr = 0.2 s, Ts = 0.5 s, robot approaching at 1500 mm/s. Roughly what does the human contribute to Sp?

Tap to revealSh = vH·(Tr+Ts) = 1600 × 0.7 = 1120 mm — before adding any robot travel or C. The human term usually dominates: reaction and stopping time are the levers that shrink zones, not sensor range.
🧠 Quick check 3

Quasi-static vs transient contact — which limits are higher and why?

Tap to revealTransient limits are 2× higher. In free impact the body part recoils and shares the energy over a short (<0.5 s) event; in clamping the force persists and the body cannot escape — so clamping gets the stricter base values, and for skull/face/throat it is not permitted at all.
🧠 Quick check 4

What does "PL d, Category 3" promise about a cobot's protective stop?

Tap to revealProbability of dangerous failure between 10⁻⁷ and 10⁻⁶ per hour, achieved with a single-fault-tolerant dual-channel architecture: one fault does not lose the stop function and is detected where practicable (cross-monitored channels, adequate MTTFd, DC and CCF score).
🧠 Quick check 5

Doubling a PFL robot's effective mass does what to its allowed speed (same body region)?

Tap to revealv_max = F/√(μk). For mR ≫ mH the reduced mass μ ≈ mH (hand: 0.6 kg), so doubling robot mass barely changes hand-contact speed — but for heavy body regions (chest, mH = 40 kg) μ grows strongly with mR and v_max falls roughly with 1/√μ. Where you hit matters as much as how heavy you are.
🧠 Quick check 6

A 30 mm-resolution light curtain guards a press-brake robot with total stop time 0.4 s. Minimum mounting distance?

Tap to revealS = K·T + C = 1600 × 0.4 + 8·(30−14) = 640 + 128 = 768 mm from the nearest hazard point (plus checking reach-over/under paths separately).
