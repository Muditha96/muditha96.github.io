What Power Quality Really Means

Power quality is not "is there voltage?" — it is "is the voltage a clean 50 Hz sine of the right size, all the time?" Every deviation has a name, a standard, and a bill.

A power system was designed around one assumption: a single-frequency sinusoid. Every rule of thumb you know — RMS = peak/√2, impedance = 2πfL, kVA = √3·V·I, a capacitor corrects power factor — is a consequence of that assumption. Power quality engineering is what happens when the assumption breaks. Non-linear loads now dominate modern buildings and factories, so the sinusoid is no longer given: it must be measured, budgeted and defended.

Two Separate Problems, Two Separate Owners

Voltage quality — the supply's job

Sags, swells, interruptions, flicker, unbalance, voltage THD, frequency deviation. The utility (and your own upstream impedance) delivers voltage; voltage disturbances are what damages your equipment. Governed by EN 50160 / IEC 61000-2-x.

Current quality — your job

Harmonic currents, poor power factor, unbalanced loading. You inject current into the network; the utility limits what you may inject. Governed by IEEE 519 / IEC 61000-3-x.

The coupling between them is Ohm's law at every harmonic:
Vh = Ih · Zsystem(h)
Your harmonic current becomes everybody's harmonic voltage across the shared source impedance. This one equation is why the whole standards architecture is split in two — and why a strong (low-Z) supply hides sins that a weak supply or a standby generator exposes.

Why Management Cares: The Cost Chain

Power quality problems rarely announce themselves as "power quality". They arrive disguised as maintenance and production problems:

Downtime

A 100 ms sag stops a whole line; restart, purge and scrap can cost hours. Process industries report sags as their single largest quality-of-supply cost.

Energy & capacity

Harmonic currents do no useful work but still heat cables and transformers, and consume kVA you paid for. Losses scale with I²R at every harmonic.

Premature failure

Capacitors, motor insulation, transformer windings and electronics age faster in hot, distorted conditions — the "mystery" failures.

The engineer's job is to translate all three into one measurable technical statement: "at the point of common coupling, TDD = x% against a limit of y%, and voltage THD = z%" — then act on the gap.

Quick check: Your plant injects 25 A of 5th-harmonic current. Nothing changes in the plant, but the utility upgrades the supply transformer from 500 kVA to 1600 kVA. What happens to your harmonic voltage distortion?
Tap to reveal ↓A: It improves (falls) even though you changed nothing. Vh = Ih·Zsystem(h), and a bigger transformer has lower impedance, so the same injected current produces less voltage distortion. This is the key asymmetry of power quality: current distortion is a property of your load, but voltage distortion is a property of the load and the network stiffness. It also explains the standby-generator trap — a genset has a much higher impedance than the grid, so voltage THD can jump from 3% to 12% the moment you transfer to it, with the load unchanged.

The Mathematics: RMS, Fourier & THD

Four definitions do 90% of the work — RMS, harmonic order, THD and TDD. Get them exactly right and every standard becomes readable.

Any periodic waveform, no matter how ugly, can be written as a sum of sinusoids at integer multiples of the fundamental. That is Fourier's theorem, and it is the single tool that turns "the current looks wrong on the scope" into a table of numbers you can compare against a limit.

① The Fourier Series of a Distorted Current
i(t) = I0 + Σh=1..∞ √2·Ih·sin(hω1t + φh)
I0 = DC component · h = harmonic order · Ih = RMS magnitude of harmonic h · ω1 = 2πf1 (fundamental, 50 Hz here) · φh = phase of that harmonic

Harmonic

h = 5

Integer multiple: 250 Hz on a 50 Hz system. Steady, load-generated.

Interharmonic

h = 4.7

Non-integer. From cycloconverters, AFE drives, arc furnaces. Causes flicker and CT ringing.

Subharmonic

h < 1

Below the fundamental (e.g. 20 Hz). Rare but nasty — torsional resonance, lamp flicker.

Frequency in hertz is fh = h·f1. On a 50 Hz system the 5th is 250 Hz, the 7th 350 Hz, the 11th 550 Hz. Always state the system frequency: the "same" 5th harmonic is 300 Hz on a 60 Hz system.

② True RMS — the Only Honest Magnitude
Irms = √( (1/T)∫0T i(t)² dt ) = √( I1² + I2² + I3² + … )
Because sinusoids of different frequencies are orthogonal, their squares add — harmonics add in quadrature, never arithmetically. This one fact is the source of half of all power-quality arithmetic errors.

RMS is defined by heating: the RMS value of a current is the DC current that would dissipate the same power in a resistor. That is precisely why RMS (not peak, not average) is the number that decides cable sizing and thermal life.

True-RMS meter

Digitises the waveform and computes the integral. Reads correctly on any shape. Essential for any modern installation.

Average-responding meter

Rectifies, averages, then multiplies by the form factor 1.11 — valid only for a pure sine. On a flat-topped SMPS current it can read 30–40% LOW, hiding an overload.

Crest factor: CF = Ipeak / Irms  ·  pure sine = 1.414 · single-phase SMPS = 2–3 · six-pulse drive ≈ 1.5
High crest factor is what saturates CTs, false-trips peak-sensing electronics and makes UPS inverters current-limit early.
③ Total Harmonic Distortion — THD
Voltage: THDV = √(Σh=250 Vh²) / V1 × 100%
Current: THDI = √(Σh=250 Ih²) / I1 × 100%
Note the denominator: the fundamental, not the RMS. (Some instruments offer "THD-R", referenced to total RMS — always check which convention is displayed, they differ noticeably above ~20%.)

And the relationship worth memorising, because it converts distortion into heating directly:

Irms = I1·√(1 + THDI²)  (THD as a fraction)
Example: THDI = 30% → Irms = 1.044·I1, only 4.4% more current — but I²R losses rise ~9%, and the loss is deposited at high frequency where skin effect makes R itself larger.
④ TDD — Why IEEE 519 Refuses to Use THDI

THDI has a fatal flaw for compliance: it is a ratio to the present fundamental. A 100 kW drive idling at 5% load draws tiny harmonic amps but its THDI may read 80% — alarming and meaningless. IEEE 519 therefore normalises to a fixed reference:

TDD = √(Σh=250 Ih²) / IL × 100%
IL = maximum demand load current at fundamental frequency (the average of the 12 highest monthly demands, or the design demand for a new installation). Interharmonics are excluded; harmonics up to the 50th are included.

Where Harmonics Come From

Harmonics are not "noise" — they are the deterministic Fourier signature of switching. Once you know a load's rectifier topology, you can predict its spectrum before you measure it.

A linear load (resistor, motor winding, incandescent lamp) draws a current proportional to the applied voltage: sine in, sine out. A non-linear load draws current only during part of the cycle, or in pulses. The current waveform is therefore non-sinusoidal by construction — and its harmonic spectrum is set by the switching pattern, not by chance.

The Rule That Predicts Everything: Pulse Number
h = p·k ± 1   (k = 1, 2, 3 …)
p = pulse number of the converter. Amplitudes fall roughly as Ih ≈ I1/h for an ideal converter with a stiff DC link.

Notice the elegance: the 5th and 7th vanish in a 12-pulse system because the 30° phase shift between the two bridges puts their 5th and 7th components in antiphase. That is not a filter — it is cancellation by geometry, and it is why phase-shifting transformers remain a first-class mitigation strategy.

The Six-Pulse Diode Bridge — Anatomy of the Dominant Villain

Every standard VFD, servo drive, DC bus and large UPS front end is this circuit. It is the single largest harmonic source in industry.

  • 1
    No triplens from a balanced 3φ bridge. A three-wire converter cannot produce zero-sequence current — there is no return path. So a factory dominated by VFDs has a 5th/7th problem, while an office dominated by single-phase electronics has a 3rd/neutral problem. Different disease, different cure.
  • 2
    A stiffer DC link (bigger capacitor, no reactor) makes the current pulse narrower and the THDI worse. Adding DC-link or AC-line inductance widens the conduction angle and cuts distortion — this is why the cheapest mitigation is a reactor, not a filter.
  • 3
    Notching is a separate, voltage-side artefact of line-commutated (SCR) converters: each commutation briefly short-circuits two phases through the supply impedance, cutting a notch in the voltage. It is high-frequency, local, and can upset zero-crossing detectors nearby.
The Modern Harmonic Inventory

Single-phase electronics (3rd-rich)

SMPS in PCs and servers, LED drivers, EV chargers (single-phase), TVs, phone chargers, small UPS. Individually trivial, collectively dominant in commercial buildings. THDI 80–140% without PFC; < 10% with active PFC (mandated by IEC 61000-3-2 above 75 W for lighting/IT classes).

Three-phase drives (5th/7th-rich)

VFDs, servo amplifiers, DC drives, large UPS, welding rectifiers, electroplating supplies, induction heating. The industrial signature.

Arcing loads (broadband + interharmonics)

Arc furnaces, arc welders, discharge lighting with magnetic ballasts. Random, time-varying, and the classic flicker source (Page 9).

Saturating iron

Transformers driven above rated volts, and especially energising inrush: heavy 2nd harmonic plus DC offset. This is why differential relays need 2nd-harmonic restraint (A32).

A special modern case: PV inverters and battery converters. Their fundamental output is clean (they must meet IEC 61000-3-x / IEEE 1547) but they raise the background distortion, contribute interharmonics and "supraharmonics" (2–150 kHz), and — critically — reduce the effective load current IL at the PCC, which tightens your TDD arithmetic. Hence the TRD method in IEEE 519-2022.

Quick check: A plant fits twelve identical 30 kW six-pulse VFDs. A colleague says "the harmonics from twelve drives will be twelve times worse". Is he right?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Not quite — and the difference matters for filter sizing. Harmonic currents from identical converters are largely in phase at low orders (5th, 7th), so they add nearly arithmetically there — twelve drives really do give roughly twelve times the 5th-harmonic amps. But at higher orders (17th and above) the phase angles scatter because of small differences in DC-link charge state, cable impedance and firing instants, so those add partially — often modelled as vector (√n) summation or via IEC 61000-3-6 summation exponents (α = 1 for h < 5, α = 1.4 for 5 ≤ h ≤ 10, α = 2 above). Practical upshot: (1) never simply multiply THDI percentages — those are ratios; add the harmonic amps; (2) diversity gives you some relief at high orders but none where the problem lives; (3) the total TDD at the PCC usually improves relative to a single drive because IL grows with the linear load too.

Sequence Components & the Triplen Problem

Why the 3rd harmonic hides in the neutral, the 5th brakes your motor, and the 7th speeds it up — all from one rotation rule.

In a three-phase system the phases are displaced 120°. A harmonic of order h multiplies that displacement: harmonic h in phase B lags the same harmonic in phase A by h × 120°. Reduce that angle modulo 360° and every harmonic falls into one of three families — and each family behaves completely differently in the machine and in the neutral.

Phase displacement of harmonic h  =  h × 120° (mod 360°)

Two consequences worth carving in stone: (1) the 5th harmonic produces a field rotating backwards, so it fights the motor and, with the forward-rotating 7th, produces a torque ripple at 6× line frequency (300 Hz) that you can hear and that fatigues couplings; (2) triplens have no phase displacement at all, so in a four-wire system they all come home down the neutral together.

The Neutral Conductor Catastrophe

Classical wisdom: "in a balanced three-phase system the neutral carries no current, so it can be sized smaller." That is true only at the fundamental. Add triplens and the arithmetic inverts:

IN = 3 × I3  (for the 3rd harmonic alone, balanced case)
In general: IN = 3·√(I3² + I9² + I15² + …) for balanced triplens, plus any fundamental unbalance in quadrature.
  • 1
    Never reduce the neutral in a circuit feeding single-phase electronic loads. Modern practice for IT/lighting distribution is a full-size or 200%-rated neutral, and cable standards require you to treat a triplen-rich circuit as four loaded conductors (a derating factor in itself — see A44/A46).
  • 2
    The neutral has no circuit breaker. Phases are protected; a neutral overheating from triplens is invisible to protection. Neutral overheating is a documented fire cause in older office risers — thermographic surveys (A41) find them.
  • 3
    In a delta winding triplens cannot leave, so they circulate inside the delta, heating it with no external symptom. A Dyn11 transformer is therefore a useful triplen trap: it blocks 3rd-harmonic current from passing upstream, at the cost of heating itself.
Symmetrical Components — the Formal Tool

Fortescue's transformation decomposes any three unbalanced phasors into three balanced sets. With a = 1∠120°:

I0 = ⅓(Ia + Ib + Ic)  — zero sequence (the neutral / earth path)
I1 = ⅓(Ia + a·Ib + a²·Ic)  — positive sequence (useful, forward)
I2 = ⅓(Ia + a²·Ib + a·Ic)  — negative sequence (damaging, backward)
and IN = 3·I0 — the neutral carries three times the zero-sequence current.

Sequence thinking is not academic decoration: protection relays are built on it. ANSI 46 (negative-sequence current) and 47 (phase-sequence voltage) are literally I2 detectors — see A55's protection table. A 5th-harmonic-rich supply raises the apparent negative-sequence content and can bias unbalance relays.

Quick check: An office riser feeds 200 A per phase of LED lighting and IT load. The neutral is sized at 50% (a 1980s design). Measurement shows 40% third harmonic. Is the neutral safe?
Tap to reveal ↓A: No — badly overloaded. The 3rd-harmonic component per phase is 0.40 × 200 = 80 A, and being zero-sequence it adds arithmetically: IN ≈ 3 × 80 = 240 A. Symptoms to expect: hot neutral bar, discoloured terminations, and elevated neutral-to-earth voltage that upsets sensitive electronics. Remedies, cheapest first: (1) rebalance and split the load across more circuits; (2) replace with a full-size or 200% neutral (and re-derate the cable as four loaded conductors); (3) fit a zig-zag/neutral-blocking transformer or a delta-primary transformer close to the load to trap triplens; (4) specify active-PFC drivers on future lighting so the 3rd is never generated. Note that a passive 5th-harmonic filter would do nothing here — you must match the cure to the harmonic order.

What Harmonics Actually Destroy

Harmonics do not blow things up dramatically. They shorten life quietly, through heat — and they trip things at random, which is worse for your reputation.

Every harmful effect of harmonics traces to one of three mechanisms: extra I²R heating (made worse because resistance rises with frequency), extra magnetic loss (core loss rises faster than frequency), or wrong information given to a device that assumed a sine wave. Diagnose which mechanism you have and the fix chooses itself.

Mechanism 1 · Frequency-Dependent Resistance
Skin depth: δ = √(ρ / (π·f·µ))  — for copper, δ ≈ 9.3 mm at 50 Hz, 4.2 mm at 250 Hz (5th), 2.8 mm at 550 Hz (11th)
Effective AC resistance rises roughly as R(h) ≈ Rdc·√h for large conductors (plus proximity effect between cores).

So the harmonic current is not merely extra amps — each harmonic amp is a more expensive amp. Total conductor loss:

Ploss = Σh Ih²·R(h)  >>  Irms²·R50Hz

Cables

Extra heating; busbars worst (large cross-section, strong skin effect). Neutral in triplen-rich circuits is the hotspot.

Transformers

Eddy losses in windings scale as h²; stray losses in tank and clamps rise. Hence K-factor / factor-K derating below.

Motors

Stator & rotor copper losses plus negative-sequence rotor currents at (h∓1)·s. Derate or accept lost insulation life.

Mechanism 2 · Rotating Machines & Negative Sequence

In a motor, harmonic currents create air-gap fields that rotate at h× synchronous speed, forward for positive-sequence orders and backward for negative-sequence orders:

Slip of harmonic h relative to the rotor: sh = (h ∓ (1−s)) / h
For the 5th (backward): s₅ ≈ 1.2 — the rotor sees a near-locked-rotor slip, so rotor resistance is high and heating is severe. The 5th and 7th together produce a pulsating torque at 6f₁ = 300 Hz.

Thermal

NEMA MG-1 uses the Harmonic Voltage Factor; a common engineering guide is that voltage THD above ~5% warrants derating, and above 8–10% derating becomes significant (a few percent of rated output per point of HVF).

Mechanical

300 Hz torque ripple excites couplings, gearbox teeth and shaft torsionals; shows up as a 6× line-frequency peak in vibration spectra — cross-reference A42's electrical-fault signatures.

Also remember the unbalance analogue: NEMA's guidance is that percentage unbalance produces roughly 2 × (%unbalance)² percent extra rotor heating — 3% unbalance ≈ 18% extra heating. Unbalance and 5th-harmonic content are both negative-sequence phenomena and both punish the rotor.

Mechanism 3 · Capacitors — the Harmonic Magnet
XC(h) = XC1 / h  — a capacitor's impedance falls with frequency
IC(h) = h · ω1 · C · Vh  — so it actively sucks in harmonic current.

A power-factor capacitor bank in a distorted network is therefore not a passive bystander — it is the lowest-impedance path at high frequency. Consequences: bulging cans, blown internal fuses, "capacitors keep failing" complaints, and — far worse — resonance (Page 7). IEC 60831 permits a capacitor RMS current of only about 1.3× rated; harmonics eat that margin quickly.

Quick check: A 500 kVA transformer feeds a data centre. It is loaded to 380 kVA (76%) yet runs hot and its top-oil alarm has operated. Load current THDI is 22% with a strong 3rd harmonic. What is happening, and what number should you compute?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The transformer is thermally overloaded even though its kVA looks fine, because kVA measured at the fundamental understates the loss the harmonics create. Winding eddy losses scale with h², so a 22% THD with substantial low-order content can add tens of percent to the load loss. Compute the Factor-K (IEC/EN 60076-1) from the measured spectrum: usable kVA = nameplate ÷ Factor-K. If Factor-K works out at, say, 1.35, then the honest capacity of this unit is 500/1.35 ≈ 370 kVA — you are already at the limit, which explains the alarm exactly. Two further clues fit the diagnosis: the strong 3rd harmonic means heavy triplen circulation in the delta winding (invisible from outside), and the neutral is probably the hottest conductor in the installation. Actions: verify with an IR survey (A41) and a Class A logger, derate or replace with a K-rated unit, and attack the 3rd at source with active-PFC power supplies or a zig-zag trap.

Power Factor in a Distorted System

There are two power factors, and capacitors only fix one of them. Confusing them is the most expensive mistake in the whole subject.

With a pure sinusoid, power factor is simply cos φ — the cosine of the angle between voltage and current. With harmonics present the current has components at frequencies the voltage does not contain; those components carry no average power at all yet still occupy capacity. The clean sinusoidal power triangle therefore becomes a three-dimensional object.

The Two Power Factors

Displacement PF (dPF)

cos φ₁

Phase shift at the fundamental only. This is what an analogue cos φ meter, and most PF-correction relays, actually measure. Fixed by capacitors.

True PF (tPF)

P / S

Real power ÷ total apparent power, harmonics and all. This is what your bill and your cables see. Not fixed by capacitors.

Full apparent power with distortion:
S² = P² + Q² + D²
P = active power (W) · Q = fundamental reactive power (var) · D = distortion power (var), the harmonic contribution

And the relation to remember:
tPF = cos φ1 / √(1 + THDI²)
(assuming voltage distortion is small, which is usually true). The second factor is called the distortion factor.

Why the VFD Is a Power-Factor Paradox

A six-pulse VFD is one of the most misunderstood loads in the plant:

  • 1
    Its displacement PF is excellent — typically 0.95–0.98 — because the diode bridge draws current roughly in phase with the line-to-line voltage. This is why "VFDs improve your power factor" is repeated by salespeople.
  • 2
    Its true PF is mediocre — typically 0.90–0.95 with a reactor, worse at light load — because of 28–45% THDI. The kVA you must provide is larger than the kW suggests.
  • 3
    If your utility charges on kVA demand or on true PF, harmonics cost you money directly. If it charges on cos φ only, harmonics cost you in cables, transformers and reliability instead.
  • 4
    Because the drive's motor-side PF is handled internally, never fit capacitors on a VFD output — it can destroy the inverter. Capacitors belong upstream of the drive, and even there they need detuning reactors (Page 7).
Correcting Power Factor Correctly
Fundamental compensation required:
QC = P·(tan φ1 − tan φ2)
φ₁ = existing angle, φ₂ = target angle. This is the same formula as A30 — it is still valid, but it only ever addresses the Q axis, never the D axis.

Q too big (lagging)

Induction motors, welders, chokes. Cure: capacitors — fixed, switched-stage, or thyristor-switched for fast loads.

D too big (distortion)

Rectifiers, SMPS. Cure: harmonic mitigation — reactors, filters, higher pulse number, AFE (Page 8). Capacitors are counter-productive.

Both

The real factory. Cure: detuned capacitor bank (Q correction that cannot resonate) or an active filter which corrects Q and D together.

Capacity you free up by correcting PF: S = P / PF
Example: 400 kW at tPF 0.75 needs 533 kVA. At tPF 0.95 it needs 421 kVA — you release 112 kVA of transformer, cable and switchgear capacity without buying any.

Harmonic Resonance — The Hidden Amplifier

The most destructive power-quality failure mode is not a big harmonic source. It is a modest source that happens to land on a resonance you built by accident.

Every installation contains inductance (the supply transformer and cables) and, if power factor is corrected, capacitance. Inductance and capacitance in the same network define a resonant frequency. If that frequency coincides with a harmonic your loads produce, the harmonic current circulating between the capacitor and the supply can be amplified five to twenty times — with nothing on the nameplate to warn you.

The Two Resonances

Parallel resonance (the dangerous one)

Seen from the harmonic source, the supply inductance and the capacitor bank are in parallel. At resonance that parallel combination looks like a very high impedance, so a small injected harmonic current produces a large harmonic voltage — which then drives large circulating currents in both the L and the C. Symptoms: exploded capacitors, blown bank fuses, transformer humming, voltage THD spiking above 10%.

Series resonance (sometimes useful)

Seen from the supply, a capacitor in series with a reactor presents a very low impedance at the tuned frequency — a deliberate sink for that harmonic. This is exactly how a passive tuned filter works: we engineer a series resonance at the harmonic we want to trap.

The single most useful formula in this module:
hr = √( SSC / QC )
hr = resonant harmonic order · SSC = short-circuit power at the busbar (MVA or kVA) · QC = installed capacitor rating (same units). Equivalent form using the transformer: hr ≈ √( Stx / (Z% · QC) ) with Z% as a fraction.

Derivation is one line and worth doing yourself: set XL(h) = XC(h), i.e. h·XL1 = XC1/h, giving h = √(XC1/XL1), then substitute XC1 = V²/QC and XL1 = V²/SSC. See Page 15.

The Impedance-versus-Frequency Picture

Read the red curve: the plant's own harmonic sources sit at 5, 7, 11, 13. If the accidental parallel resonance lands on the 7th, the 7th-harmonic voltage explodes even though nothing about the load changed. Read the green curve: adding a small series reactor to the capacitor bank slides the resonance below the 5th, into a frequency band where no significant harmonic exists — the whole trick of a "detuned" bank.

The Ladder as a Picture
How Each Device Actually Works

Line reactor — widen the pulse

Series inductance limits di/dt into the DC capacitor, so the conduction angle widens from a narrow spike toward a broader hump. Fewer high-order components, lower peak. Specify by impedance % at rated current: 3% and 5% are standard. Note the volt-drop: 5% costs you a few percent of maximum output voltage, so check top-speed torque.

Passive tuned filter — engineered series resonance

An L and C in series, tuned slightly below the target order (e.g. 4.7 for the 5th) so component tolerance and ageing cannot drift it upward onto the harmonic. Presents low impedance to that harmonic — it drains into the filter instead of the supply. Provides fixed kvar, so it must be switched with the load.

12-pulse — cancel by phase shift

Two 6-pulse bridges fed by a Δ and a Y secondary, 30° apart. The 5th and 7th arrive in antiphase at the primary and cancel; the lowest surviving orders are the 11th and 13th. Utterly passive and immune to network change — the most robust passive solution for big, permanent loads.

Active filter — inject the inverse

An IGBT inverter measures the load current, computes its harmonic content and injects an equal-and-opposite current so the supply sees a sine. Bandwidth typically to the 50th. Sized in harmonic amps. Can also do PF and unbalance correction, and cannot resonate — but it is a power converter itself, so it needs cooling, filtering and maintenance.

Sizing an active filter: IAHF = √( Σh≥2 Ih² ) at the point of installation, plus margin (typically 20–25%) and any kvar duty you also assign to it. Never size an AHF from THD% alone — you must have the amps.
Design Rules That Prevent Rework
  • 1
    Mitigate at the source when the source is few and large; mitigate centrally when the sources are many and small. One AHF at the main board is usually cheaper than forty small filters.
  • 2
    Match the cure to the order. Triplen problem (3rd) → zig-zag transformer, delta-primary transformer, or active-PFC equipment. 5th/7th problem → reactors, tuned filter, 12-pulse, AHF. A 5th-harmonic filter does nothing for a 3rd-harmonic neutral fire.
  • 3
    Never install a plain capacitor bank in a plant with > 15–20% non-linear load. Specify detuned as the default; the reactor costs far less than one failure.
  • 4
    Model before you buy for anything above rung 3. A simple harmonic load-flow (or the manufacturer's study) checks resonance for every capacitor-stage combination, and for generator operation.
  • 5
    Verify afterwards. Contract acceptance should be a measured TDD/THDV at the PCC over a 7-day survey, not a datasheet claim.
  • 6
    Remember the transformer route. Sometimes the correct answer is a dedicated transformer for the dirty load — cheap, permanent segregation that also protects sensitive equipment from notching and transients.

Voltage Events, Unbalance & Flicker

Harmonics shorten life; voltage sags stop production today. This page is the one that saves money in a process plant.

Surveys of industrial power-quality complaints consistently find that voltage sags (dips) cause the majority of process interruptions, not harmonics. A sag lasting 100 ms — three grid cycles, invisible to a human, invisible to lighting — will drop a contactor, stall a drive on undervoltage, and reboot a PLC, costing a full changeover.

Sags: Where They Come From, and Why They Are Unavoidable

A fault anywhere on the network draws enormous current for the 60–150 ms it takes protection to clear. That current, flowing through shared source impedance, depresses the voltage at every connected customer — a voltage divider you do not own:

Vsag = Vnom · Zdownstream / (Zsource + Zfault path)
Consequence: sag depth depends on electrical distance to the fault, and the duration is set by the protection clearing time, not by you. You cannot prevent sags — you can only ride through them.

Sources

Remote network faults, lightning on overhead lines, large motor DOL starts, transformer energisation, autoreclose sequences (repeat sags seconds apart).

Victims (most→least sensitive)

PLC/IPC power supplies, contactors & relays (drop out at ~70% for 1–2 cycles), VFD undervoltage trips, discharge lighting, DC drives.

Rides through

Induction motors (inertia), resistive heating, incandescent lamps — they barely notice.

The ITIC / CBEMA Voltage-Tolerance Curve

The industry's shared language for "what should equipment survive?" — magnitude on the vertical axis, duration on the horizontal (log) axis. Inside the envelope: equipment should keep working. Outside: it is entitled to misbehave.

  • 1
    Buy to the curve. Specify in purchase orders that equipment must meet the ITIC (or IEC 61000-4-11/-4-34 immunity) envelope. Retro-fixing an intolerant machine is far more expensive.
  • 2
    Ride-through toolkit, cheapest first: undervoltage-tolerant DC control supplies (or a small DC UPS on the PLC), latching control circuits instead of hold-in contactors, drive "kinetic buffering"/flying-restart settings, then dynamic voltage restorers or full UPS/flywheel for critical lines.
  • 3
    Note the frequent single cheap win: the whole line stops because one 24 V control PSU browns out. A DIN-rail DC UPS on the control supply, plus latching logic, can convert a plant-wide stop into an unnoticed blip.
Voltage Unbalance
NEMA (simple, field-usable): %unbalance = max deviation from mean / mean × 100
IEC (true, sequence-based): u2 = |V2| / |V1| × 100%  — negative-sequence ratio
Motor rotor heating penalty ≈ 2 × (%unbalance)² percent extra loss.

Limits: EN 50160 allows up to 2% (3% in some cases) negative-sequence unbalance for 95% of a week; NEMA MG-1 wants motors derated above 1% and prohibits operation above 5%. Causes: uneven single-phase load distribution, one blown fuse in a capacitor bank, high-impedance connection, open delta. A 2% voltage unbalance can produce 6–10% current unbalance in an induction motor, because the negative-sequence impedance is close to the locked-rotor impedance.

Flicker — When the Eye Is the Instrument

Flicker is a perception phenomenon: repetitive voltage fluctuation that makes lighting shimmer and irritates people. IEC 61000-4-15 defines a flickermeter that models the lamp–eye–brain chain and outputs two indices:

Standards: IEEE 519 & IEC 61000

Two philosophies. IEEE 519 sets a contract at one point of common coupling. IEC 61000 builds a layered system of compatibility levels, emission limits and measurement methods.

The Two Architectures Side by Side

IEEE 519 (North American style)

One document, one interface: the Point of Common Coupling (PCC). Sets current limits on the user and voltage limits on the supplier, both statistically evaluated. Current limits scale with how stiff the supply is (ISC/IL).

IEC 61000 (European style)

A layered family: -2-x compatibility levels (what the environment shall be), -3-x emission limits (what a product may inject), -4-x measurement & immunity tests (how to prove it). Product-level control rather than a site contract.

Mental model: IEC controls the products so the environment stays clean; IEEE controls the site so the shared network stays clean. Serious projects use both — IEC-compliant equipment plus an IEEE 519 site study.

Where to Clamp — and Why It Decides the Answer

At the PCC / incoming board

The only place IEEE 519 compliance means anything. You see the net effect of all loads, including diversity and cancellation. Expect much lower THDI than at any single drive.

At an individual load

Where you diagnose a source, verify a filter, or gather spectra for a study. Expect high THDI — this is normal and not a failure.

On the neutral

Non-negotiable for any four-wire circuit with single-phase electronic load. This is where triplens hide, and where nobody looks.

On the capacitor bank

The fastest resonance test in existence: if the bank's RMS current substantially exceeds its rating (IEC 60831 allows ~1.3×) you have a resonance or an over-duty problem.

  • 1
    Mind the transducers. Protection-class CTs and especially capacitive voltage transformers have limited bandwidth and can be resonant themselves; iron-cored clamps saturate on high crest factor. For harmonics above ~the 25th, use metering-class CTs or Rogowski coils and check the VT's frequency response.
  • 2
    Burden and lead length on a CT secondary can shift phase and attenuate high orders — keep leads short, avoid extra converters in the chain.
  • 3
    Record voltage AND current together. Without both you cannot compute power, direction, or true PF — and you cannot tell whether the distortion you see is imported from the network or generated by your own load.
  • 4
    Harmonic direction: comparing the sign of harmonic active power (or comparing voltage/current spectra on either side of a boundary) tells you which side of the PCC the harmonic source lies on. Utilities and customers argue about exactly this.

Quick check: Your logger reports a maximum THDV of 14% during the week, and a 95th-percentile THDV of 4.1%. The limit is 8%. What do you write in the report?
Tap to reveal ↓A: You report compliant — the 95th-percentile value of 4.1% is the figure the standards test against, and it is well inside 8%. But a professional report does not stop there. Designing a Clean Installation · Recap

Power quality is cheapest at the design stage, expensive at commissioning, and ruinous after the fire. Here is what to build in from day one.

Design Rules for a New Installation
  • 1
    Estimate the non-linear fraction early. If > 20% of the load is rectifier-fed, budget for mitigation and harmonic derating in the transformer specification — not as a variation order later.
  • 2
    Segregate. Feed heavy converter loads from a dedicated transformer or a separate busbar section, keeping sensitive control, instrumentation and IT loads electrically distant from notching and distortion.
  • 3
    Size neutrals honestly. Full-size or 200% neutral for circuits feeding single-phase electronics; treat the circuit as four loaded conductors when derating; use separate neutrals per phase-group rather than one shared undersized neutral.
  • 4
    Specify equipment to standards, not adjectives. "IEC 61000-3-2/-3-12 compliant", "THDI < 5% at rated load", "ride-through per IEC 61000-4-11 / the ITIC envelope", "Class A metering at the PCC" — measurable clauses only.
  • 5
    Default to detuned capacitor banks (5.67% or 7%), rated for the elevated capacitor voltage, and interlocked to disconnect on generator operation.
  • 6
    Line reactors on every drive as standard practice — 3% minimum, 5% where the supply is stiff or the drive is large. Cheap insurance for both the network and the drive's DC bus.
  • 7
    Design the control circuits for sag immunity: DC control voltage with a small DIN-rail UPS, latched (not hold-in) logic, and drive undervoltage/kinetic-buffer settings tuned for ride-through instead of instant trip.
  • 8
    Instrument permanently. A fixed Class S/A analyser at the main incomer costs less than one unexplained line stoppage and turns future arguments into data. Trend THDV, TDD, true PF, neutral current and events.
  • 9
    Check the generator case in the study, not on the night of the black-start test. Lower SSC means higher voltage THD and a different resonance order.
  • 10
    Plan for PV / EV / storage. They change IL, add interharmonics and supraharmonics, and may push you onto IEEE 519's TRD route. Leave space and capacity for filtering.
Special Cases Worth Knowing

Standby generators

Subtransient reactance gives an SSC perhaps 4–8× lower than the grid, so the same harmonic current produces several times the voltage distortion. Genset sizing for a UPS/VFD load typically needs a 1.5–2× kVA oversize, and the AVR must be one that tolerates distorted current.

UPS systems

Double-conversion UPS with a 6-pulse rectifier is itself a harmonic source; modern IGBT-rectifier units achieve < 5% THDI. On the output, a UPS is a weak source — peaky loads can trigger current limit and force a bypass transfer.

EV chargers

Three-phase DC fast chargers are usually active-front-end and clean; cheap single-phase AC chargers can be 3rd-harmonic-rich. Add-on issue: EV charging on a PME/TN-C-S supply brings the open-PEN question (A44) — a separate hazard from PQ.

PV inverters & storage

Clean at the fundamental but contribute supraharmonics (2–150 kHz) and interharmonics, and can interact with other inverters. Reduces net IL, which tightens TDD arithmetic — the reason for IEEE 519-2022's DER decision tree.

The Eight Ideas That Carry Everything
  • 1
    Vh = Ih · Z(h). Your current distortion becomes everyone's voltage distortion across the shared impedance. Current is yours; voltage is shared.
  • 2
    Harmonics add in quadrature (Irms = √ΣIh²) — except zero-sequence triplens in the neutral, which add arithmetically.
  • 3
    Pulse number predicts the spectrum: h = pk ± 1, amplitude ≈ 1/h. Six-pulse gives 5, 7, 11, 13; single-phase gives 3, 5, 7, 9.
  • 4
    Sequence decides the damage: 5th (negative) brakes and heats rotors, 3rd (zero) burns neutrals, 7th (positive) mostly heats.
  • 5
    tPF = cos φ₁ / √(1+THDI²). Capacitors fix Q, never D. A load can be "in phase" and still have PF 0.6.
  • 6
    hr = √(SSC/QC). Every capacitor bank creates a resonance; detune it below the 5th and re-check it whenever the network changes.
  • 7
    Sags cause the stoppages. You cannot prevent them — buy ride-through to the ITIC envelope, and protect the control supply first.
  • 8
    Compliance is statistical. TDD (not THDI) at the PCC, 95th percentile over a week, on a Class A instrument, with flagging applied.
⚠️ Six Ways Power-Quality Work Goes Wrong
① Capacitors fitted "to fix the power factor"

Installed on a rectifier-heavy plant without a harmonic study. True PF barely improves (the deficit was D, not Q), and the bank creates a parallel resonance near the 5th or 7th. Symptoms: exploded cans, blown fuses, voltage THD spike. Cure: detuned banks as default; study before purchase.

② Undersized neutral in an electronics riser

Legacy 50% neutral carrying 3× the third-harmonic current at 150 Hz, with no protective device watching it. Discovered by thermography or by fire. Cure: full/200% neutral, rebalance, triplen trap, active-PFC equipment.

③ Averaging clamp meter used for survey

Reads 30–40% low on peaky current, so "loading is fine" while cables and transformers are overloaded. Cure: true-RMS instruments only; record crest factor.

④ Transformer loaded on nameplate kVA

Factor-K / K-factor derating never applied, so a "76% loaded" unit runs at its thermal limit and its life halves for every ~6–8 °C of extra hotspot. Cure: compute Factor-K from the measured spectrum; specify K-rated units for electronic loads.

⑤ Spot readings instead of a survey

A five-minute handheld snapshot at 10 a.m. declared "compliant" — missing the shift pattern, the weekend, the capacitor stages and the generator case. Cure: 7-day Class A/S survey with percentile statistics and flagging.

⑥ Wrong cure for the harmonic order

A 5th-harmonic tuned filter bought to solve a hot neutral (a 3rd-harmonic, zero-sequence problem), or an active filter installed while the real fault was voltage sags. Cure: measure the spectrum and the event log first; match mechanism to remedy.

Final check 1: Why does IEEE 519 give a customer with a very stiff supply (ISC/IL > 1000) permission to inject 20% TDD, while a customer on a weak feeder is limited to 5%?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because the harm is caused by voltage distortion, and Vh = Ih·Z(h). A very high ISC/IL means the customer is electrically small compared with the source — the network impedance seen by that customer is low, so even a large harmonic current produces little voltage distortion for anyone. On a weak feeder the same current would create severe voltage distortion for every neighbour. The standard is therefore not rewarding good behaviour; it is allocating a shared voltage-distortion budget in proportion to each user's ability to damage it. The same logic drives IEC 61000-3-12's RSCE banding and IEC/TR 61000-3-6's emission allocation for MV customers.
Final check 2: A plant measures THDI = 65% at the main incomer at 3 a.m. (weekend, minimal load) and 22% at 10 a.m. on a weekday. Which is the compliance problem?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Probably neither — and the 65% is almost certainly a measurement artefact of the ratio, not a real problem. THDI divides by the present fundamental, which at 3 a.m. is tiny (only a few standby loads running), so the percentage explodes while the actual harmonic amps may be trivial. This is precisely why IEEE 519 uses TDD, referenced to the fixed maximum demand current IL: computed that way, the 3 a.m. condition would show a very small TDD. Convert both readings into harmonic amps, divide by IL, take the weekly 95th percentile, and compare with the table. Practical corollary: never let anyone quote a light-load THDI as evidence of a harmonic problem — ask for amps.
Final check 3: You are asked to guarantee that a new production line will not be stopped by power quality. What can you honestly promise, and what can you not?
Tap to reveal ↓A: You can promise emission compliance and immunity design; you cannot promise the grid. Specifically, you can guarantee: (1) the line's own injection will meet a stated TDD/THDI at the PCC, verified by a 7-day Class A survey; (2) all equipment will meet a stated immunity envelope (IEC 61000-4-11/-4-34, ITIC), with the control supply on a DC UPS and latched logic so short dips do not stop production; (3) capacitor banks will be detuned and resonance-checked in every stage combination including generator operation; (4) neutrals, transformer Factor-K and cable derating will be sized for the measured spectrum. You cannot guarantee: the absence of network-side sags and interruptions, whose depth is set by fault location and whose duration is set by the utility's protection — those are outside your control, and the honest engineering answer is a documented ride-through specification plus, if the process value justifies it, a UPS/flywheel/DVR for the critical section. Writing that distinction into the contract before commissioning is what separates a professional scope from an argument.
Final check 4: In one sentence each, state what the 3rd, 5th and 7th harmonics respectively do to a plant.
Tap to reveal ↓A: 3rd (zero sequence): adds arithmetically in the neutral, so it overheats neutral conductors and transformer delta windings and produces no rotating field — the office/lighting/IT disease. 5th (negative sequence): creates a backward-rotating field that brakes and heats induction-motor rotors, biases unbalance relays, and is the harmonic most often trapped by filters — the factory-drive disease. 7th (positive sequence): creates a forward-rotating field that mostly adds heating, and — together with the 5th — produces the 300 Hz (6× line-frequency) torque pulsation that fatigues couplings and shows up in vibration spectra. Memory aid: 3rd burns the neutral, 5th brakes the motor, 7th heats and hums.
Final check 5: A colleague proposes solving a 9% voltage THD problem by installing an active harmonic filter at the main incomer. What single question tells you whether that will work?
Tap to reveal ↓A: "Is the distortion generated inside this plant, or imported from the network?" A shunt active filter cancels harmonic current drawn by your loads; if the 9% voltage THD is background distortion arriving from the utility (other customers, a nearby converter station, a weak feeder), the filter will spend its capacity chasing a voltage it cannot fix and the improvement will be disappointing. Determine the source by measuring on both sides of the PCC and checking harmonic power direction — or simply by observing THDV during a plant shutdown: if voltage THD stays high with your load off, it is imported. Imported distortion calls for different answers: a dedicated transformer and series reactance to reduce your exposure, tighter equipment immunity, a series active conditioner/DVR, or a formal complaint to the utility referenced to EN 50160 / IEEE 519's voltage table. Bonus: also check whether a capacitor bank is amplifying a modest background harmonic through resonance — if so, a detuning reactor costing a fraction of the filter may remove most of the 9%.

WE1 · THD, RMS Current & True Power Factor of a Drive

Given: A 400 V, 50 Hz six-pulse VFD draws a fundamental current I₁ = 100 A with measured harmonics I₅ = 25 A, I₇ = 12 A, I₁₁ = 7 A, I₁₃ = 5 A. Displacement power factor cos φ₁ = 0.98. Find THDI, Irms, true PF, kW, kVA and the distortion power D.

Step 1 — harmonic RMS (quadrature sum):
IH = √(25² + 12² + 7² + 5²) = √(625 + 144 + 49 + 25) = √843 = 29.03 A

Step 2 — THDI:
THDI = 29.03 / 100 = 0.2903 = 29.0%

Step 3 — total RMS current:
Irms = √(100² + 843) = √10 843 = 104.13 A  (check: 100·√(1+0.2903²) = 100·1.0413 ✓)

Step 4 — apparent power:
S = √3 · VLL · Irms = 1.732 × 400 × 104.13 = 72.14 kVA

Step 5 — real power (only the fundamental carries average power):
P = √3 · VLL · I1 · cos φ1 = 1.732 × 400 × 100 × 0.98 = 67.88 kW

Step 6 — true power factor:
tPF = P/S = 67.88 / 72.14 = 0.941  (check: 0.98/1.0413 = 0.941 ✓)

Step 7 — the three power components:
Q1 = √3·V·I1·sin φ1 = 1.732 × 400 × 100 × 0.199 = 13.79 kvar
D = √3·V·IH = 1.732 × 400 × 29.03 = 20.11 kvar (distortion)
Verify: √(67.88² + 13.79² + 20.11²) = √(4608 + 190 + 404) = √5202 = 72.1 kVA ✓

Discussion / insight. Note the hierarchy of the deficits: the distortion power D (20.1 kvar) is larger than the reactive power Q (13.8 kvar). If someone "corrects the power factor" with a 14 kvar capacitor, they will cancel Q and lift the PF only to about 0.955 — and they will have installed a capacitor into a network with 25 A of 5th harmonic, inviting resonance. The honest reading is that this load's kVA burden is dominated by distortion, so the correct engineering response is a line reactor (which would cut I₅ substantially) or a filter, not capacitance. Also note the deceptive smallness of the RMS increase: 29% THD raised the current only 4.1%, but the extra loss is I²-weighted and deposited at 250–650 Hz where skin effect raises R.

WE2 · IEEE 519 Compliance Assessment at the PCC

Given: A factory is supplied by a 1000 kVA, 400 V, Z = 5% transformer. Maximum demand current IL = 600 A. The weekly 95th-percentile measurements at the PCC are: I₅ = 42 A, I₇ = 27 A, I₁₁ = 19 A, I₁₃ = 12 A, I₁₇ = 7 A, I₁₉ = 5 A. Assess compliance with IEEE 519-2022.

Step 1 — transformer full-load current:
IFL = S / (√3·V) = 1 000 000 / (1.732 × 400) = 1443 A

Step 2 — short-circuit current at the PCC:
ISC = IFL / (Z as fraction) = 1443 / 0.05 = 28 868 A  (≈ 20 MVA of short-circuit power)

Step 3 — stiffness ratio:
ISC/IL = 28 868 / 600 = 48.1 → falls in the 20–50 band

Step 4 — applicable limits (% of IL = 600 A):
h < 11 → 7.0% = 42.0 A  ·  11 ≤ h < 17 → 3.5% = 21.0 A  ·  17 ≤ h < 23 → 2.5% = 15.0 A  ·  TDD → 8.0%

Step 5 — compare each harmonic:
I₅ = 42 A = 7.00% → at the limit (marginal fail/pass — no margin)
I₇ = 27 A = 4.50% → limit 7.0% ✓
I₁₁ = 19 A = 3.17% → limit 3.5% ✓ (tight)
I₁₃ = 12 A = 2.00% → limit 3.5% ✓
I₁₇ = 7 A = 1.17% → limit 2.5% ✓  ·  I₁₉ = 5 A = 0.83% → limit 2.5% ✓

Step 6 — TDD:
Σ Ih² = 42² + 27² + 19² + 12² + 7² + 5² = 1764 + 729 + 361 + 144 + 49 + 25 = 3072
√3072 = 55.43 A → TDD = 55.43 / 600 = 9.24%  vs limit 8.0% → NON-COMPLIANT

Discussion / insight. The plant fails on TDD by 1.24 percentage points, and the 5th harmonic sits exactly on its individual limit. Two lessons an examiner is looking for. First, the arithmetic order matters: had the ratio been 50.5 instead of 48.1 (a slightly larger transformer, or a slightly smaller demand), the applicable row would have been 50–100 with a 12% TDD limit and the same measurements would have passed comfortably. Compliance is band-sensitive, so the ISC/IL calculation must be documented and defensible. Second, the cheapest route to compliance is the 5th: it contributes 1764 of the 3072 total, i.e. 57% of the sum of squares. Halving I₅ to 21 A drops Σ to 1749, giving TDD = √1749/600 = 6.97% — compliant with margin, and achievable with reactors on the largest drives rather than a plant-wide filter. Attack the dominant term, not the whole spectrum.

WE3 · Resonance Check & Detuning Design

Given: The same 1000 kVA / 5% transformer (SSC ≈ 20 MVA). The plant wants power-factor correction: 400 kvar total in four 100 kvar stages. Check resonance for every stage combination, then design a detuned bank. Also check the case on a 500 kVA standby generator with subtransient reactance X″d = 15%.

Step 1 — short-circuit power:
SSC = Stx/Z = 1000/0.05 = 20 000 kVA

Step 2 — resonant order per stage combination, hr = √(SSC/QC):
100 kvar → √(20 000/100) = √200 = 14.1 (near 13th ⚠)
200 kvar → √100 = 10.0 (near 11th ⚠)
300 kvar → √66.7 = 8.16 (between 7th and 11th — the safest of the four)
400 kvar → √50 = 7.07 (ON the 7th ⚠⚠ worst case)

Step 3 — verdict on the plain bank: unacceptable. Three of four combinations sit on or beside a harmonic the plant's six-pulse drives generate strongly. Full correction lands exactly on the 7th.

Step 4 — detuned design, p = 7%:
ht = 1/√p = 1/√0.07 = 3.78 → the bank's series resonance is below the 5th, and the parallel resonance with the network is pushed below any significant harmonic. Safe for all stage combinations.

Step 5 — capacitor voltage rise (why standard capacitors fail here):
VC = Vline / (1 − p) = 400 / 0.93 = 430 V → specify capacitors rated ≥ 480 V (with margin for tolerance, harmonics and overvoltage), and note the bank's delivered kvar also rises by 1/(1−p), so a "100 kvar" capacitor behind a 7% reactor delivers ≈ 107 kvar at the busbar.

Step 6 — generator case:
SSC,gen = 500/0.15 = 3333 kVA → with 400 kvar in: hr = √(3333/400) = √8.33 = 2.89 → near the 3rd harmonic. With 100 kvar in: √33.3 = 5.77 → near the 5th/7th. Both dangerous.

Discussion / insight. Three exam-grade takeaways. (1) A multi-stage bank does not have a resonance — it has one per switching combination, and the tabulation above is a mandatory deliverable of any PF-correction design in a plant with converters. (2) The full-correction case is often the worst, which is counter-intuitive: the more capacitance, the lower the resonant order, so the bank walks down the harmonic ladder toward the biggest harmonics (7th, then 5th). (3) The generator case is the trap: SSC collapses by 6×, so hr falls by √6 ≈ 2.4×, and even a detuned bank should normally be interlocked off during generator operation — the genset's AVR also dislikes leading power factor and can go unstable. Standard practice: detune at 5.67% or 7%, rate capacitors for the elevated voltage, tabulate every combination, and interlock on transfer to standby.

WE4 · Neutral Sizing and Transformer Derating in an IT Riser

Given: A 400/230 V riser feeds a data-hall floor: per-phase fundamental 180 A, with I₃ = 45% of fundamental, I₅ = 22%, I₇ = 12%, I₉ = 9%, I₁₁ = 6%. The feeder cable has a 50% neutral. The supply is a standard 500 kVA transformer. Assess the neutral, the phase RMS, and the transformer's usable capacity.

Step 1 — harmonic amps per phase:
I₃ = 0.45×180 = 81 A · I₅ = 39.6 A · I₇ = 21.6 A · I₉ = 16.2 A · I₁₁ = 10.8 A

Step 2 — phase RMS:
Σ Ih² = 81² + 39.6² + 21.6² + 16.2² + 10.8² = 6561 + 1568 + 467 + 262 + 117 = 8975
Irms = √(180² + 8975) = √(32 400 + 8975) = √41 375 = 203.4 A
THDI = √8975 / 180 = 94.7 / 180 = 52.6%

Step 3 — neutral current (triplens add arithmetically, others cancel):
IN = 3·√(I₃² + I₉²) = 3·√(6561 + 262) = 3 × 82.6 = 247.8 A

Step 4 — neutral verdict:
Neutral rating = 50% × 180 A ≈ 90 A. Actual = 247.8 A → 2.75× overload, unprotected, at 150 Hz.
Also note IN/Iphase = 247.8/203.4 = 1.22 — the neutral is the most heavily loaded conductor in the cable.

Step 5 — transformer Factor-K (IEC/EN 60076-1), with e = 0.05, q = 1.7:
Σ h1.7(Ih/I₁)²:  31.7=6.47 × 0.45² = 1.310  ·  51.7=15.4 × 0.22² = 0.745
71.7=27.3 × 0.12² = 0.393  ·  91.7=41.9 × 0.09² = 0.339  ·  111.7=59.2 × 0.06² = 0.213
Σ = 3.000  ·  (e/(1+e)) = 0.05/1.05 = 0.0476  ·  (I₁/Irms)² = (180/203.4)² = 0.783
K = √(1 + 0.0476 × 0.783 × 3.000) = √1.1118 = 1.054
Usable capacity = 500 / 1.054 = 474 kVA (i.e. derate about 5%)

Discussion / insight. The two answers point in different directions, and understanding why is the whole lesson. The neutral is catastrophically overloaded (2.75×) while the transformer needs only a modest 6.5% derating. The reason is the different weighting each victim applies: the neutral cares about the arithmetic sum of triplen amps (weight ∝ h⁰, so the 3rd dominates completely), whereas the transformer's eddy loss cares about hq-weighted content, which spreads the burden across orders and is diluted by the fact that only a fraction (e) of the loss is eddy loss. So a triplen-rich load is primarily a conductor and connection problem, and only secondarily a transformer problem — the opposite of a 5th/7th-rich drive load, which stresses the transformer and the motors while leaving the neutral quiet. Practical actions here: replace the neutral with a full-size (ideally 200%) conductor and re-derate the cable as four loaded conductors, thermographically survey every neutral termination in the riser (A41), rebalance phases, and specify active-PFC PSUs so the 45% third is never generated in the first place. Then, and only then, revisit the transformer.

P2. A 6-pulse drive has cos φ₁ = 0.96 and THDI = 38%. What is its true power factor, and how much kVA does a 150 kW mechanical load at 92% motor efficiency demand?
Tap to reveal ↓A: tPF = 0.96/√(1+0.38²) = 0.96/√1.1444 = 0.96/1.0698 = 0.897. Electrical input power P = 150/0.92 ≈ 163 kW (ignoring drive losses; add ~2–3% for the drive itself → ≈ 167 kW). S = P/tPF = 167/0.897 = 186 kVA. Insight: a naive designer sizing "150 kW → 160 kVA" underestimates by ~16%; the transformer, cables and switchgear must carry the distortion current too. P3. A 1600 kVA, 415 V, Z = 6% transformer supplies a plant with maximum demand 1100 A. Which IEEE 519 limit row applies, and what is the TDD limit?
Tap to reveal ↓A: IFL = 1 600 000/(1.732×415) = 2226 A. ISC = 2226/0.06 = 37 100 A. Ratio = 37 100/1100 = 33.7 → the 20–50 band: individual limits 7.0 / 3.5 / 2.5 / 1.0 / 0.5%, TDD limit 8.0%. In amps, the 5th may not exceed 0.07 × 1100 = 77 A and the total harmonic current may not exceed 0.08 × 1100 = 88 A. Trap avoided: the limits are percentages of IL = 1100 A (the demand), not of IFL = 2226 A (the transformer) — using the wrong denominator would double your apparent allowance.
P4. A 2000 kVA / 6% transformer feeds a busbar with 600 kvar of plain capacitors. Find the resonant harmonic order. Is it safe in a plant full of six-pulse drives? What detuning would you specify?

That is dangerously close to the 7th (and the bank's partial stages will be worse or better in turn — check them all: 300 kvar → √111 = 10.5, near the 11th; 450 kvar → √74 = 8.6, acceptable). Verdict: not safe. Specify a 5.67% detuned bank (tuned order 4.2) or 7% (3.78); with the reactor, capacitors must be rated for 415/0.943 ≈ 440 V (5.67%) → specify ≥ 480 V units, and remember the delivered kvar rises by 1/(1−p) ≈ 6%, so the bank must be re-rated to hit the target compensation.
P5. Three phases each carry 150 A fundamental with 38% third harmonic and 10% ninth. Compute the neutral current. If the cable's neutral is rated 100% (150 A), is it adequate?
Tap to reveal ↓A: I₃ = 57 A, I₉ = 15 A. IN = 3·√(57² + 15²) = 3·√(3249+225) = 3 × 58.94 = 176.8 A. The 100%-rated neutral (150 A) is overloaded by 18% — not catastrophic, but with no protective device on the neutral and skin effect at 150/450 Hz, it will run hotter than the phases and its terminations will age. Correct answers: use a 200% neutral (or two neutrals), derate the cable as four loaded conductors, and note that the phase RMS here is √(22 500+3474) = 161 A, so the neutral exceeds the phase current — the classic inversion of the textbook rule.
P6. Measured line voltages are 405, 396 and 387 V. Compute NEMA percentage unbalance and estimate the extra rotor heating in an induction motor. Is derating required?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Mean = (405+396+387)/3 = 396 V. Maximum deviation = |405−396| = 9 V (and |387−396| = 9 V). %unbalance = 9/396 × 100 = 2.27%. Extra rotor heating ≈ 2 × (2.27)² = ≈ 10.3% additional loss. Verdict: derating required — NEMA MG-1 calls for derating above 1% unbalance (roughly a 0.95–0.96 factor at ~2.3%), and this level also exceeds EN 50160's 2% expectation for the supply. Investigate the cause first: uneven single-phase load distribution, a high-resistance joint, or one failed capacitor element. Note the mechanism: unbalance is negative-sequence voltage, whose impedance in the motor is near locked-rotor impedance, so a 2.3% voltage unbalance can produce 6–10× that in current unbalance.
P7. A plant's measured spectrum (as fractions of I₁) is I₅ = 0.30, I₇ = 0.18, I₁₁ = 0.09, I₁₃ = 0.06. Size a shunt active harmonic filter if I₁ = 800 A. What rating do you order?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Harmonic amps: I₅ = 240, I₇ = 144, I₁₁ = 72, I₁₃ = 48 A. IAHF = √(240²+144²+72²+48²) = √(57 600+20 736+5184+2304) = √85 824 = 293 A. Add 20–25% margin for load growth, unmeasured higher orders and any kvar duty you assign to the unit → order approximately 350–370 A of harmonic-current capability. Two traps: (1) never size an AHF from THD% — the units of an AHF rating are amps of harmonic current, not kvar and not a percentage; (2) check the filter's bandwidth covers your dominant orders (most cover to the 50th, but verify the full-rated performance at the 5th, since capability often falls off with order). Cheaper alternative to price alongside: reactors on the largest drives could remove much of the 240 A of 5th, shrinking the AHF you need.
P8. A 500 kVA transformer supplies a load whose spectrum gives Factor-K = 1.42. The connected load is 400 kVA. Comment, and state two remedies with their trade-offs.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Usable capacity = 500/1.42 = 352 kVA, but 400 kVA is connected → the transformer is loaded to 114% of its harmonic-corrected rating even though it reads 80% on a conventional kVA meter. Expect elevated hotspot temperature, accelerated insulation ageing (roughly halving life per 6–8 °C of excess hotspot) and possible top-oil/winding-temperature alarms. Remedy 1: replace with a K-rated unit (e.g. K-13) or a larger standard unit — certain, capital-intensive, and it fixes nothing upstream or in the cables. Remedy 2: reduce the harmonics — reactors/filters/AFE at the dominant sources, which also relieves cables, the neutral and the capacitor bank and improves true PF, but requires a study and may not achieve the full reduction needed. Best practice is usually a combination: mitigate the two largest sources, re-measure the spectrum, recompute Factor-K, and then size the replacement transformer on the improved figure rather than the present one.
P9. On grid supply a plant measures THDV = 3.2%. On its 800 kVA standby generator (X″d = 14%) the same load gives THDV = 11.8%. The grid transformer is 1250 kVA at 5%. Explain quantitatively, and state two actions.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Voltage distortion follows Vh = Ih·Z(h), so it scales inversely with source strength. Grid: SSC = 1250/0.05 = 25 000 kVA. Generator: SSC = 800/0.14 = 5714 kVA — a factor of 4.4× weaker. With the same harmonic currents, expected THDV ≈ 3.2% × 4.4 = 14%, which brackets the measured 11.8% nicely (the generator's impedance is not perfectly proportional at all harmonic orders, and some load drops off on transfer). Verdict: this is normal physics, not a generator fault — but 11.8% exceeds the usual 8% LV planning level, so equipment is at risk during every outage. Actions: (1) oversize the genset (a common rule for converter-heavy loads is 1.5–2× the kVA, i.e. specify by allowable voltage THD, not just by kW) and specify an AVR/PMG excitation tolerant of distorted current; (2) reduce harmonic current at source (reactors/12-pulse/AFE on the biggest drives) and interlock capacitor stages off during generator running — recompute hr = √(5714/QC), which for even 200 kvar gives 5.3, right on the 5th. Optionally shed non-essential converter load on transfer.
P10. Synthesis. You are handed a plant with: THDV = 6.8%, TDD = 11% against an 8% limit, a hot neutral in the office block, capacitor fuses failing monthly, three unexplained line stops a month, and a transformer running at 78% kVA with a temperature alarm. Produce a prioritised action plan with justification and the measurement you would use to close each item.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Recognise three distinct problems sharing one root cause, plus one that does not belong to harmonics at all. Priority 1 — safety: the hot neutral. Triplen (3rd) overload with no protection; a fire risk. Measure neutral true-RMS current and spectrum, plus an IR survey of terminations (A41). Fix: rebalance, full/200% neutral, re-derate cable as four loaded conductors; specify active-PFC PSUs going forward. Priority 2 — the capacitor bank. Monthly fuse failures indicate parallel resonance; compute hr = √(SSC/QC) for every stage combination and measure bank RMS current against its rating (IEC 60831 ≈ 1.3× limit). Fix: detune at 5.67%/7% with correctly voltage-rated capacitors and a generator interlock. This step alone often cuts THDV materially, because resonance was amplifying the distortion. Priority 3 — TDD compliance (11% vs 8%). Confirm with a 7-day Class A survey at the PCC (95th percentile, flagging applied) and establish IL from 12 months of demand data. Then attack the dominant order — usually the 5th, which typically contributes over half of ΣIh²: 5% line reactors on the largest drives first, re-measure, and only then price a central active filter for the residual, sized in harmonic amps. Priority 4 — the transformer. Compute Factor-K from the measured spectrum; the 78% kVA reading is meaningless without it. Note that Priorities 2 and 3 will reduce the harmonic content and hence the Factor-K, so re-evaluate before buying a transformer — you may not need one. Priority 5 — the three line stops. These are almost certainly not harmonics: they are voltage sags. Log half-cycle RMS with event capture, plot events on the ITIC curve, and separate "supply outside the envelope" (network problem → ride-through investment) from "events inside the envelope" (equipment immunity problem → DC UPS on control supplies, latched logic, drive undervoltage/kinetic-buffer settings). The professional point: sequence the work so that each step's measurement re-informs the next, and never buy the big-ticket item (transformer or active filter) until the cheap structural fixes — rebalancing, detuning, reactors, immunity — have been made and re-measured.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations you should be able to produce from memory, a symbol glossary, and the seven mistakes that cost marks and money.

Derivation 1 · True PF = cos φ₁ / √(1 + THDI²)
Start from definitions. True power factor is real power over apparent power:
tPF = P / S

Step 1 — real power. Average power over a cycle is the sum of per-harmonic contributions, P = Σh Vh·Ih·cos φh. But cross-frequency products average to zero, so only harmonics present in both V and I contribute. If the supply voltage is nearly sinusoidal (Vh≥2 ≈ 0):
P ≈ V1·I1·cos φ1  — the harmonic currents carry no average power at all.

Step 2 — apparent power. By definition S = Vrms·Irms. With Vrms ≈ V1:
S ≈ V1·Irms, and Irms = √(I1² + Σh≥2Ih²)

Step 3 — factor out I1. Since THDI = √(Σh≥2Ih²)/I1, we have Σh≥2Ih² = (THDI·I1)², so:
Irms = I1·√(1 + THDI²)

Step 4 — divide.
tPF = (V1I1cos φ1) / (V1·I1√(1+THDI²)) = cos φ1 / √(1 + THDI²) ∎

What the derivation teaches. The result factorises into displacement factor × distortion factor, and the two are physically independent: cos φ₁ is about timing at the fundamental, the second factor is about extra current at frequencies the voltage doesn't have. That is precisely why a capacitor — a fundamental-frequency device — can only ever move the first factor. It also shows the hidden assumption: if voltage distortion is not small (say THDV > 8%), the approximation Vrms ≈ V₁ degrades and harmonic power terms VhIhcos φh become non-negligible — those terms can even be negative, meaning harmonic power flows out of the load back into the network, which is exactly how you identify the harmonic source at a boundary.

Derivation 2 · Why Triplens Add in the Neutral (and Others Cancel)
Setup. Balanced three-phase currents, harmonic order h, amplitude Ih:
ia,h(t) = √2 Ih sin(hωt)
ib,h(t) = √2 Ih sin(h(ωt − 120°)) = √2 Ih sin(hωt − h·120°)
ic,h(t) = √2 Ih sin(hωt − h·240°)

Kirchhoff at the star point: iN = ia + ib + ic

Case A — h not a multiple of 3. The three phase angles 0, −h·120°, −h·240° reduce (mod 360°) to a permutation of {0°, −120°, −240°}: still three vectors 120° apart. Three equal vectors at 120° sum to zero:
1 + e−j120° + e−j240° = 0 → iN = 0
(Whether the order is a-b-c or a-c-b decides positive vs negative sequence, i.e. forward or backward rotation — but either way the neutral sees nothing.)

Case B — h = 3k (triplens). Then h·120° = 3k·120° = k·360° ≡ 0°, and likewise h·240° ≡ 0°. All three harmonics are exactly in phase:
iN = 3·√2 Ih sin(hωt) → IN = 3·Ih ∎

Generalising over several triplens (they are different frequencies, hence orthogonal):
IN = 3·√(I3² + I9² + I15² + …)

What the derivation teaches. The neutral current is 3× the zero-sequence component — which is exactly the symmetrical-components statement IN = 3I₀. Two corollaries follow immediately and are examinable. (1) A three-wire system (delta load, or a 3φ rectifier with no neutral) physically cannot conduct zero-sequence current, so it produces no triplens at all — which is why VFD plants have a 5th/7th problem and office blocks have a 3rd problem. (2) In a delta winding the triplens have a closed loop available and therefore circulate inside the delta, heating it while remaining invisible from outside — the basis of the Dyn11 transformer as a triplen trap, and a reason delta windings in harmonic-rich installations should be thermally checked.

Derivation 3 · Resonant Order hr = √(SSC/QC)
Setup. Looking into the busbar, the supply is inductive (XL1 at 50 Hz) and the capacitor bank is capacitive (XC1 at 50 Hz). At harmonic h:
XL(h) = h·XL1   (∝ frequency)  ·  XC(h) = XC1/h   (∝ 1/frequency)

Step 1 — resonance condition. Resonance occurs where the two reactances are equal in magnitude:
hr·XL1 = XC1/hr  →  hr² = XC1/XL1  →  hr = √(XC1/XL1)

Step 2 — express both reactances in power terms at nominal voltage V:
Capacitor: QC = V²/XC1  →  XC1 = V²/QC
Supply: SSC = V²/XL1 (the short-circuit power, since XL1 is the source reactance)  →  XL1 = V²/SSC

Step 3 — substitute.
hr = √( (V²/QC) / (V²/SSC) ) = √( SSC / QC ) ∎

Corollary — the transformer form. With SSC = Stx/Z (Z as a fraction):
hr = √( Stx / (Z · QC) )

Corollary — detuned bank. Adding a reactor with p = XL,reactor/XC1 gives the series-tuned order
ht = 1/√p  (p = 0.07 → ht = 3.78), and the capacitor voltage rises to V/(1−p).

What the derivation teaches. The elegance is that the voltage cancels: you never need to know the actual reactances, only two ratings you already have on the nameplates. Three insights fall out. (1) More capacitance lowers hr (QC in the denominator), so as a staged bank switches in, the resonance walks down toward the large 7th and 5th harmonics — the fully-corrected case is often the worst. (2) A stronger supply raises hr, so a transformer upgrade or a parallel transformer moves your resonance — this is why "it worked for years" is not evidence of safety. (3) On a generator, SSC collapses, so hr falls by roughly √(ratio of short-circuit powers), which is why capacitor stages should be interlocked out during standby operation.

⚠️ Seven Exam & Field Traps
  • 1
    Adding harmonics arithmetically. Irms = √(ΣIh²), not ΣIh. The single exception is zero-sequence triplens in the neutral, which really do add arithmetically (×3) because they are in phase. Know which rule applies where — examiners test exactly this boundary.
  • 2
    THDI quoted as TDD. At light load THDI explodes while the harmonic amps are trivial. Compliance uses TDD (÷ IL). Conversely, a low THDI at full load can still breach TDD if the plant's demand is small relative to the transformer.
  • 3
    Wrong denominator for the IEEE 519 limits. Limits are percentages of IL (max demand), not of transformer full-load current, and the band comes from ISC/IL. Also: the LV voltage THD limit is 8%, not 5% — 5% applies from 1 kV to 69 kV.
  • 4
    Capacitors "fix" power factor. They fix Q only. If the deficit is D (distortion), the PF barely improves and you have added a resonant element. Always check hr = √(SSC/QC) for every stage combination and on the generator. And never put capacitors on a VFD output.
  • 5
    K-factor vs Factor-K. K-factor (UL) is a rating you specify for the transformer; Factor-K (IEC) is a divisor you use to derate an ordinary transformer. Using one where the other belongs inverts the answer. Remember Factor-K weights each harmonic by hq (q ≈ 1.7), so high orders punish transformers disproportionately.
  • 6
    Maxima instead of percentiles. Every limit is statistical — weekly 95th percentile (and daily 99th for very-short-time values), with flagged intervals excluded per IEC 61000-4-30. Quoting the week's maximum as a failure, or a spot reading as a pass, is not an engineering statement.
  • 7
    Assuming all "dirty power" is harmonics. Most production stoppages are voltage sags, an event phenomenon that no harmonic filter addresses. Separate the steady-state problem (harmonics, unbalance, PF) from the event problem (sags, swells, transients) on the very first page of any report — and check whether distortion is generated in-plant or imported, because a shunt active filter cannot fix imported voltage distortion.

Eight-Step Hands-On Ladder
  • 1
    Measure your own home or office board. Clamp a true-RMS meter with harmonic function on a lighting circuit and on the neutral of a socket circuit. Record THDI, crest factor and the 3rd-harmonic percentage. You will find the theory of Page 4 in your own building.
  • 2
    Compare two meters on the same conductor — an averaging clamp and a true-RMS clamp on a peaky load. Quantify the error. This single experiment inoculates you against a career of bad survey data.
  • 3
    Simulate a six-pulse bridge in LTspice/PSIM/Python, take the FFT of the line current, and verify h = 6k ± 1 and Ih ≈ I₁/h. Then add a 3% and a 5% line reactor and re-measure THDI — you should reproduce the ladder on Page 8.
  • 4
    Compute your plant's real numbers. From the transformer nameplate and 12 months of demand data, work out IFL, ISC, IL, ISC/IL and hence the applicable IEEE 519 row. Write it on the single-line diagram. Most plants have never done this.
  • 5
    Tabulate resonance for every capacitor stage using hr = √(SSC/QC) — grid and generator cases. Flag any combination within ±10% of the 5th, 7th, 11th or 13th. Take the table to whoever owns the bank.
  • 6
    Run a 7-day survey at a main incomer with a Class S/A logger: 10-min aggregation, event capture at 90%/110%. Produce percentile statistics, not maxima, and plot the events on an ITIC curve.
  • 7
    Compute Factor-K for a real transformer from a real measured spectrum, then compare its usable kVA with its actual loading. Cross-check with an infrared survey (A41) and, if it is a motor-heavy board, a vibration check for 300 Hz content (A42).
  • 8
    Write a two-page recommendation for one real problem: measured vs limit, mechanism, three costed options, and the acceptance test that would close it. Being able to do this is the difference between knowing power quality and practising it.
