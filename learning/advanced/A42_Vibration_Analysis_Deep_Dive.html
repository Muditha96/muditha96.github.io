Why Vibration Is the Earliest Warning

On the P-F curve, vibration speaks first — months before heat, noise or smoke.

Definition

Vibration analysis is the measurement and interpretation of a machine's oscillatory motion to detect, diagnose and trend developing faults — which fault, how severe, and how fast it is progressing.

Every rotating element — shaft, rotor, bearing, gear, impeller — generates forces at frequencies fixed by its geometry and speed. A fault changes those forces in a characteristic way. Because frequency identifies the source and amplitude scales with severity, a vibration spectrum is a fingerprint: unbalance cannot masquerade as a bearing defect, and a gear problem cannot hide at shaft speed.

The P-F Curve — where vibration sits
The P-F curve: a bearing defect is measurable in vibration (P) months before failure (F). Each later symptom shortens your planning window — vibration buys the longest lead time of any CM technology.

This is why vibration is the backbone of predictive maintenance (→ A23 TPM): the point P where a fault first becomes detectable arrives earliest in the vibration spectrum. Thermal imaging (→ A41) and oil analysis confirm and refine; vibration finds.

Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration

One motion, three views — and the frequency decides which one you should measure.

The core relations

For sinusoidal motion at frequency f, the three amplitudes are locked together by ω = 2πf: each differentiation multiplies by ω and advances phase by 90°.

x(t) = X sin(ωt) displacement [µm] v(t) = ωX cos(ωt) velocity [mm/s] (leads x by 90°) a(t) = −ω²X sin(ωt) acceleration [m/s² or g] (leads x by 180°) peak relations: V = ωX A = ωV = ω²X with ω = 2πf

The ω² term is the whole story: at low frequency, displacement is large but acceleration tiny; at high frequency, a microscopic displacement produces violent acceleration. A 100 µm wobble at 10 Hz is only 0.4 g — but 1 µm at 5 kHz is ~100 g. That is why bearing defects (high f) live in acceleration and shaft problems (low f) in displacement or velocity.

Which parameter for which band
Parameter selection by band. Velocity is the general-purpose severity parameter because typical machine faults (10–1000 Hz) produce roughly constant velocity for equal damage energy — which is why ISO 20816 limits are in mm/s RMS.
Amplitude descriptors
RMS = 0.707 × peak (sine only!) peak-to-peak = 2 × peak (used for displacement / prox probes) crest factor CF = peak / RMS — sine: 1.414 · impacting bearing: 3…>5

RMS measures energy content and is the ISO severity descriptor. Peak responds to short impacts that barely move RMS — so a rising crest factor with steady RMS is a classic early-bearing-damage flag: impacts have begun, but the energy is still small. When RMS finally rises and CF falls back, damage has spread — the spikes have merged into broadband roughness.

Sensors & Mounting

The best analyser cannot fix a bad measurement point — mounting sets your usable bandwidth.

The workhorse: piezoelectric accelerometer

A seismic mass on a piezoelectric crystal: acceleration → force (F = ma) → charge. IEPE / ICP types integrate a charge amplifier and run on a 2–20 mA constant-current supply over the same coax that carries the signal.

Typical general-purpose spec: 100 mV/g sensitivity, ±80 g range, 0.5 Hz–10 kHz (±3 dB), mounted resonance ~25 kHz. Below its resonance the response is flat; the usable band is roughly ⅓ of the mounted resonance frequency. Sensor choice trades sensitivity against bandwidth: 500 mV/g seismic units for low-speed machines, 10 mV/g for gearboxes with high-g content.

sensor family: accelerometer (IEPE) casing vibration — 95% of CM work velocity sensor self-generating coil-magnet; rugged, 10–1500 Hz, no power proximity probe eddy-current, non-contact shaft relative displacement — journal-bearing turbomachines (API 670)
Mounting — the hidden filter
Mounting resonance falls as coupling stiffness drops: stud > adhesive > magnet > hand-held probe. Above ~⅓ of the mounting resonance, amplitude readings are unreliable — a hand-held stinger cannot see bearing frequencies.

Rules that survive the plant floor: same point, same direction, every survey (mark it with a paint dot or install pads); measure horizontal, vertical and axial at each bearing; load the machine consistently; flat, clean, bare-metal contact; never trust a magnet mount above ~2 kHz on rough paint.

The FFT & Analyser Setup

The FFT turns time waveform into spectrum — but only a correct setup makes the spectrum honest.

Time domain → frequency domain
One accelerometer signal contains every source at once. The FFT separates them by frequency so each component can be identified and trended individually.
Δf = Fmax / LOR bin width = resolution T = LOR / Fmax time to acquire ONE average N_samples = 2.56 × LOR (analysers sample at 2.56×Fmax for anti-alias margin)
The setup decisions that matter
  • 1
    Fmax — set by what you must see. Rule of thumb: ≥ 3.25× the highest fault frequency of interest (gear mesh + sidebands, or ~50× shaft speed for bearing work).
  • 2
    LOR — set by what you must separate. To split two peaks Δ apart you need Δf ≤ Δ/2, ideally Δ/4. Sidebands at ±1× around GMF on a slow shaft demand high LOR.
  • 3
    Window: Hanning for routine CM (amplitude error ≤ ~16%, corrected by the analyser); flat-top for calibration accuracy; uniform only for true transients/bump tests.
  • 4
    Averaging: 4–8 linear averages stabilises the spectrum against random content; overlap 50% cuts acquisition time.
  • 5
    Anti-aliasing is non-negotiable — the analyser low-pass filters above Fmax before sampling (Nyquist: f_s > 2·f_max). Aliased peaks appear at false frequencies and cannot be diagnosed.

Fault Signatures I — The Synchronous Family

Faults tied to shaft speed: unbalance, misalignment, looseness, bent shaft, resonance.

Reading a spectrum in orders

Analysts think in orders — multiples of running speed (1× = shaft frequency). Synchronous faults sit exactly on integer orders; bearing and gear faults do not. Normalising to orders makes the pattern speed-independent.

The four classic synchronous signatures. Confirm with direction (radial vs axial), phase, and the time waveform — never from one peak alone.

Resonance test: a bump test (uniform window, peak-hold) reveals f_n with the machine off; a coast-down shows the 1× peak swelling as it sweeps through f_n. Fix by shifting stiffness/mass (move f_n) or slowing the exciter — not by balancing harder. Separation margin: keep forcing frequencies ≥ 15–20% away from f_n.

Fault Signatures II — Bearings, Gears, Motors

Non-synchronous frequencies: the geometry-locked tones that name the failing part.

Rolling-bearing defect frequencies

Each defect location generates a tone fixed by bearing geometry — n rolling elements, ball diameter Bd, pitch diameter Pd, contact angle θ, shaft speed fr (→ A19 Bearings).

FTF = (fr/2)·(1 − (Bd/Pd)cosθ) cage ≈ 0.38–0.45× BPFO = (n·fr/2)·(1 − (Bd/Pd)cosθ) outer race ≈ 0.4×n BPFI = (n·fr/2)·(1 + (Bd/Pd)cosθ) inner race ≈ 0.6×n BSF = (fr·Pd/2Bd)·(1 − (Bd/Pd)²cos²θ) ball spin (×2 = ball defect)

Key tell: these are never integer orders (e.g. 3.58×, 5.42×). BPFI tones carry 1× sidebands (the defect rides in and out of the load zone each rev); ball defects carry FTF sidebands. Integer-order peaks are the shaft's family; non-integer peaks with sidebands are the bearing's.

The four stages of bearing failure
1

Ultrasonic only

>20 kHz content (spike energy, HFD). No spectral tones yet. Months of life left.

2

Resonance ringing

Impacts excite bearing natural frequencies (1–5 kHz). Envelope spectrum shows defect tone.

3

Defect tones visible

BPFO/BPFI + harmonics + sidebands appear in the normal velocity spectrum. Plan replacement.

4

Broadband collapse

Tones smear into a raised noise floor, 1× rises (clearance), crest factor drops. Replace now.

Gears & induction motors
GMF = N_teeth × f_shaft gear mesh frequency sidebands at GMF ± f_shaft: which shaft's spacing names the bad gear motors: stator/eccentricity → 2×LF (100/120 Hz) · broken rotor bars → 1× ± 2s·LF sidebands (s = slip) · loose bars → RBPF ± 2×LF

Gear rule: a healthy mesh shows GMF with small symmetric sidebands. A cracked or worn tooth modulates the mesh once per rev of its own shaft — sideband spacing identifies the culprit, and a growing sideband family (not GMF height alone) signals deterioration. Motor rule: electrical peaks vanish the instant power is cut — the coast-down test separates electrical from mechanical in one second.

Envelope Analysis & the Advanced Toolbox

Early bearing impacts are too weak for the normal spectrum — demodulation makes them shout.

Why the normal spectrum misses early defects

A stage-2 defect impact lasts microseconds and carries little energy at BPFO itself. But each impact rings the bearing's natural frequencies (1–5 kHz) like a bell struck repeatedly. The information is in the repetition rate of the ringing, not its amplitude.

Envelope (demodulation) analysis: band-pass at the ringing band, detect the envelope, FFT it. Commercial flavours: PeakVue (Emerson), gSE/Spike Energy (Bently/GE), SPM HD — same physics, different packaging.

Order of appearance: a fault shows in the envelope spectrum (stage 2) long before the velocity spectrum (stage 3). Trending both gives you the full arc: envelope finds it, velocity times the repair.

How Bad Is Bad? — ISO 20816 Severity

Broadband velocity zones give the first answer; your own baselines give the real one.

ISO 20816 (ex-10816) zone logic

Measure broadband velocity, mm/s RMS, 10–1000 Hz on non-rotating parts (bearing housings). Compare to zone boundaries set by machine group and mounting stiffness.

"Flexible" mounting means the support's natural frequency is well below running speed (e.g. springs/isolators); "rigid" means well above. The same 5 mm/s that is acceptable on a flexibly-mounted 500 kW fan is zone C on a rigid 22 kW pump. Special parts of the standard cover turbines (20816-2), coupled industrial machines (-3), gas turbines (-4), hydro (-5), reciprocating (-8), gears (-9).

Zone boundaries are screening levels, not diagnostics: a machine can pass broadband while a bearing tone doubles every month underneath. That is why spectra + trending beat a single number.

Alarms that actually work
  • 1
    Baseline every point when healthy. Alert = baseline + 6 dB (×2), fault = baseline + 12 dB (×4) is a robust starting scheme — tighter than generic ISO zones.
  • 2
    Band alarms: split the spectrum into fault bands (1×, 2×, bearing band, GMF band) and alarm each — catches a rising bearing tone hidden under a healthy 1×.
  • 3
    Statistical alarms: mean + 2σ…3σ over the point's own history beats any handbook value once you have ~10 surveys.
  • 4
    Trend on log time. Bearing degradation accelerates; a straight line on log-amplitude vs time extrapolates surprisingly well to stage 4.

Fixing It — Balancing & Alignment

Diagnosis earns its keep only when the correction is precise: ISO 21940 grades and laser alignment.

Field balancing & ISO 21940-11 grades

Unbalance U = m·e (g·mm): a mass m offset e from the rotation axis. The permissible residual scales inversely with speed — the G-number is the constant.

G = e_per × ω [mm/s] ⇒ e_per = G / ω = 9549 × G / N [µm, N in rpm... careful: e_per(µm) = 9549·G/N with G in mm/s] U_per = m_rotor × e_per permissible residual unbalance grades: G0.4 precision spindles · G1 grinding · G2.5 pumps, fans, motors · G6.3 general machinery · G16 drive shafts · G40 car wheels

Single-plane vector method: measure original 1× vector O (amplitude∠phase); add trial mass mt, measure new vector T. The effect vector is T−O, so the correction mass is mc = mt·|O|/|T−O|, placed at the angle that rotates (T−O) to oppose O. Rotors longer than ~½ their diameter, or running above ~70% of first critical, need two-plane or modal balancing.

Shaft alignment — the geometry
Misalignment = offset + angularity, each in two planes (H & V). Laser systems measure both directly and compute foot corrections; dial methods (rim-face, reverse-dial) remain the fallback.
  • 1
    Kill soft foot first. A frame that rocks >0.05 mm on one foot makes alignment unrepeatable and adds 2× vibration on its own.
  • 2
    Align at operating temperature or apply thermal-growth targets — a hot pump grows hundreds of µm vertically.
  • 3
    Flexible couplings tolerate misalignment; machines do not. The coupling survives — the bearings and seals either side absorb the preload.
  • 4
    Verify after: post-repair spectrum should show 2× and axial 1× collapse. The before/after pair is the proof your CM programme sells itself with.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

The measurement can fail as easily as the machine — know both failure catalogues.

Eight ideas that carry the module
  • 1
    Frequency names the fault, amplitude sizes it, trend times it.
  • 2
    V = ωX, A = ω²X — displacement low-f, velocity mid-band severity, acceleration high-f.
  • 3
    Δf = Fmax/LOR and T = LOR/Fmax — resolution costs time.
  • 4
    1× unbalance · 2×+axial misalignment · harmonic forest looseness — the synchronous triad.
  • 5
    Bearing tones are non-integer orders with sidebands; BPFO+BPFI = n·fr.
  • 6
    Envelope analysis demodulates resonance ringing — finds bearings a stage earlier than the velocity spectrum.
  • 7
    ISO 20816 zones screen; baselines + band alarms diagnose.
  • 8
    e_per = G/ω — balance quality tightens as speed rises; alignment tolerance too.
Six ways the measurement lies
⚠️ Ski-slope spectrum

Huge low-f ramp from sensor overload, thermal transient, or cable strain — integration noise, not machine motion. Re-take with settled sensor.

⚠️ Wrong Fmax

Fmax at 20× on a gearbox misses GMF; Fmax at 200× buries 1× resolution. One setup per machine class, saved in the route.

⚠️ Loose magnet / paint

Mounting resonance drops into the analysis band — false peaks around 1–2 kHz, and every bearing band reads high.

⚠️ Clipped sensor

Impacts beyond sensor range fold energy across the whole spectrum (harmonic smear). Check the time waveform for flat tops.

⚠️ Different point each survey

Trend becomes noise: 20 mm of position shift can double or halve a reading. Mark points; use installed pads.

⚠️ Overall-only alarming

Broadband RMS hides a bearing tone growing 4× under a dominant 1×. Band alarms or envelope trending are mandatory for bearings.

🧠 Quick Check 1

A pump shows 6 mm/s at exactly 2× running speed, with axial vibration 70% of radial. Most likely fault?

Tap to reveal Misalignment — dominant 2× plus high axial is the classic coupling-misalignment signature. Confirm with 180° phase change across the coupling, then laser-align (after checking soft foot).
🧠 Quick Check 2

Why does an inner-race defect (BPFI) show 1× sidebands while an outer-race defect (BPFO) usually does not?

Tap to reveal The inner race rotates with the shaft, so its defect passes in and out of the load zone once per revolution — amplitude-modulating the BPFI tone at 1× and creating BPFI ± 1× sidebands. The outer race is stationary; its defect sits at constant load, so the tone is unmodulated.
🧠 Quick Check 3

You need to resolve sidebands spaced 4.8 Hz apart around a 1.2 kHz gear-mesh peak. Propose Fmax and LOR.

Tap to reveal Need Δf ≤ 4.8/2 = 2.4 Hz (better ≤ 1.2). With Fmax = 2000 Hz (covers GMF + sidebands with margin): LOR = 2000/1.2 ≈ 1600 lines → Δf = 1.25 Hz, T = 0.8 s per average. 3200 lines would give Δf = 0.625 Hz if the sidebands crowd.
🧠 Quick Check 4

A reading's crest factor rose from 1.5 to 4.5 over three months but velocity RMS barely moved. What stage is this, and what tool confirms it?

Tap to reveal Early (stage 2) bearing damage: sharp low-energy impacts raise peak but not RMS. Confirm with envelope/demodulation spectrum — a clean BPFO/BPFI tone with harmonics identifies the defective race while months of planning time remain.
🧠 Quick Check 5

A 2-pole motor's vibration at ~100 Hz disappears the instant the breaker opens. Mechanical or electrical? Why?

Tap to reveal Electrical — magnetically-induced vibration (2×LF from stator/eccentricity forces) vanishes when the field collapses; mechanical vibration decays gradually with speed during coast-down. The power-cut test is the one-second discriminator.
🧠 Quick Check 6

Compute e_per for a G2.5 rotor at 3000 rpm, and U_per if the rotor weighs 100 kg.

Tap to reveal ω = 2π·50 = 314 rad/s → e_per = G/ω = 2500 (µm·rad/s)/314 ≈ 7.96 µm. U_per = m·e = 100 kg × 7.96 µm ≈ 796 g·mm (i.e. 7.96 g at 100 mm radius). Note how G2.5 at 3000 rpm is 8 µm, but at 300 rpm would be 80 µm — speed sets the target.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals, solved step by step.

Worked Example 1 — Bearing defect frequencies (6205, 1780 rpm)

Given: deep-groove bearing, n = 9 balls, Bd = 7.94 mm, Pd = 39.04 mm, θ = 0°, shaft 1780 rpm. Find all four defect frequencies in Hz and orders.

f_r = 1780/60 = 29.67 Hz B_d/P_d = 7.94/39.04 = 0.2034 (cosθ = 1) FTF = (29.67/2)(1 − 0.2034) = 14.83 × 0.7966 = 11.82 Hz (0.40×) BPFO = 9 × 11.82 = 106.4 Hz (3.59×) BPFI = (9 × 29.67/2)(1 + 0.2034) = 133.5 × 1.2034 = 160.6 Hz (5.41×) BSF = 29.67 × (39.04/15.88) × (1 − 0.2034²) = 29.67 × 2.458 × 0.9586 = 69.9 Hz (2.36×) check: BPFO + BPFI = 106.4 + 160.6 = 267.0 = 9 × 29.67 ✓

Discussion: all four are non-integer orders — the analyst's tell. In a real spectrum expect BPFO harmonics (2·BPFO, 3·BPFO) for an outer-race spall, and BPFI ± 29.67 Hz sidebands for an inner-race defect. A ball defect appears at 2·BSF (the flaw strikes both races per spin) with FTF sidebands.

Worked Example 2 — FFT setup for broken rotor bars

Given: 4-pole motor on 50 Hz mains, nameplate 1480 rpm, running near full load at 1480 rpm. Choose Fmax and LOR to resolve rotor-bar sidebands, and estimate acquisition time with 4 linear averages at 50% overlap.

n_s = 120·50/4 = 1500 rpm slip s = (1500 − 1480)/1500 = 0.0133 sideband spacing = 2s·LF = 2 × 0.0133 × 50 = 1.33 Hz around 1× = 24.67 Hz need Δf ≤ 1.33/4 ≈ 0.33 Hz (want ~4 bins between peaks) choose Fmax = 100 Hz, LOR = 800 → Δf = 100/800 = 0.125 Hz ✓ T_one = LOR/Fmax = 800/100 = 8 s 4 averages @ 50% overlap: T ≈ 8 + 3×4 = 20 s

Discussion: this is why rotor-bar tests use a dedicated low-Fmax, high-resolution measurement — a routine 1 kHz / 1600-line spectrum has Δf = 0.625 Hz and smears the 1.33 Hz sidebands into the 1× skirt. Severity guide: sidebands within 45–50 dB of 1× are normal; −35 dB warrants trending; −25 dB or better means cracked/broken bars.

Worked Example 3 — ISO 20816 severity call

Given: 45 kW end-suction pump, rigidly mounted on a grouted baseplate, drive-end bearing reads 4.1 mm/s RMS broadband (10–1000 Hz); six months ago it read 2.0 mm/s. Classify and recommend.

45 kW, rigid → Group 2, rigid support zones: A ≤ 1.4 · B ≤ 2.8 · C ≤ 4.5 · D > 4.5 4.1 mm/s ⇒ Zone C — restricted operation trend: 2.0 → 4.1 mm/s = +6.2 dB in 6 months (doubling)

Discussion: the zone alone says "plan action"; the doubling trend says "soon". Next step is diagnostic, not administrative: capture a spectrum + phase set, identify whether the growth is 1× (balance), 2×/axial (alignment) or a bearing band, and schedule the specific correction — not a generic overhaul. If the same 4.1 mm/s were on a flexibly-mounted Group 1 fan, it would sit mid-Zone B: context is everything.

Worked Example 4 — Single-plane trial-mass balancing

Given: overhung fan, original 1× vector O = 7.2 mm/s ∠60°. Trial mass mt = 20 g at the 0° blade. New vector T = 4.8 mm/s ∠150°. Find the correction mass and its location.

O = 7.2∠60° = (3.60, 6.24) T = 4.8∠150° = (−4.16, 2.40) effect of trial mass E = T − O = (−7.76, −3.84) → |E| = 8.66 mm/s ∠206° sensitivity = |E|/m_t = 8.66/20 = 0.433 (mm/s)/g correction mass m_c = |O|/sens = 7.2/0.433 = 16.6 g angle: E must rotate from 206° to oppose O (60°+180° = 240°) Δ = 240° − 206° = +34° ⇒ move mass 34° from the trial position (in the direction of shaft rotation used for phase reference)

Discussion: the trial mass answered two questions at once — how many (mm/s per gram) and where (the angular lag between mass position and response). A good trial mass changes O by 30% or 30°; if T ≈ O the trial was too small to trust. After correction, repeat the measurement: residual ≤ 1 mm/s (or the ISO 21940 e_per target) closes the job.

Problem Set — 10 Questions

Work each one before revealing. Calculator + the equation pages are all you need.

Problem 1

Convert 8 mm/s peak at 25 Hz into displacement (µm peak-to-peak).

Tap to reveal X = V/ω = 8/(2π×25) = 0.0509 mm = 50.9 µm peak → ≈ 102 µm p-p. Low frequency: modest velocity is already large displacement.
Problem 2

What acceleration (in g) corresponds to 2 mm/s peak at 3.2 kHz?

Tap to reveal A = ωV = 2π×3200×0.002 m/s = 40.2 m/s² ≈ 4.1 g. Same velocity at high frequency = violent acceleration — why bearing work is done in g's.
Problem 3

A 3-blade fan runs at 1180 rpm. You see a clean peak at 59 Hz. Identify it.

Tap to reveal f_r = 1180/60 = 19.67 Hz; 59/19.67 = 3.0 → blade-pass frequency (3× = blades × 1×). Rising BPF suggests flow disturbance: duct obstruction, damper position, or blade-to-housing clearance.
Problem 4

Fmax = 800 Hz, LOR = 3200. Give Δf, acquisition time per average, and samples taken.

Tap to reveal Δf = 800/3200 = 0.25 Hz; T = 3200/800 = 4 s; N = 2.56 × 3200 = 8192 samples.
Problem 5

Bearing: n = 13, Bd/Pd = 0.18, shaft 1490 rpm. Compute BPFO and BPFI (Hz and orders).

Tap to reveal f_r = 24.83 Hz. BPFO = (13/2)(24.83)(0.82) = 132.4 Hz = 5.33×; BPFI = (13/2)(24.83)(1.18) = 190.5 Hz = 7.67×. Check: 132.4+190.5 = 322.9 ≈ 13×24.83 ✓
Problem 6

Time waveform: peak 28 m/s², RMS 4 m/s². Compute crest factor and interpret.

Tap to reveal CF = 28/4 = 7 (sine = 1.414). Severe repetitive impacting — advanced rolling-element damage, gear tooth fault, or extreme looseness. Envelope spectrum next to name the source.
Problem 7

A 250 kg fan rotor must meet G6.3 at 1500 rpm. Find e_per and U_per.

Tap to reveal ω = 157.1 rad/s → e_per = 6300/157.1 ≈ 40 µm. U_per = 250 kg × 40 µm = ≈ 10 000 g·mm (e.g. 25 g at 400 mm radius). At 3000 rpm the same grade would halve to ~20 µm.
Problem 8

Why are ISO 20816 severity limits written in velocity rather than displacement or acceleration?

Tap to reveal Over the 10–1000 Hz machine band, equal velocity ≈ equal vibrational energy and equal fatigue-damage potential regardless of frequency (energy ∝ v²). Displacement over-weights low f; acceleration over-weights high f. Velocity is the flattest proxy for "how hard the machine is shaking itself apart".
Problem 9

A gearbox spectrum shows GMF at 1312 Hz with sidebands spaced 43.75 Hz. Input shaft: 2625 rpm; output shaft: 875 rpm. Which gear is modulating?

Tap to reveal Input shaft = 2625/60 = 43.75 Hz → sideband spacing matches the input pinion. (Output would give 14.58 Hz spacing.) Bonus: teeth = 1312/43.75 = 30 on the pinion.
Problem 10

A machine passes its broadband alarm at 2.1 mm/s, but the envelope-spectrum BPFO tone has grown 10× in four surveys. Your call?

Tap to reveal Plan a bearing replacement now, at the next opportunity window. Broadband velocity screens overall energy and misses early-stage tones; envelope trend growth of 10× signals stage 2→3 progression. Order parts, watch crest factor and the velocity-band bearing region for stage 3–4 confirmation, and shorten the survey interval.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

The three derivations examiners reach for, and the unit traps that sink real reports.

Derivation 1 — the ω ladder (x → v → a)
x(t) = X sin(ωt) v(t) = dx/dt = ωX cos(ωt) = ωX sin(ωt + 90°) a(t) = dv/dt = −ω²X sin(ωt) = ω²X sin(ωt + 180°) ⇒ each differentiation: amplitude ×ω, phase +90° ⇒ V_peak = 2πf·X_peak A_peak = (2πf)²·X_peak

Why it matters: analysers integrate acceleration to display velocity (÷ω per step). Integration amplifies low-frequency noise by 1/ω — the origin of the "ski-slope" artefact — which is why integrated channels are high-pass filtered around 1–10 Hz.

Derivation 2 — bearing frequencies from rolling kinematics
surface speeds (inner rotating at ω_r, outer fixed): v_inner = ω_r·(P_d − B_d cosθ)/2 v_outer = 0 cage rolls between them ⇒ v_cage = (v_inner + v_outer)/2 ω_cage/ω_r = ½(1 − B_d/P_d·cosθ) ⇒ FTF = (f_r/2)(1 − B_d/P_d cosθ) outer-race defect: struck by each of n elements at cage speed ⇒ BPFO = n·FTF inner-race defect: relative speed = f_r − FTF ⇒ BPFI = n·(f_r − FTF) = (n·f_r/2)(1 + B_d/P_d cosθ) ⇒ BPFO + BPFI = n·f_r (the sanity identity)

Why it matters: deriving rather than memorising lets you handle the odd cases — rotating outer race (flip the roles), thin-section bearings, and the ~1–2% slip that makes measured tones sit slightly below calculated (calculated values assume pure rolling; real bearings skid a little, so search a window, not a line).

Derivation 3 — balance grade to permissible unbalance
G-grade defines constant severity: G = e_per·ω [mm/s] (the velocity the CG offset alone would impose) e_per [mm] = G/ω = G/(2πN/60) → e_per [µm] ≈ 9549·G/N (N in rpm) U_per [g·mm] = m_rotor [kg] × e_per [µm]

Why it matters: it shows the physics behind "balance tighter when faster": centrifugal force F = m·e·ω² grows as ω², so holding e·ω constant still lets force grow linearly with speed — the standard is a severity compromise, not a force limit. For fixed-speed machines you may balance to the grade; for VFD machines balance for the top speed.

Seven exam & field traps
  • 1
    RMS vs peak vs pk-pk. A "6 mm/s" alarm in RMS is 8.5 pk and 17 p-p for a sine. State the descriptor on every number, always.
  • 2
    Hz vs CPM. American charts run in CPM (= Hz × 60). A 29.67 Hz shaft is 1780 CPM — mixing them shifts every diagnosis by ×60.
  • 3
    mm/s vs in/s. 1 in/s = 25.4 mm/s. Legacy US severity charts (0.3 in/s "rough") ≈ 7.6 mm/s — do not compare across unit systems unconverted.
  • 4
    Orders vs absolute Hz. A "2× peak" moves with speed; a 100 Hz electrical peak does not. On VFD machines only order-normalised spectra trend cleanly.
  • 5
    Bearing tones sit slightly LOW of calculation (1–2% slip). An exact integer match is more likely a shaft harmonic than a bearing tone.
  • 6
    Envelope amplitude is not ISO severity. gE / envelope units are relative trend indicators — never grade them against 20816 mm/s zones.
  • 7
    ISO zones apply to broadband, not to one spectral peak. Grading a single 1× line against the zone table misuses the standard (the zone value is the whole 10–1000 Hz RMS).

Hands-on ladder — eight exercises
  • 1
    Phone spectroscope. Use a phone accelerometer app on a desk fan: find 1× and blade-pass. Tape a coin to one blade and watch 1× jump.
  • 2
    20-line FFT. In Python/NumPy, synthesise 1× + 2× + noise, window it (Hanning), FFT it, and verify Δf = Fmax/LOR yourself.
  • 3
    Catalogue drill. Pull Bd, Pd, n for three bearings from the SKF catalogue and compute all four defect frequencies at your plant's common speeds.
  • 4
    Bump test. Accelerometer on a bracket, tap with a mallet, peak-hold spectrum → its natural frequencies. Then add a clamp mass and watch f_n drop.
  • 5
    Simulate envelope analysis. Multiply a 3 kHz ring-down train (rep rate 107 Hz) in code, band-pass + rectify + FFT — recover 107 Hz. This one exercise cements page 7.
  • 6
    Baseline a real machine. Three points × three directions on one pump, weekly for a month. Plot the trend; set mean+2σ alarms.
  • 7
    Before/after proof. Next alignment or balance job at work: capture spectra before and after, quantify the dB drop at 1×/2×, and write the one-page case study.
  • 8
    Sit the syllabus. Download the ISO 18436-2 Cat I/II body of knowledge and self-test; the gaps it exposes are your next study list.
