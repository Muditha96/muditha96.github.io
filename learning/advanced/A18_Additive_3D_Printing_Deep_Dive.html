Page 1 · Foundations

Additive vs Subtractive — A Different Logic

Precise Definition

Additive Manufacturing (AM) builds a part by adding material layer upon layer from a 3-D model — the inverse of subtractive machining, which cuts material away from a solid block.

This inversion changes the economics. In machining, complexity costs more (more cuts, fixtures, time). In AM, complexity is free — a lattice is no harder to print than a solid block, and one machine makes any geometry. What AM trades away is speed, surface finish, isotropic strength and per-part cost at volume.

AM wins when…

Geometry is complex, volumes are low, parts are customised, lead time is short, or you want consolidated assemblies & internal channels impossible to machine.

Machining wins when…

Volumes are high, tolerances are tight, surfaces must be smooth, or the part is simple — subtractive is faster, cheaper and isotropic at scale.

The defining inversion: Subtractive: complexity ↑ ⇒ cost ↑ (more cuts, fixtures, time) Additive: complexity ↑ ⇒ cost ≈ flat ("complexity is free") But AM adds a new axis: anisotropy — parts are weaker between layers (Z).
Where this sits in the plant

AM spans rapid prototyping, rapid tooling (printed jigs, fixtures, moulds), and true end-use production (aerospace brackets, dental, medical implants). It pairs with GD&T for tolerancing, feeds custom end-effectors for robot cells, and is a pillar of Industry 4.0 digital manufacturing.

Page 2 · The Landscape

The Seven Process Families (ISO/ASTM 52900)

Micro 18 covered one process — FDM. The standard ISO/ASTM 52900 defines seven AM process categories. Knowing which family fits a job is the first engineering decision; they differ in material, resolution, strength, speed and cost by orders of magnitude.

Three dominant principles: push molten material, selectively cure a liquid, or selectively fuse a powder bed.

The selection shortcut

Need it cheap and tomorrow? FDM. Need fine detail & smooth finish? SLA/DLP. Need strong, functional, no-support plastic? SLS/MJF. Need a load-bearing metal part? DMLS/SLM. Need a huge metal part fast? WAAM/DED. Match the family to the requirement before designing — DfAM rules differ per process.

Page 3 · The Physics

FDM in Depth — Melt, Deposit, Solidify

FDM (Fused Deposition Modelling / FFF) is the most common process, so it's worth understanding deeply. A motor pushes filament into a heated hot end; molten polymer extrudes through a nozzle and is laid as a bead that fuses to its neighbours and the layer below, then freezes.

The thermal gradient is everything: the filament must stay solid in the heat break and melt only in the block, or it jams.

Hot end temp

Set per polymer (PLA ~200, PETG ~240, ABS ~250 °C). Too cold → poor fusion & clogs; too hot → stringing & degradation.

Layer height

Typically 0.1–0.3 mm. Thin = finer detail & better Z-strength but slower; thick = fast but visible steps & weaker bond.

Bed adhesion

Heated bed + surface (PEI, glue) stops the warping & lifting that ruins prints. First layer is the foundation of the whole part.

Volumetric flow the hot end must sustain: Q = (line width) × (layer height) × (print speed) e.g. 0.45 mm × 0.2 mm × 80 mm/s = 7.2 mm³/s If demanded Q > hot-end's max melt rate → under-extrusion (gaps, weak walls). Print time ∝ part volume ÷ Q → thicker layers & faster speed cut time, cost surface & strength.
The first-layer obsession

Seasoned operators spend their tuning effort on the first layer: correct nozzle-to-bed gap, level bed, right temperature. A perfect first layer prevents warping, lifting and the cascade of failures that follow. "First layer right, print right" is the FDM mantra.

Page 4 · The Core Weakness

Layer Adhesion & Why Z Is Weak

Micro 18 hinted that printed parts are "weaker between layers." This is the single most important fact in AM. A bead bonds to the layer below by polymer chain diffusion across a still-hot interface — and that bond is weaker than the bulk material.

A printed part is an engineered laminate: strong along the beads (XY), weaker across the layer interfaces (Z).

WHAT HELPS ADHESION

Keep the interface hot & merging

Higher hot-end temp, enclosed/heated chamber (esp. ABS/PC), thinner layers (more bond area per height), slower cooling, and clean dry filament. The hotter the prior layer when the next lands, the deeper the chains inter-diffuse.

WHAT HURTS IT

Anything that cools too fast

Too-cold nozzle, aggressive part-cooling fan on tall thin parts, draughts, wet filament (steam bubbles), and very thick layers. The result: parts that snap cleanly along a layer line under load.

Design rule of thumb (orient for load): Tensile load should run along the layers (XY), never pull them apart (Z). A printed hook loaded in Z can be 2–3× weaker than the same hook printed flat. Healing depends on time above the glass transition T_g and interface temperature.
Orientation is a strength decision, not just a finish one

Before worrying about supports or surface, ask: "which way will this part be loaded?" Then orient so the layers carry the load in-plane. This one choice — covered more on Page 6 — separates a part that survives from one that delaminates in service.

Page 5 · From Model to Machine

Slicing & Toolpaths

A printer can't read a 3-D model directly. A slicer converts the model (STL / 3MF mesh) into horizontal layers, then into machine instructions (G-code) describing every move, temperature and flow. The slicer settings decide strength, speed, weight and finish.

Perimeters / walls

The outer shells. More walls = stronger, watertight parts. Wall count drives strength far more than infill for most loads.

Infill

Internal lattice (gyroid, grid, honeycomb) at a chosen %. 15–20% is typical; trades weight & time vs stiffness. 100% rarely needed.

Top / bottom layers

Solid skins that close the part. Too few → "pillowing" (gaps over infill). Usually 4–6 solid layers each.

Strength comes from walls, not infill

The classic beginner error is cranking infill to 100% for a "strong" part — slow, heavy, and barely stronger. For most loads, more perimeters (e.g. 4–6 walls) and correct orientation give far more strength per gram and per minute. Infill mainly resists buckling and supports the top skins.

Page 6 · Geometry Constraints

Supports, Overhangs & Orientation

You can't print into thin air. Steep overhangs and bridges need support structures — sacrificial material removed afterward. Supports cost material, time and surface quality, so the art is orienting the part to need as few as possible.

The ~45° rule: an overhang shallower than ~45° from vertical self-supports; steeper needs support or a redesign.

SUPPORT STRATEGY

Minimise, don't eliminate blindly

Orient to put overhangs ≤45°, use chamfers instead of sharp overhangs, and prefer soluble supports (PVA/HIPS on dual-extruder) for complex internal geometry. Support contact leaves scars — keep it off cosmetic faces.

ORIENTATION TRADE-OFFS

One choice, four consequences

Orientation simultaneously sets strength (layers vs load, Page 4), support volume, surface finish (top faces smoother than support-side), and print time (height drives time). These usually conflict — you optimise for the dominant requirement.

The four-way orientation conflict

The "best" orientation for strength is often the worst for supports or finish. A bracket loaded in one axis wants its layers across that load — but that may stand it tall (long print) with a big overhang (lots of support). Resolving this consciously is core DfAM: change the design so a single orientation satisfies all four.

Page 7 · Designing for the Process

DfAM — Design for Additive Manufacturing

DfAM is designing parts to exploit AM's freedoms and respect its constraints — not just printing a part drawn for machining. Done well, it produces parts lighter, stronger and simpler than any subtractive equivalent.

Part consolidation

Merge an assembly of many machined parts + fasteners into one printed part — fewer joints, less weight, faster assembly, no leaks. A famous example: a fuel nozzle going from 20 parts to 1.

Lattices & infill design

Replace solid volume with engineered lattices (gyroid, TPMS) for stiffness-to-weight no casting can match — energy absorption, heat exchangers, implants.

Topology optimisation

Algorithms remove material everywhere stress is low, leaving organic load paths. AM is the only way to make the resulting shapes.

Internal channels

Conformal cooling channels that follow a mould's surface — impossible to drill, trivial to print. Cuts injection-mould cycle time dramatically.

The DfAM constraint checklist (FDM-centric)
  • 1
    Overhangs ≤45° where possible; add chamfers/fillets instead of flat overhangs.
  • 2
    Mind the Z direction — orient so loads run along layers, not across them.
  • 3
    Wall thickness ≥ ~2–3× nozzle width (≈0.8–1.2 mm) so walls are printable & strong.
  • 4
    Avoid large flat areas prone to warping; add ribs or break up mass.
  • 5
    Design clearances (~0.2–0.5 mm) for press/clearance fits and assembled-in-place joints.
DfAM flips the design mindset

Subtractive design asks "how do I make this with available tools?" DfAM asks "what is the ideal shape for the function, given complexity is free?" That reframing — lattices, consolidation, organic load paths, internal channels — is where AM stops being a slow plastic printer and becomes a genuine engineering advantage. It ties directly to GD&T-driven functional tolerancing.

Page 8 · Materials

Material Selection & Anisotropy

Material choice sets strength, temperature resistance, chemical resistance, flexibility and printability. And every printed part carries anisotropy — different properties along XY vs Z — which the data sheet number alone won't tell you.

PLAEasy, stiff, biodegradable, low warpBrittle, low heat (softens ~60 °C)
PETGTough, chemical & moisture resistantStringing; weak Z if printed hot/fast
ABS / ASAHeat & impact resistant, machinableWarps badly — needs enclosure; fumes
Nylon (PA)Tough, wear & fatigue resistantVery hygroscopic — must dry; warps
PCStrong, high-temp, transparent-ishHigh temps, enclosure, moisture-sensitive
Fibre-filled (CF/GF)Stiff, dimensionally stableAbrasive — needs hardened nozzle

The red Z-bars draw in after the green XY-bars to dramatise the interlayer-strength penalty — the bigger the gap, the more the part depends on orientation.

Treat a printed part like a composite laminate, not a solid: σ_XY > σ_Z always, for layer-based AM Apply a knock-down factor on data-sheet strength for Z loads (often ×0.4–0.8). Powder-bed (SLS) & resin (SLA) are more isotropic than FDM — fewer, smaller interfaces.
Dry your filament — it's not optional

Nylon, PC, PETG and TPU absorb water from air. Wet filament boils in the nozzle: popping, stringing, weak layers, rough surface. Store hygroscopic filament with desiccant and dry it before printing. Half of "my prints suddenly got bad" cases are simply wet filament.

Page 9 · Production-Grade AM

Metal AM, Post-Processing & QA

For functional metal parts, powder bed fusion (DMLS/SLM, EBM) melts metal powder with a laser or electron beam. It unlocks aerospace, medical and tooling parts — but demands rigorous post-processing and quality control that plastic prototyping does not.

METAL PBF

Laser-melted metal, layer by layer

Ti-6Al-4V, Inconel, aluminium, stainless & tool steels. Near-full density, real load-bearing parts — but high residual stress, supports needed, and powder handling is a safety concern (reactive, fine, inhalable).

WHY POST MATTERS

As-built ≠ finished

As-built metal parts have rough surfaces, internal stress, and sometimes porosity. They are rarely usable straight off the plate — post-processing is part of the process, not an afterthought.

Typical metal AM post-processing chain

HIP = Hot Isostatic Pressing (heat + pressure closes internal porosity). Critical metal parts get CT-scanned to verify there are no hidden voids.

Porosity

Trapped gas or unfused powder = internal voids that crack under fatigue. HIP closes them; CT scanning finds them.

Residual stress

Rapid melt/freeze locks in stress → warping & cracking. Stress-relieve before cutting off the build plate.

Repeatability

For production, every build is monitored (melt-pool sensing) & logged — feeding the Industry 4.0 quality record.

Where AM connects forward — the digital thread

Production AM is inseparable from data: build parameters, melt-pool monitoring, powder lot, post-process records and CT results form a digital thread per part, tying into SCADA/MES and digital twins (A31). This traceability is exactly what lets a printed bracket fly on an aircraft — the link between AM and Industry 4.0.

What actually goes wrong on the floor
⚠️ Warping / corners lifting

First layer or bed adhesion + thermal contraction (ABS, nylon). Enclose the printer, heat the bed, add a brim, slow the first layer.

⚠️ Layers split / delamination

Z-bond too weak: nozzle too cold, cooling fan too strong, wet filament, or part loaded in Z. Raise temp, reduce cooling, dry filament, re-orient.

⚠️ Under-extrusion / gaps

Demanded flow > hot-end max melt rate (too fast/thick), clog, or wet filament. Slow down, raise temp, clean nozzle, dry filament.

⚠️ Supports won't come off / scar

Support too dense or interface gap wrong. Tune interface layers, use soluble supports, or re-orient to avoid supports on key faces.

Real Industrial Example — full circle to Micro 18

A maintenance team prints a custom robot gripper jaw (the kind of jig behind Micro 18): designed with DfAM to consolidate three machined parts into one, oriented so the gripping load runs along the layers (Page 4), printed in CF-Nylon (dried first) with 5 perimeter walls and 20% gyroid infill, supports only on the non-mating face, then bolted onto a robot as an end-effector — in hours, not the weeks a machined version would take. Quick Check

You print a hook that hangs a 5 kg load. (a) The first version snaps cleanly along a layer line. What happened, and how should you re-orient it? (b) A colleague says "just set infill to 100% to make it stronger." Why is increasing wall count usually the better move?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
(a) The hook was oriented so the hanging load pulled the layers apart in Z — the weak interlayer-adhesion direction. The clean break along a layer line is the signature. Re-orient (or redesign) so the tensile load runs along the layers (XY) — e.g. print the hook lying in the loading plane — which can be 2–3× stronger for the same material. (b) Strength in FDM comes mostly from the perimeter walls, not the infill. 100% infill is slow, heavy and barely stronger; adding more walls (e.g. 4–6 perimeters) plus correct orientation gives far more strength per gram and per minute. Infill mainly resists buckling and supports the top skins.
