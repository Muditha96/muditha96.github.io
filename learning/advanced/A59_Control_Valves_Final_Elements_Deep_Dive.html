The Final Element Is the Weak Link

A controller is software and costs nothing to retune. The valve is iron in a pipe, and it is where most loop problems actually live.

Control loop audits across process industries repeatedly reach the same conclusion: the largest single cause of poor loop performance is the final control element — not the tuning, not the controller, not the measurement. A valve with stiction, wrong trim, wrong size or a failing positioner will defeat any control strategy, however sophisticated. And unlike a tuning error, you cannot fix it from the control room.

Why the Valve Dominates

It is the only nonlinear element you cannot avoid

The controller is linear, the transmitter is (or can be made) linear — but the valve's gain varies with travel, with pressure drop and with flow. Everything in Pages 7–8 exists because of this.

It is mechanical, so it wears

Packing friction, seat erosion, cavitation damage, actuator diaphragm fatigue, positioner drift. A loop that was excellent at commissioning degrades because the valve does — silently.

It is usually the wrong size

Oversizing is endemic: designers add margin on margin, so the valve spends its life 10–25% open where its resolution and characteristic are worst.

It is invisible to the control system

The DCS knows what it commanded. Without position feedback it has no idea what the valve did — which is why smart positioners and diagnostics (Page 10) matter so much.

The chain the controller's output must survive:
OP % → I/P converter → positioner → actuator thrust → stem friction → plug position → Cv → flow → process
Every stage adds gain error, deadband, hysteresis or delay. The controller's beautiful 4–20 mA signal is only a request.

    The oversizing spiral, and why it matters. The process engineer adds 20% to the flow, the sizing engineer rounds the Cv up, the vendor offers the next standard trim, and someone picks the next line size "for the future". The result is a valve that delivers design flow at 15% travel — where the characteristic is steepest, resolution is worst, seat wear is concentrated and stiction has most effect. Quick check: A flow loop cycles continuously. The controller has been retuned three times by three engineers, each making it worse. What single test would you do first, and what three valve faults could produce this?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Put the loop in manual with a fixed output. If the cycle stops, the controller and valve are interacting; if it continues, the disturbance is external and every retune was wasted effort. Then, with the loop still in manual, do a valve travel test: step the output in small increments (say 0.5%, then 1%, then 2%) up and down, and compare commanded position with actual stem position from the positioner feedback. Three valve faults that produce continuous cycling: (1) Stiction — the stem sticks until the actuator force overcomes static friction, then jumps past the required position; the controller's integral winds up, breaks it loose, overshoots, and repeats. The signature is a stepped MV with a sawtooth or square-wave PV, and the cure is packing or actuator repair, never retuning. (2) Deadband / backlash — a reversal costs several percent of signal before anything moves, so the loop hunts around setpoint; typically from a worn stem connection, loose actuator coupling or a positioner needing calibration. (3) Gross oversizing — at 12% travel the valve's gain is so high, and its resolution so poor, that the loop cannot be tuned to be both stable and responsive; the effective process gain is several times the design value. Two honourable mentions worth checking in the same visit: a positioner tuned too aggressively (it is itself a fast inner cascade loop and can oscillate independently — Page 10), and a valve that is cavitating, whose flow becomes erratic and noisy regardless of position.

Quick check: A specification calls for a 300 mm butterfly valve on a pump discharge with P₁ = 12 bar, P₂ = 3 bar, water at 40 °C. What is your immediate concern, and what would you propose?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Cavitation. The pressure drop is 9 bar out of an inlet of 12 bar — a very high ratio — and a butterfly valve is a high-recovery body with FL ≈ 0.6. The allowable pressure drop before choking/cavitation begins is roughly ΔPchoked = FL²(P₁ − FFPv); water at 40 °C has Pv ≈ 0.074 bar absolute, so with FF ≈ 0.96 the term in brackets is essentially P₁, giving ΔPchoked ≈ 0.36 × 12 ≈ 4.3 bar. The duty demands 9 bar — more than twice the onset — so the valve will cavitate severely and continuously, destroying the disc, seat and downstream pipe within months, with noise and vibration to match. What to propose, in order. (1) Change the body style to a low-recovery globe (FL ≈ 0.9 → ΔPchoked ≈ 0.81 × 12 ≈ 9.7 bar, marginally acceptable) — but at 9 bar it is still at the boundary, so (2) specify anti-cavitation / multi-stage trim, which splits the drop across several stages so no single vena contracta reaches vapour pressure. (3) Alternatively stage the pressure drop between two valves in series, or add a fixed downstream restriction orifice to raise the back pressure at the valve. (4) Question the process: is 9 bar of throttling necessary at all, or is the pump oversized? Trimming the impeller or fitting a VFD (A08/A47) removes the energy waste and the cavitation together — usually the best engineering and the best economics. (5) If a butterfly must be used for space or cost reasons, verify with the vendor's sigma (cavitation index) data at the actual duty and expect to plan for periodic trim replacement. Also check the noise prediction — 9 bar across a butterfly in a 300 mm line will be far above 85 dBA.

Cv, Kv & the Sizing Equation

One coefficient describes a valve's capacity. Understand where it comes from and the whole of sizing follows.

Where the Coefficient Comes From
A valve is a variable orifice. From Bernoulli, flow through a restriction is Q = Cd·A·√(2ΔP/ρ) Everything about the valve's geometry — the discharge coefficient Cd and the flow area A at a given travel — is bundled into one empirically measured number: Q = C · √(ΔP/ρrel) so that C = Q / √(ΔP/ρrel) THE TWO CONVENTIONS Cv (US customary) — the flow of water in US gallons per minute at 60 °F that produces a pressure drop of 1 psi: Cv = Q·√(SG/ΔP) Q [USgpm], ΔP [psi] Kv (metric / IEC) — the flow of water in m³/h at 5–40 °C that produces a pressure drop of 1 bar: Kv = Q·√(SG/ΔP) Q [m³/h], ΔP [bar] CONVERSION Kv = 0.865 · Cv Cv = 1.156 · Kv IEC 60534 uses the symbol C for whichever coefficient the chosen unit set implies, and expresses everything through numerical constants N₁, N₆, N₉ … so one equation serves all unit systems. Always state which coefficient and which units you mean — a factor of 1.156 in a valve size is a real and expensive error.

Cv is not dimensionless

It carries units (gpm/√psi). That is why the conversion factor exists and why "Cv = 100" means nothing without the convention.

Cv is a function of travel

Catalogues quote Cv at rated travel. The characteristic (Page 7) describes how Cv varies from 0 to that value.

Cv is measured, not calculated

Determined on a flow test bench to IEC 60534-2-3. Vendor data is authoritative; generic formulas are estimates.

The Full IEC 60534-2-1 Liquid Equation
The turbulent, non-choked liquid sizing equation in its complete form: C = Q / (N₁ · FP) · √( (ρ₁/ρ₀) / (p₁ − p₂) ) N₁ numerical constant fixing the unit system (N₁ = 0.865 for Q [m³/h], p [kPa] with C as Cv; N₁ = 1 for Kv with p [bar]) FP piping geometry factor — corrects for reducers/expanders fitted to the valve (FP = 1 for a line-size valve with no fittings; typically 0.85–0.98 with reducers) ρ₁/ρ₀ relative density (specific gravity) at inlet conditions Two further correction factors appear when the flow is not fully turbulent: FR Reynolds number factor — for viscous or very low flows. When Rev < ~10 000 the orifice equation breaks down and C must be increased. Needed for heavy oils, polymers and small trims; ignore it and the valve is undersized. FLP combined liquid-pressure-recovery and piping factor for the choked case. Full non-turbulent form: C = Q / (N₁ · FR) · √( (ρ₁/ρ₀) / ΔP ) THE PRACTICAL WORKING FORMULA (line-size valve, turbulent water-like fluid): Kv = Q · √(SG / ΔP) Q [m³/h], ΔP [bar] Cv = 1.156 · Kv This is what you use on site. The correction factors matter when the installation has reducers (FP), the fluid is viscous (FR), or the drop is large enough to choke (FL, Page 5) — and a professional sizing sheet shows all of them explicitly.

The pressure drop trap. ΔP across the valve is NOT a constant — it is whatever the system leaves over, and it FALLS as flow rises (because pipe and equipment losses rise as Q²): ΔPvalve = ΔPavailable − K·Q² So the maximum-flow case has the smallest ΔP and needs the largest Cv, while the minimum-flow case has the largest ΔP. Sizing at a single "design ΔP" — a very common error — produces a valve that is wrong at both ends. This ΔP-versus-flow relationship is exactly what creates the installed characteristic of Page 8.
  • 1
    Get the pump curve, not just the design point. For a centrifugal pump the available head falls as flow rises, so ΔPavailable itself is a function of Q. Sizing without the pump curve is guesswork (A47).
  • 2
    Never size from the line size. A valve is sized from Cv, then a body is chosen — often one or two sizes smaller than the line, fitted with reducers (and FP applied). "Same size as the pipe" is the single most common route to a grossly oversized valve.
  • 3
    Check the vendor's Cv at the travel you will actually use, not the rated Cv. A valve whose rated Cv is 120 but which will run at 30% travel on an equal-percentage trim is delivering roughly Cv 14 — a completely different valve.
Quick check: A valve must pass 40 m³/h of water with 2.5 bar drop. Compute Kv and Cv. Then the process engineer says maximum flow is 60 m³/h, at which the available drop falls to 1.2 bar. What Cv is really needed, and what does the comparison tell you?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Normal case: Kv = Q√(SG/ΔP) = 40 × √(1.0/2.5) = 40 × 0.6325 = 25.3, so Cv = 1.156 × 25.3 = 29.2. Maximum case: Kv = 60 × √(1.0/1.2) = 60 × 0.9129 = 54.8, so Cv = 1.156 × 54.8 = 63.3. What the comparison tells you: the maximum-flow case needs more than twice the capacity of the normal case — 63.3 against 29.2 — because flow rose 50% and the available pressure drop fell by half at the same time. Both effects push the same way, which is why the maximum case almost always sizes the valve. Had you sized on the normal duty alone you would have specified a valve roughly half the required capacity and the plant would never reach maximum rate. Conversely — and this is the more common error — sizing everything at the largest ΔP (2.5 bar) for the largest flow (60 m³/h) would give Kv = 60 × 0.6325 = 38, understating it differently, while adding the usual safety margins on top of the max case gives a valve that runs at 20% travel on normal duty. The professional answer: size for Cv ≈ 63 at 80–90% travel, then verify the normal and minimum cases fall at sensible travel on the chosen characteristic — that verification step is the whole point of three-point sizing, and it is what the Page 8 demo does.

Liquid Sizing in Practice

The calculation is trivial. The judgement — which ΔP, which margins, which travel — is what makes it engineering.

The Sizing Procedure
1. Collect process data at MINIMUM, NORMAL and MAXIMUM flow: Q, p₁, p₂, temperature, SG (at temperature), viscosity, vapour pressure pv, and the fluid's critical pressure pc if cavitation is possible. 2. Compute the available ΔP at each case from the pump curve and system losses. 3. Check for CHOKED FLOW at each case (Page 5). If ΔP > ΔPchoked, use ΔPchoked in the sizing equation — using the actual ΔP would oversize the valve badly. 4. Compute the required C at each case. 5. Apply FP if reducers are fitted; apply FR if the flow is viscous or laminar. 6. Select a body and trim whose rated C, with the chosen characteristic, puts max flow at 80–90% travel and min flow above 10–20%. 7. Verify noise (Page 12), cavitation (Page 5), materials and actuator thrust (Page 9). 8. Record everything on the valve specification sheet — including the assumptions. THE MARGIN QUESTION A modest capacity margin is prudent; compounded margins are destructive: process engineer +10% on flow × sizing engineer +20% "safety" × rounding up to the next standard trim (+15%) × next line size "for the future" (+40%) = a valve with 2× the necessary capacity, operating at 15–25% travel. Add margin ONCE, deliberately, and state it. Then check the travel at all three cases.
The Reynolds Correction — When the Orifice Equation Fails
The standard equation assumes fully turbulent flow through the trim. For viscous fluids or very small flows the valve Reynolds number Rev = (N₄ · Fd · Q) / (ν · √(C · FL)) · ( (FL²C²)/(N₂D⁴) + 1 )¼ falls below about 10 000 and the required C rises — sometimes by a factor of two or more. Practical guidance: Rev > 10 000 FR ≈ 1.0 no correction needed (water, most light liquids) Rev ≈ 1 000 FR ≈ 0.7–0.8 significant — apply it Rev < 100 FR ≈ 0.3–0.5 laminar; the valve behaves nothing like the catalogue Rule of thumb: suspect a correction whenever viscosity exceeds roughly 20–50 cSt, or when sizing a very small trim (C < 1) on any fluid. Heavy fuel oil, glycol, syrups, polymer solutions and lube oil all need checking — and a valve sized without FR on such a service will simply not pass the required flow.

Quick check: Why does using the actual pressure drop rather than the choked pressure drop oversize a valve, and what happens in service to a valve sized that way?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because once flow is choked, additional pressure drop produces no additional flow. Physically the vena contracta has reached vapour pressure and vapour is forming; the flow is limited by conditions at that throat, not by the downstream pressure. The sizing equation Q = C√(ΔP/SG) therefore stops being valid: if you insert the full (larger) ΔP, the equation returns a smaller C than reality requires, because it credits the valve with pressure drop that is not doing any work. IEC 60534 handles this by capping the ΔP used in the calculation at ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv). Wait — which way does the error go? Using the full ΔP understates the required Cv, so the valve is undersized and will not pass the required flow no matter how far it opens. (The oversizing case arises the other way round: engineers who notice the flow shortfall then add capacity margin on top of an already-wrong calculation, ending with a valve that is both undersized in principle and oversized in travel terms.) What happens in service: the loop drives the valve wide open trying to reach setpoint, saturates, and the controller winds up (A58, Page 5); the plant cannot achieve maximum rate; and because the valve is fully open across a choked drop, it is cavitating hard at its most damaging condition, so trim life is measured in months. The correct sequence is always: check for choking first, cap ΔP at ΔPchoked, size on that, and then fix the cavitation — with a lower-recovery body, staged trim, higher back pressure, or by removing the excess pressure at source.

Choked Flow, Cavitation & Flashing

One pressure profile explains all three. Learn to draw it and you will never confuse them again.

As liquid accelerates through the trim, static pressure falls to a minimum at the vena contracta — the narrowest, fastest point just downstream of the throttling gap — and then partially recovers as the flow decelerates. Everything damaging happens because that minimum can fall below the liquid's vapour pressure, so the liquid boils inside the valve.

Quick check: A boiler feedwater valve handles water at 150 °C (pv ≈ 4.76 bar a) with p₁ = 20 bar a and p₂ = 8 bar a. Globe valve, FL = 0.9, water pc = 221 bar. Is it cavitating, flashing, or safe?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Work through the tests in order. 1 · FF: FF = 0.96 − 0.28√(pv/pc) = 0.96 − 0.28√(4.76/221) = 0.96 − 0.28 × 0.1468 = 0.96 − 0.0411 = 0.919. Note it is meaningfully below 0.96 here because the water is hot — a correction that matters on feedwater and is often skipped. 2 · Flashing test: is p₂ ≤ FF·pv? FF·pv = 0.919 × 4.76 = 4.37 bar, and p₂ = 8 bar > 4.37, so not flashing ✓. 3 · Choked test: ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv) = 0.81 × (20 − 4.37) = 0.81 × 15.63 = 12.66 bar. Actual ΔP = 20 − 8 = 12 bar, which is just below 12.66 — so not choked, but only by 5%. 4 · Cavitation index: σ = (p₁ − pv)/(p₁ − p₂) = (20 − 4.76)/12 = 15.24/12 = 1.27. That is a low sigma; for most globe trims the incipient-cavitation limit is well above this, so the valve is operating in developed, damaging cavitation even though it has not technically choked. Verdict: cavitating hard, and within 5% of choking. The service is also demanding because hot water flashes readily as it moves downstream. Recommendations: specify multi-stage anti-cavitation trim (feedwater valves are a classic application and are almost always supplied this way), verify with the vendor's σ data at the actual duty rather than the generic FL, use hardened trim materials, check the minimum-flow and start-up cases which are usually worse than the normal case, and confirm there is a minimum-flow recirculation path so the valve is never throttled to near-closed against full pump head.

Gas & Steam Sizing

Compressible flow expands as it passes the valve, so density changes on the way through. That single fact produces the expansion factor and the sonic limit.

Why Gas Is Different
A liquid's density is essentially constant through the valve. A gas EXPANDS as pressure falls, so its density at the vena contracta is lower than at the inlet, and the simple Q = C√(ΔP/ρ) relation overstates the flow. IEC 60534 handles this with the expansion factor Y, and caps the usable pressure ratio at the sonic limit. The pressure differential ratio: x = ΔP / p₁ = (p₁ − p₂)/p₁ The expansion factor: Y = 1 − x / (3·Fγ·xT) bounded by 0.667 ≤ Y ≤ 1.0 xT = pressure differential ratio factor at choked flow (a VALVE property, the gas analogue of FL): globe ≈ 0.70–0.75, rotary ≈ 0.60, butterfly ≈ 0.30–0.50, anti-noise trim ≈ 0.80+ Fγ = specific heat ratio factor = k / 1.40 air/N₂ k = 1.40 → Fγ = 1.00 · steam k = 1.33 → 0.95 · natural gas k = 1.31 → 0.94 CHOKING (sonic / critical flow): Flow chokes when x ≥ Fγ·xT , at which point Y = 0.667 and flow no longer increases with further pressure drop. Always cap x at FγxT in sizing. Note this typically happens around x ≈ 0.7 — i.e. p₂ ≈ 0.3·p₁ — far earlier than beginners expect, and much earlier still on a butterfly valve (xT ≈ 0.3 → x ≈ 0.3).     Choking is normal in steam and gas service, not exceptional. Pressure-reducing duties routinely run at x well above FγxT. Once choked, the valve's capacity depends only on p₁ — so a downstream pressure change does nothing, and the loop's process gain changes character entirely.
  • 3
    Choked gas flow means high velocity and therefore noise. Aerodynamic noise rises steeply once the flow goes sonic, and 100+ dBA is easily reached.   • 4
    Check the outlet velocity. Typical limits are roughly 0.3 Mach for continuous service in the valve outlet, and lower still where noise or erosion matter. A valve that is correctly sized on Cv can still be unacceptable on velocity, which is what drives you to a larger body with reduced trim.

The practical lesson: on compressible service always compute x against FγxT for the specific body being considered — the same duty can be comfortably unchoked on a globe and firmly choked on a butterfly.

Inherent Characteristics

How Cv varies with travel — a property of the trim alone, measured at constant pressure drop.

The Three Standard Characteristics
Measured on a test bench at constant ΔP, so it is purely a geometric property of the plug and cage. Let h = fractional travel (0 to 1) and φ = C/Cmax: LINEAR φ = h Equal increments of travel give equal increments of Cv. Constant inherent gain: dφ/dh = 1. EQUAL PERCENTAGE (=%) φ = R(h−1) R = inherent rangeability, typically 50 Equal increments of travel give equal PERCENTAGE increases in Cv. At R = 50: φ(0) = 1/50 = 0.02 · φ(0.5) = 0.141 · φ(1) = 1.0 Gain rises exponentially with travel: dφ/dh = φ·ln R. The gain is PROPORTIONAL to the current flow — remember this, it is the key to Page 8. QUICK OPENING φ ≈ √h (approximately; the shape varies by manufacturer) Most of the capacity in the first third of travel. Used for on/off and relief duty, rarely for throttling. MODIFIED / CUSTOM: hyperbolic, modified parabolic, and characterised digital positioners (Page 10) can produce any curve you specify.
Why Equal Percentage Exists
Differentiate the equal-percentage law: φ = R(h−1) dφ/dh = φ · ln R The gain at any point is PROPORTIONAL to the flow at that point. That is the entire design intent, and it exists to cancel a specific problem. In a real piping system the pressure drop available to the valve FALLS as flow rises (Page 8), which by itself makes the valve's effective gain DROP at high flow. An equal-percentage trim's gain RISES with flow by exactly the kind of factor needed to compensate — so the two effects cancel and the installed characteristic comes out approximately LINEAR. This is why: · Use EQUAL PERCENTAGE when the valve takes a SMALL share of the system drop (typically < 25–30%), i.e. most temperature, level and pressure loops on long pipe runs, heat exchangers, and pump systems with steep curves. · Use LINEAR when the valve takes a LARGE share of the system drop (> 50–60%, so ΔP is nearly constant), and for most FLOW loops where the measurement itself closes the loop around the nonlinearity. · Use QUICK OPENING for on/off, relief and self-actuated duties — not for modulating control. Rangeability caution: the catalogue "50:1" is an INHERENT figure derived from the trim geometry. The usable installed rangeability is always smaller — limited by the valve's resolution, deadband and the point at which the seat begins to throttle, which in practice is around 10–20% travel.

That is worth pausing on: at nearly half travel the valve is passing under a tenth of its capacity — which is exactly the intended behaviour, and also why an equal-percentage valve looks oversized to someone reading travel alone. Inherent gain: dφ/dh = φ·ln R = 0.0956 × 3.912 = 0.374. A linear valve has dφ/dh = 1.0 everywhere, so at 40% travel the equal-percentage valve's gain is 0.374× the linear valve's — about 37%. At 80% travel it would be φ = 50−0.2 = 0.4573 with gain 1.789, i.e. 1.8× the linear valve; and at full travel the gain is 3.91×. So across the 40–100% travel band the equal-percentage gain varies by more than ten to one. The engineering point: that large inherent gain variation is not a defect — it is deliberately shaped to cancel the opposite variation produced by the piping system, so that the installed gain (the only one the controller experiences) comes out roughly constant. Whether it succeeds depends entirely on the valve's share of the system pressure drop, which is the subject of the next page.

Installed Characteristic & Valve Authority

The characteristic the controller actually feels. Get this wrong and the loop is untunable — get it right and one fixed tuning works across the range.

How the System Distorts the Characteristic
The valve does not sit on a test bench with constant ΔP. It sits in a system where ΔPvalve = ΔPtotal available − (pipe + equipment losses) = ΔPtotal − K·Q² As the valve opens, flow rises, losses rise as Q², and the valve's own pressure drop collapses. So opening further buys less and less extra flow: the installed curve saturates. VALVE AUTHORITY (also called the pressure-drop ratio): β = ΔPvalve at maximum flow / ΔPtotal at maximum flow β → 1.0 the valve owns nearly all the drop; installed ≈ inherent characteristic β ≈ 0.5 moderate distortion β ≈ 0.25 heavy distortion — a linear valve behaves almost quick-opening β < 0.1 the valve barely controls anything; the pipe does. Unusable. DESIGN RULES · Aim for β ≥ 0.25–0.5 at maximum flow. Below 0.25 the valve has too little authority for good control, whatever trim you fit. · Low authority (β < 0.3) → EQUAL PERCENTAGE trim, whose rising gain cancels the falling ΔP. · High authority (β > 0.5) → LINEAR trim, since there is little to cancel. · Higher authority costs pumping energy — you are deliberately throttling. That trade-off is why VFDs often beat throttling outright (A47). THE FLOW EQUATION FOR THE INSTALLED CASE (constant total head): Q = C·√(ΔPv/SG) and ΔPv = ΔPt − K·Q² → Q²·SG = C²(ΔPt − K·Q²) → Q = C·√( ΔPt / (SG + C²·K) ) A closed form — which is what the demo below evaluates at every travel point.

Quick check: A heat exchanger's temperature loop uses a linear-trim valve that owns 15% of the system pressure drop at maximum flow. Describe the loop's behaviour, and give two fixes and one non-fix.
Tap to reveal ↓A: With β = 0.15, a linear trim is heavily distorted toward quick-opening behaviour: most of the achievable flow arrives in the first 20–30% of travel, after which further opening buys almost nothing because the valve's own ΔP has collapsed. In practice the loop is oscillatory at low load (where it operates on the steep part) and sluggish and unable to reach setpoint at high load (where the curve has flattened). Operators will describe it as "twitchy in the morning, useless at full rate", and successive engineers will have retuned it in opposite directions. Fix 2 — increase the authority. Fit a smaller valve (higher ΔP share) or reduce the system's parasitic losses. This addresses the root cause rather than compensating for it, and it improves resolution too — but it costs pumping energy, so check the pump curve and consider whether a VFD would be better overall (A47). The non-fix: retuning. No set of PID constants can serve a 10:1 gain variation; every retune simply chooses which end of the range to be wrong at. A partial software workaround is a characteriser in a digital positioner (Page 10) to synthesise equal-percentage behaviour, or gain scheduling on flow in the controller — both are legitimate mitigations, but they are compensating for a valve chosen wrongly at specification stage, which is the real lesson.

Fail-Safe Action — a Process Safety Decision
  • 1
    Fail-safe is determined by hazard analysis (HAZOP/LOPA), not by the loop's convenience. Ask: "if the instrument air fails right now, which position leaves the plant in the least dangerous state?" Then check the answer holds during start-up, shutdown and abnormal operation too — some valves have no single safe position, which itself is a finding requiring an engineered solution (lock-up, accumulator, or a second valve).
  • 2
    "Fail in place" is a decision, not a default. Electric actuators fail in place unless given a spring or battery pack. For some duties that is right; for a fuel valve it certainly is not.
  • 3
    Controller action must be set to match. A reverse-acting controller with an ATO valve and a direct-acting one with an ATC valve produce the same loop sign — get the combination wrong and the loop runs away on the first automatic transfer. Verify by pushing the output manually and watching the sign of the PV before leaving the loop in auto (A57, Page 15).
  • 4
    Check the air supply is part of the safety case. A fail-closed valve is only fail-closed if the spring can actually overcome the process force and friction at the worst-case ΔP. Verify the spring thrust against the maximum unbalance force, not the normal one.
Quick check: A 100 mm unbalanced globe valve throttles 30 bar to 5 bar. Estimate the unbalance force on the plug and comment on the actuator implications and the alternative.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Take the plug/seat bore as roughly the valve size, 100 mm, so A = π/4 × 0.1² = 7.85 × 10⁻³ m². The differential is 25 bar = 25 × 10⁵ Pa. (2) Use a piston (double-acting) actuator with higher supply pressure, which develops far more thrust per unit area — but then fail-safe action must be engineered with a spring return, or an air reservoir and lock-up valve, and that system becomes safety-critical and must be tested. (3) Consider a rotary valve, where the fluid force produces torque rather than axial thrust and the geometry is more favourable. Positioners & Diagnostics

A positioner is a fast inner cascade loop around the valve stem — and a modern one is also the best diagnostic instrument on the plant.

What a Positioner Does
Without a positioner, the controller's 4–20 mA drives an I/P converter and the resulting air pressure balances against the spring. Stem position then depends on friction, process force, spring tolerance and supply pressure — none of which the control system knows about. A positioner closes a local feedback loop on actual stem position: controller OP → positioner setpoint → compares with measured stem position → modulates air to the actuator until they agree This is cascade control (A58, Page 2) applied to the valve itself, and it delivers exactly the cascade benefits: · rejects packing friction, process force and supply-pressure variation locally · linearises the actuator · makes the valve's response repeatable, so the DCS can trust "50% means 50%" · allows characterisation, split-range, travel limits and calibration in software Rule of thumb: a positioner should be at least 5× faster than the process loop around it — the same speed-separation requirement as any cascade. On a fast flow loop that is not automatic, and an over-aggressive positioner can oscillate against the process loop; on a slow temperature loop it is never an issue.

The Stiction Limit Cycle — Why It Cannot Be Tuned Out
Why the cycle is self-sustaining: 1. The PV is off setpoint, so the integral term ramps the output. 2. Nothing moves — static friction exceeds the actuator force. 3. Eventually the force overcomes friction and the stem jumps, overshooting the position that would have corrected the error. 4. The error reverses; the integral ramps back the other way; repeat. The period is set by the integral time and the size of the stiction band, NOT by the process dynamics — which is why the cycle period often looks "wrong" for the loop, and why it survives every retune.

The correct actions: confirm the diagnosis by putting the loop in manual (the cycle stops) and by the sawtooth-PV / stepped-MV signature or a positioner's deadband diagnostic; then repair the valve — repack, service the actuator, check for a bent stem or a failing positioner. Erosion & Material Damage

Cavitation damage

Pitted, spongy, "cinder-like" metal loss downstream of the vena contracta — on the plug skirt, cage outlet or body wall. Caused by bubble implosion microjets. Hardened trim delays it; only removing the cavitation stops it.

Flashing erosion

Smooth, polished, directional wear — high-velocity droplets sand-blasting the outlet and downstream pipe. Cure: hardened materials, expanded outlet, straight downstream run, velocity limits.

Particulate erosion

Slurries and catalyst fines. Cure: rotary/ball valves with shear action, hardened or ceramic trim, avoid small clearances (which rules out most anti-cavitation trim).

Wire drawing

A fine groove cut across the seat by high-velocity flow through a nearly-closed valve. The classic symptom of a grossly oversized valve throttling at 5% travel — and it destroys the shut-off class.

  • !
    Read the damage pattern as a diagnosis. Pitting downstream of the throat = cavitation. Polished directional wear = flashing or particulate erosion. A groove across the seat = the valve is too big and has been throttling on the seat. Each points at a different fix, and a valve autopsy at the next shutdown is one of the most informative half-hours available to a control engineer.
Quick check: A gas letdown valve measures 103 dBA at 1 m. The proposal is to lag the downstream pipe. Assess.

(5) Inspect the downstream small-bore connections for existing fatigue cracking, and check whether any have been added since commissioning. The principle: noise is a symptom of energy being dumped in the wrong place; move the dissipation into properly designed trim rather than into the pipe wall.

Quick check: A specification demands Class VI shut-off, ISO 15848 Class A emissions, and "excellent control performance" on a modulating loop. What is the conflict, and how would you resolve it?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The conflict is friction. Class VI needs a soft seat and a high seat load; ISO 15848 Class A needs very tight packing. Both demand more force, and packing friction is the direct cause of stiction — which is the single biggest destroyer of modulating control performance (Page 11). The specification is asking for a valve that seals like an isolation valve, emits like a sealed vessel, and moves like a laboratory instrument. Those pull in opposite directions. How to resolve it, in order. (1) Challenge the shut-off requirement. Ask what Class VI is actually for. If it is maintenance isolation, that belongs to a separate isolation valve, not the control valve — and Class IV is then perfectly adequate, removing most of the seat-load problem. If it is process leakage cost, quantify it: Class IV allows 0.01% of rated capacity, which is often trivially small in absolute terms. (2) Keep the emissions requirement — it is usually regulatory and non-negotiable — and specify live-loaded, low-emission packing, which gives stable, predictable friction rather than friction that rises as packing relaxes. (3) Specify a smart digital positioner with sufficient actuator thrust margin. The positioner closes a fast local loop on stem position and rejects much of the residual friction, and its diagnostics let you trend the friction so degradation is caught before the loop starts cycling. (4) Oversize the actuator deliberately — a generous thrust margin over the calculated friction-plus-seat-load is the cheapest insurance for control quality on a high-friction valve. (5) Write measurable acceptance criteria rather than the phrase "excellent control performance": specify maximum deadband (say ≤ 0.5% of travel), resolution, T86 stroking time and travel-deviation limits, tested at the actual operating pressure — because a valve tested on the bench with no ΔP will pass criteria it cannot meet in service. The professional point: when a specification contains conflicting requirements, the engineering contribution is to identify the conflict, quantify each requirement's real purpose, and relax the one that is not doing useful work — not to pass the contradiction to the vendor.

Other Final Elements

A valve is not the only way to manipulate a process — and often not the best one.

Variable-Speed Drive vs Throttling Valve
For a centrifugal pump or fan, the affinity laws give: Q ∝ N H ∝ N² P ∝ N³ Throttling to reduce flow keeps the machine at full speed and burns the excess head across the valve. Reducing speed instead cuts power by the CUBE. Example: reduce flow to 70% of design. Throttled: power ≈ 90–94% of full load (the pump rides up its curve) VFD: power ≈ 0.70³ = 34% of full load A saving of roughly 60 percentage points of motor power, continuously. When the VFD wins: centrifugal pumps and fans with a mostly friction-dominated system, long operating hours, significant turndown. Payback is often under two years (A08, A47). When the valve still wins: mostly static head (the pump must still produce it, so speed reduction has little effect and risks running below minimum flow); positive-displacement machines (speed control works, but a recycle valve may be needed anyway); very fast response requirements; multiple users on a common header that each need independent control; and any duty where a fail-safe closure is required — a VFD cannot shut off flow reliably. Very often the answer is both: a VFD for the bulk energy saving and a small trim valve for fine control.

(6) Power quality — a large VFD adds harmonic current; check the transformer's capacity and TDD against IEEE 519 (A56), and specify a line reactor as standard. (7) The NPSH and cavitation margin at reduced speed. If those check out, this is one of the highest-return interventions available on any plant, and the control-valve question resolves itself.

The Specification Checklist
  • 1
    Process data at three flows — minimum, normal, maximum — with p₁, p₂, temperature, SG, viscosity, vapour pressure and critical pressure. Get the pump curve, not just a design point.
  • 2
    Size for Cv at each case, checking choked flow first and capping ΔP at ΔPchoked. Apply FP for reducers and FR for viscous service.
  • 3
    Verify travel at all three cases: max at 80–90%, normal at 50–70%, min above 10–20%. If not, the valve is the wrong size — this check, not the Cv number, is the deliverable.
  • 4
    Compute valve authority at maximum flow and choose the characteristic from it: β < 0.3 → equal percentage; β > 0.5 → linear. Confirm installed gain varies less than about 2:1 across the working band.
  • 5
    Check cavitation, flashing and noise at every case — including start-up and minimum flow, which are frequently the worst. Specify anti-cavitation or low-noise trim where the numbers demand it.
  • 6
    Determine fail-safe action from hazard analysis, then verify the spring can actually achieve it against maximum unbalance force and friction.
  • 7
    Specify dynamics measurably: deadband ≤ 0.5% of travel, resolution, T86 ≤ 10% of the process time constant, tested at operating pressure — not on a bench with no ΔP.
  • 8
    Materials, ANSI class at design temperature, leakage class, packing and certifications (NACE, ATEX, ISO 15848, fire-safe) — and do not over-specify shut-off at the cost of control.
  • 9
    Smart positioner with diagnostics configured, a commissioning valve signature captured, and travel-deviation alarms routed to maintenance rather than to the operator.
  • 10
    Document everything, including any characterisation curve loaded into the positioner. An undocumented curve makes the valve behave unlike its own datasheet for the next engineer.
The Eight Ideas That Carry Everything
  • 1
    The final element is the weak link. Most poor loops are valve problems wearing a tuning disguise. Put the loop in manual and travel-test the valve before touching a controller.
  • 2
    Cv = Q√(SG/ΔP) in gpm/psi; Kv the same in m³/h and bar; Kv = 0.865 Cv. Size at three flows, because ΔP falls as flow rises.
  • 3
    ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv). Beyond it, more pressure drop buys no more flow — cap ΔP at this value in sizing, then fix the cavitation.
  • 4
    Cavitation collapses, flashing does not. Cavitation needs p₂ > pv and is a valve problem you can solve; flashing means p₂ ≤ pv and is a process condition you must design for.
  • 5
    Inherent is measured at constant ΔP; installed is what the controller feels. Equal-percentage gain rises with flow, which cancels the falling ΔP of a real system — that is the entire reason it exists.
  • 6
    Valve authority decides the trim. β = ΔPvalve/ΔPtotal at maximum flow: below 0.3 use equal percentage, above 0.5 use linear, below 0.1 nothing will work.
  • 7
    A positioner is a cascade loop on stem position — it rejects friction and process force locally, and a smart one is the best diagnostic instrument on the plant.
  • 8

⚠️ Six Ways Valve Engineering Goes Wrong
① The oversizing spiral

Margin on margin on rounding on line size, producing a valve that delivers design flow at 15% travel — worst resolution, steepest characteristic, seat wire-drawing and unstable control. Cure: add margin once, deliberately, and check travel at all three flow cases.

② Sizing at a single design ΔP

Ignoring that ΔP falls as Q² losses rise, so the valve is wrong at both ends of the range. Cure: get the pump curve and size at minimum, normal and maximum with their own pressure drops.

③ Wrong trim for the authority

Linear trim on a low-authority system gives quick-opening behaviour and 10:1 gain variation — oscillatory at low load, sluggish at high. Cure: compute β at specification stage and choose the characteristic from it.

④ Ignoring cavitation, or treating flashing as cavitation

A high-recovery butterfly on a high-ΔP liquid duty destroys itself in months; anti-cavitation trim fitted to a flashing service does nothing at all. Cure: run the FL, FF and σ arithmetic at every case, and test p₂ against pv first.

⑤ Over-specifying shut-off and emissions

Class VI plus tight packing means high seat load and high friction, so the valve seals beautifully and controls badly. Cure: use a separate isolation valve; specify Class IV plus live-loaded packing and a smart positioner.

⑥ Retuning a mechanical fault

Three engineers retuning a loop whose valve has 3% stiction, each making it worse, while the real fault is packing friction. Cure: loop in manual, travel test, positioner diagnostics — then a work order, not a tuning change.

Final check 1: Explain in one paragraph why a valve sized on the normal duty alone is almost always wrong, using the relationship between ΔP and flow.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because the pressure drop available to the valve is not a fixed property of the service — it is whatever the system leaves over after the pipe, fittings and equipment have taken their share, and those losses rise as the square of flow: ΔPvalve = ΔPavailable − K·Q². (With a centrifugal pump, ΔPavailable falls as flow rises too, compounding the effect.) So the maximum-flow case combines the largest flow with the smallest pressure drop, and both push the required Cv upward — Cv = Q√(SG/ΔP) grows in the numerator and shrinks in the denominator simultaneously. A valve sized at the normal duty therefore has nowhere near enough capacity for maximum flow, and the plant simply cannot reach rate while the loop saturates and winds up. Conversely, at minimum flow the valve has the largest ΔP available, so it must throttle down to a very small Cv — and if the valve was sized generously it will be sitting at a few percent travel there, where resolution is worst, the characteristic is steepest and the seat is being wire-drawn. Sizing at one point cannot see either failure. That is why the deliverable of a sizing exercise is not a Cv number but a travel check at three flows: maximum at 80–90%, normal at 50–70%, minimum above 10–20%.
Final check 2: A globe valve (FL = 0.9) and a butterfly (FL = 0.65) are offered for the same liquid duty. Quantify the difference in allowable pressure drop and explain the physical reason.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Allowable (choked) pressure drop is ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv), so for the same fluid and inlet pressure the two valves differ by the ratio of the squares: (0.90/0.65)² = 0.81/0.4225 = 1.92. The globe valve tolerates almost twice the pressure drop before choking and cavitating. Concretely, with p₁ = 12 bar a and cold water (FFpv ≈ 0.07, negligible), the globe allows about 0.81 × 12 = 9.7 bar while the butterfly allows only 0.4225 × 12 = 5.1 bar. The physical reason is pressure recovery. FL measures how much of the pressure dropped at the vena contracta is recovered downstream. A butterfly valve is a high-recovery body: the flow path is smooth and streamlined, so the fluid accelerates to a very high velocity at the disc edge — a very deep pressure dip — and then decelerates efficiently, recovering most of it. The measured inlet-to-outlet ΔP therefore understates how low the pressure actually went inside the valve. A globe valve is low-recovery: its tortuous path dissipates energy in turbulence, so the vena contracta is shallower for the same overall ΔP and much less is recovered. Since cavitation is decided by the minimum pressure reached, not the net drop, the streamlined valve cavitates first. This is the counter-intuitive result worth remembering: the more efficient valve is the one that cavitates sooner, and it is why the cheap large-bore butterfly is so often the wrong answer on high-ΔP liquid service.
Final check 3: Why does equal-percentage trim linearise a low-authority system? Argue from the two gain terms.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The installed gain the controller experiences is the product of two effects. First, the trim's inherent gain. For equal percentage, φ = R(h−1), so dφ/dh = φ·ln R — the gain is proportional to the current flow, small when nearly closed and large when nearly open. Second, the system's effect on pressure drop. In a low-authority installation the valve owns only a small share of the total drop, and as flow rises the pipe losses (∝ Q²) consume more of the available head, so ΔPvalve collapses. Since Q = C√(ΔPv/SG), a falling ΔPv means each additional unit of Cv buys progressively less extra flow — the system contributes a gain that decreases with flow. Multiply the two together: an inherent gain rising roughly in proportion to flow, times a system factor falling with flow, gives an installed gain that is approximately constant — a straight line from travel to flow, which is precisely what a fixed-gain PID controller needs. That cancellation is the entire design intent of the equal-percentage law, and it explains both selection rules: at high authority there is nothing to cancel (ΔP is nearly constant), so equal percentage over-compensates and linear trim is correct; at very low authority (β below about 0.1) the system's distortion is so severe that even equal percentage cannot recover it, and the answer is to give the valve more authority rather than to keep changing trim.
Final check 4: Name the four properties of any final element that determine loop performance, and state what each one costs the loop.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Gain, and how it varies with travel — the installed characteristic. A constant gain lets one tuning serve the whole range; a gain varying more than about 2:1 means the loop is mis-tuned somewhere in its operating band, oscillatory at one end and sluggish at the other. Cost: either gain scheduling, or accepting poor performance over part of the range. (2) Deadband, hysteresis and resolution — the smallest change the element can reliably make, and the signal wasted on a reversal. Cost: a floor on achievable control precision, and if stiction is present, a sustained limit cycle whose amplitude is roughly the stiction band times the process gain, driven by the integral term and immune to retuning. (3) Dynamics — dead time Td and stroking time T86. The element appears as an FOPDT block in series with the process, so its dead time is subtracted straight from the loop's phase budget Δθ = PM(rad)/ωgc, and its lag adds phase at crossover. Cost: lost phase margin, hence a slower loop or reduced robustness; the guideline is T86 ≤ 10% of the process time constant. (4) Fail-safe behaviour and available thrust/authority — what it does on loss of signal or power, and whether it can reach the positions the loop demands at worst-case process force. Cost: if the element saturates or cannot move against the differential, the controller winds up and the strategy fails regardless of tuning. The general point: these four apply unchanged to a globe valve, a VFD, a damper, a dosing pump or a thyristor stack — characterise them, and the control analysis of A57 and A58 follows directly.
Final check 5: Give three situations where the correct answer is not a control valve at all.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) A throttled centrifugal pump or fan in a friction-dominated system with long operating hours. The valve exists only to destroy energy the machine produced; a VFD reduces power as the cube of speed (70% flow → about 34% power against roughly 90% throttled), typically paying back in under two years. Check static head, minimum flow, fail-safe requirements and harmonics before committing (A08, A47, A56). (2) Positive isolation for maintenance or safety. A control valve leaks even at Class VI, and its shut-off degrades as the balance seal and seat wear. Specifying a tighter leakage class to get isolation is an expensive way to get the wrong thing — fit a proper isolation valve, and for a hazard, an independent SIS shutdown valve with documented PFD and proof testing (A52). (3) Very low or very precise dosing duty. Below the smallest practical trim a control valve throttles on its seat, wire-draws and controls badly; a metering/dosing pump gives far better accuracy and rangeability at low flow. Honourable mentions: an on/off element with hysteresis or time-proportioning where a solenoid or heater is the natural actuator and a PID would simply chatter it to destruction; electric heating via a thyristor stack instead of a steam valve where response and cleanliness matter; and the case where the honest answer is that the pressure drop should not exist — resize the pump, trim the impeller or change the line size, so that neither a valve nor its cavitation is needed at all.

The rule is authority-driven, not habitual. WE2 · Cavitation Assessment and Remedy Selection

Given: Condensate at 90 °C (pv = 0.70 bar a, pc = 221 bar) is let down from p₁ = 9.0 bar a to p₂ = 1.6 bar a, 25 m³/h. A butterfly valve (FL = 0.65) has been proposed. Assess and recommend.

Step 1 — liquid critical pressure ratio factor FF = 0.96 − 0.28√(pv/pc) = 0.96 − 0.28√(0.70/221) = 0.96 − 0.28 × 0.05628 = 0.96 − 0.01576 = 0.944 Step 2 — flashing test is p₂ ≤ FF·pv ? FF·pv = 0.944 × 0.70 = 0.661 bar. p₂ = 1.6 bar > 0.661 → NOT flashing ✓ (so a valve-side solution is possible) Step 3 — choked pressure drop, butterfly ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv) = 0.4225 × (9.0 − 0.661) = 0.4225 × 8.339 = 3.52 bar Actual ΔP = 9.0 − 1.6 = 7.40 bar → 7.40 > 3.52 CHOKED — and by more than a factor of two. Step 4 — cavitation index σ = (p₁ − pv)/(p₁ − p₂) = (9.0 − 0.70)/7.40 = 8.30/7.40 = 1.12 A σ of 1.12 is very low — severe, damaging cavitation. Step 5 — what a globe valve would give ΔPchoked = 0.81 × 8.339 = 6.75 bar — still below the required 7.40 bar. Even a globe valve chokes on this duty. Body style alone is not enough. Step 6 — required capacity, sized on the CHOKED ΔP (Page 4's rule) Butterfly: Kv = 25 × √(1/3.52) = 25 × 0.533 = 13.3 (Cv 15.4) Globe: Kv = 25 × √(1/6.75) = 25 × 0.385 = 9.6 (Cv 11.1) Note the butterfly needs ~39% MORE capacity for the same duty, because it can only use half the pressure drop productively. Step 7 — two-stage check (anti-cavitation trim, FL = 0.95) ΔPchoked = 0.9025 × 8.339 = 7.53 bar > 7.40 bar required ✓ (just) σ is still low, so specify multi-stage trim and confirm against the vendor's own σ data rather than FL alone.

Discussion / insight. The instructive part is Step 5: the reflex answer "use a globe valve instead of a butterfly" is not sufficient here — the duty is severe enough that even a low-recovery body chokes. That is the difference between a marginal case (where body style fixes it) and a genuinely severe one (where staged trim is mandatory). Step 6 also quantifies a point that surprises people: a high-recovery valve needs more capacity on a choked duty, not less, because the sizing equation may only credit it with ΔPchoked. Finally, note the professional caveat: FL predicts choking, while damaging cavitation begins well before choking. Passing the ΔPchoked test is necessary but not sufficient — always check σ against the vendor's incipient and damage limits for the specific trim.

WE3 · Valve Authority and Trim Selection

Given: A heat exchanger circuit. Pump delivers 4.5 bar at zero flow; at the design flow of 100 m³/h the exchanger and pipework absorb 3.6 bar, leaving 0.9 bar for the valve. Determine the authority, choose the trim, and quantify the installed gain variation.

Step 1 — authority β = ΔPvalve/ΔPtotal at design flow = 0.9/4.5 = 0.20 → LOW authority Step 2 — system resistance K = 3.6/100² = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ bar/(m³/h)² Step 3 — size the valve for design flow at 85% travel Required Kv at design = 100 × √(1/0.9) = 100 × 1.054 = 105.4 LINEAR: Kvrated = 105.4/0.85 = 124 EQUAL %: Kvrated = 105.4/0.556 = 190 Step 4 — installed flow at any travel (closed form from Page 8) Q = Kv·√( ΔPt / (SG + Kv²·K) ) with ΔPt = 4.5, SG = 1, K = 3.6e−4 LINEAR trim (Kvrated 124): h = 0.20 → Kv = 24.8 → Q = 24.8√(4.5/(1+0.2214)) = 24.8 × 1.9203 = 47.6 m³/h h = 0.50 → Kv = 62.0 → Q = 62.0√(4.5/(1+1.3838)) = 62.0 × 1.3739 = 85.2 m³/h h = 0.80 → Kv = 99.2 → Q = 99.2√(4.5/(1+3.5426)) = 99.2 × 0.9952 = 98.7 m³/h Gain over 20→50% travel: (85.2−47.6)/0.30 = 125 m³/h per unit travel Gain over 50→80% travel: (98.7−85.2)/0.30 = 45 m³/h per unit travel Gain ratio ≈ 125/45 = 2.8 : 1 — and worse at the extremes EQUAL % trim (Kvrated 190, R = 50): h = 0.20 → φ = 50^(−0.8) = 0.0437 → Kv = 8.31 → Q = 8.31 × 2.0954 = 17.4 h = 0.50 → φ = 50^(−0.5) = 0.1414 → Kv = 26.87 → Q = 26.87 × 1.8899 = 50.8 h = 0.80 → φ = 50^(−0.2) = 0.4573 → Kv = 86.89 → Q = 86.89 × 1.1002 = 95.6 (check: h = 0.85 → Kv = 105.7 → Q = 100.0 ✓ design flow at 85% travel) Gain 20→50%: (50.8−17.4)/0.30 = 111 Gain 50→80%: (95.6−50.8)/0.30 = 149 Gain ratio ≈ 149/111 = 1.34 : 1 — and in the opposite direction Step 5 — verdict Equal percentage cuts the gain variation from 2.8:1 to 1.34:1 and, crucially, spreads the flow range across the stroke instead of delivering nearly half of it in the first 20% of travel. Specify equal percentage.

Discussion / insight. Look at what the linear trim does at 20% travel: it is already passing 47.6 m³/h — nearly half the design flow in the first fifth of the stroke — and by 50% travel it is at 85% of design. The equal-percentage trim passes only 17.4 m³/h at the same 20% travel. The valve has almost no useful control above half travel; that is the quick-opening distortion of Page 8 made numerical. The equal-percentage trim spreads the same range far more evenly. Note the gain ratio also reverses direction: linear gives high gain at low travel, equal percentage slightly high at high travel — meaning equal percentage mildly over-compensates at β = 0.20, which is expected and harmless. The professional habit demonstrated here is to compute installed gain at three travel points rather than to argue about characteristics in the abstract; a 2.8:1 variation is a loop that cannot be tuned once, and a 1.6:1 variation is one that can.

WE4 · Actuator Thrust and Fail-Safe Verification

Given: An 80 mm unbalanced globe valve, air-to-open, must fail closed. Maximum ΔP at the closed position is 18 bar. Packing friction is 900 N, required seat load for Class IV is 1500 N. The spring–diaphragm actuator has an effective diaphragm area of 0.093 m² and a bench set of 0.4–2.0 bar with 1.4 bar available supply. Verify.

Step 1 — unbalance force at the seat Plug area A = π/4 × 0.080² = π/4 × 0.0064 = 5.027 × 10⁻³ m² Funbalance = ΔP × A = 18 × 10⁵ × 5.027e−3 = 9 048 N Step 2 — force the SPRING must deliver to close and seat the valve On air failure the spring must overcome friction and provide seat load; the unbalance force here ASSISTS closure (flow-to-open arrangement pushes the plug toward the seat) — but a conservative check assumes it opposes: Worst case: Fspring required = 9 048 + 900 + 1 500 = 11 448 N Assisting case: Fspring required = 900 + 1 500 = 2 400 N → the FLOW DIRECTION relative to the plug decides which applies. This must be confirmed from the valve drawing, not assumed. Flow-to-close arrangements can also cause instability near the seat, so vendors often specify flow-to-open. Step 3 — spring force available Bench set 0.4–2.0 bar over area 0.093 m²: Spring force at fully-closed end = 0.4 × 10⁵ × 0.093 = 3 720 N Spring force at fully-open end = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.093 = 18 600 N The spring stores ~18 600 N when compressed (valve open) and delivers a MINIMUM of 3 720 N as it reaches the seat. Step 4 — verify the ASSISTING case Available at seat 3 720 N vs required 2 400 N → margin 1.55× ✓ ACCEPTABLE Step 5 — verify the WORST case (unbalance opposing) Available 3 720 N vs required 11 448 N → FAILS by a factor of 3 ✗ In that arrangement the valve would NOT close on air failure — the fail-safe action would not occur, which is a safety finding, not a performance one. Step 6 — check the OPENING capability Air force at 1.4 bar = 1.4 × 10⁵ × 0.093 = 13 020 N Must overcome spring (up to 18 600 N at full travel) + friction 900 N → 13 020 N < 19 500 N: the actuator cannot reach full travel at this supply pressure. Requires higher supply (≈ 2.1 bar) or a larger diaphragm.

Discussion / insight. This example deliberately fails two checks, because that is what real verification does. Three lessons. (1) Flow direction matters enormously on an unbalanced plug — a 9 kN force either helps or defeats your fail-safe action depending on which way the fluid pushes the plug, and it must be read off the drawing. (2) Fail-safe is a calculation, not a label. Writing "fail closed" on a data sheet does not make the spring strong enough; the spring must be verified against the maximum unbalance force plus friction plus seat load, and this is exactly the check that gets skipped. (3) Check both directions. Step 6 shows the actuator cannot fully open at the available supply pressure — a valve that fails closed reliably but only opens to 70% is equally unfit. The engineering conclusion here is either a balanced plug (cutting the 9 kN to a few hundred newtons, at the cost of shut-off class), a larger actuator, or a piston actuator with engineered fail-safe — each with the trade-offs of Page 9.

WE5 · Valve Dynamics and the Loop's Phase Budget

Given: A flow loop runs at ωgc = 2.5 rad/s with 55° phase margin. The installed valve has Td = 0.6 s, T86 = 5 s, deadband 2.5%, and the process gain is 0.8 (m³/h)/% travel. Assess the valve's suitability and predict the limit cycle.

Step 1 — the loop's dead-time budget (A57, Derivation 4) Δθavailable = PM(rad)/ωgc = (55 × π/180)/2.5 = 0.9599/2.5 = 0.384 s Step 2 — the valve's dead time alone Td = 0.6 s vs budget 0.384 s → the valve's PURE DEAD TIME ALONE EXCEEDS the entire phase margin budget. Phase cost = 57.3 × ωgc × Td = 57.3 × 2.5 × 0.6 = 86° against 55° available → the loop is unstable with this valve. Step 3 — the valve's lag as well T86 ≈ Td + 2τv → τv = (5 − 0.6)/2 = 2.2 s Phase cost of the lag = atan(ωgc·τv) = atan(2.5 × 2.2) = atan(5.5) = 79.7° Total valve phase cost ≈ 86 + 80 = 166° — catastrophic. Step 4 — the T86 guideline check Loop closed-loop time constant ≈ 1/ωgc = 0.4 s Guideline: T86 ≤ 0.1 × process τ. Here T86 = 5 s against a loop that wants to respond in 0.4 s → the valve is 12× too slow for this loop. Step 5 — the limit cycle from deadband/stiction Amplitude ≈ stiction band × process gain = 2.5% × 0.8 = 2.0 m³/h peak jump plus the integral-driven overshoot, so expect a cycle of that order or larger, with a period set by the integral time rather than the process dynamics. Step 6 — the verdict and the options This valve cannot serve this loop. Either: (a) Slow the loop down — retune for ωgc ≈ 0.15 rad/s, where the valve's 0.6 s dead time costs 57.3 × 0.15 × 0.6 ≈ 5° and the lag ≈ 18°. The loop becomes ~17× slower, which may be unacceptable for a flow loop. (b) Replace the valve/actuator with a faster one: a smaller actuator, a volume booster, or a higher-capacity positioner relay. Flow loops need T86 of the order of 1 s. (c) Repair the deadband — 2.5% is a maintenance defect regardless.

Discussion / insight. This is the calculation that connects hardware to control theory, and the numbers are deliberately stark. A flow loop is fast — its closed-loop time constant is a fraction of a second — so a valve with a 5-second stroke is not a marginal choice, it is the dominant dynamic element in the loop. The phase budget makes this quantitative: 0.384 seconds of total latency is all the loop has, and the valve's dead time alone spends 0.6 s of it. Note the practical significance of option (a): you can always retune around a slow valve, but on a flow loop the result is a controller that responds in tens of seconds to a disturbance the process creates in one — so the loop nominally works while delivering none of the performance it was installed for. The examinable principle: the final element's dynamics must be specified from the loop's required bandwidth, which means the control engineer must be involved at valve selection, not handed the result.

Problem Set

Ten problems in ascending difficulty. Attempt each on paper before revealing.

P1. A valve passes 120 m³/h of a liquid with SG = 0.86 at a pressure drop of 3.2 bar. Find Kv and Cv.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Kv = Q√(SG/ΔP) = 120 × √(0.86/3.2) = 120 × √0.26875 = 120 × 0.51841 = 62.2. Cv = 1.156 × 62.2 = 71.9. Check the sanity of the answer: a Kv of 62 corresponds roughly to a 50–80 mm globe valve depending on trim, which is consistent with 120 m³/h. Note the specific gravity works in your favour here — a lighter liquid needs less capacity for the same volumetric flow and pressure drop, because there is less mass to accelerate. A very common error is to omit SG entirely (giving Kv = 67.1, an 8% error) or to invert it. Always carry SG at the operating temperature, not at 15 °C: a hot hydrocarbon can be 10–15% lighter than its ambient value.
P2. Water at 20 °C (pv = 0.023 bar a), p₁ = 10 bar a, p₂ = 2 bar a, globe valve FL = 0.9, pc = 221 bar. Is the flow choked?
Tap to reveal ↓A: FF = 0.96 − 0.28√(0.023/221) = 0.96 − 0.28 × 0.010203 = 0.96 − 0.00286 = 0.957. ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv) = 0.81 × (10 − 0.957 × 0.023) = 0.81 × (10 − 0.022) = 0.81 × 9.978 = 8.08 bar. Actual ΔP = 10 − 2 = 8.0 bar. Since 8.0 < 8.08, the flow is not choked — but only just, at 99% of the choking drop. The engineering reading matters more than the arithmetic: this valve is right on the boundary, so any increase in p₁, any fall in p₂, or any warming of the water (which raises pv steeply) will tip it into choking and cavitation. The cavitation index σ = (10 − 0.023)/8.0 = 1.25, which is low, so it is almost certainly cavitating already even though it has not choked — remember that damaging cavitation begins well before the choking limit. Treat this as a service needing anti-cavitation trim or a raised back pressure, and check the minimum-flow and start-up cases which will be worse.
P3. An equal-percentage valve (R = 50, rated Kv 100) must pass Kv 18. What travel is required? What is its inherent gain there relative to full travel?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Travel: φ = 18/100 = 0.18, and φ = 50(h−1), so h = 1 + ln(0.18)/ln(50) = 1 + (−1.7148)/3.9120 = 1 − 0.4383 = 0.562 = 56.2% travel. Gain: dφ/dh = φ·ln R, so at this point the gain is 0.18 × 3.912 = 0.704 (in units of fractional Cv per fractional travel), while at full travel (φ = 1) it is 1.0 × 3.912 = 3.912. The ratio is 0.704/3.912 = 0.18 — that is, the gain at 56% travel is only 18% of the gain at full travel, which is no coincidence: for an equal-percentage trim the gain is directly proportional to φ, so gain ratio = flow ratio, always. That single property is the definition of the characteristic and the reason it cancels a falling system ΔP. Practical note: 56% travel for the required duty is a good result — comfortably inside the 20–80% working band with room to move in both directions.
P4. A system has 5 bar total head at zero flow. At the 90 m³/h design flow the pipework absorbs 4.1 bar. Compute valve authority and recommend a trim.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Valve ΔP at design = 5.0 − 4.1 = 0.9 bar, so authority β = 0.9/5.0 = 0.18. That is low authority — the valve owns less than a fifth of the system's pressure drop, and the pipework dominates. Recommendation: equal-percentage trim, whose rising gain compensates the sharply falling ΔP, giving an installed characteristic close to linear (Page 8). A linear trim here would behave almost quick-opening, delivering most of the flow in the first quarter of travel with a gain variation of the order of 3:1 or worse. But also flag the underlying issue: β = 0.18 is below the usual 0.25 minimum, so even with the right trim the valve has limited authority, resolution will be poor, and the loop will be sensitive to any change in system resistance (fouling, a partly closed manual valve, a filter blinding). Ask whether the pipework losses are necessary — 4.1 bar of parasitic loss suggests undersized pipe, an over-restrictive exchanger, or a pump oversized for the duty. Increasing β by removing losses or fitting a smaller valve improves control and saves pumping energy; the alternative of a VFD (A47) removes the throttling loss altogether. Required Kv at design = 90 × √(1/0.9) = 94.9, and if you size for design at 85% travel on equal percentage, rated Kv = 94.9/0.556 = 171.
P5. Steam at 15 bar a is reduced to 9 bar a through a globe valve (xT = 0.72). Inlet density is 7.6 kg/m³, flow is 3500 kg/h. Compute x, Y and the required Kv.
Tap to reveal ↓A: x = ΔP/p₁ = (15 − 9)/15 = 0.40. For steam k = 1.33, so Fγ = 1.33/1.40 = 0.95. Choking check: FγxT = 0.95 × 0.72 = 0.684; since x = 0.40 < 0.684, the flow is not choked and we use the actual x. Expansion factor: Y = 1 − x/(3FγxT) = 1 − 0.40/(3 × 0.684) = 1 − 0.40/2.052 = 1 − 0.1949 = 0.805. Required Kv: C = W/(N₆·Y·√(x·p₁·ρ₁)) with N₆ = 27.3 for W [kg/h], p [bar], ρ [kg/m³]. The radicand is x·p₁·ρ₁ = 0.40 × 15 × 7.6 = 45.6, whose square root is 6.753. So Kv = 3500/(27.3 × 0.805 × 6.753) = 3500/148.4 = 23.6, giving Cv = 1.156 × 23.6 = 27.3. Two checks worth stating: Y = 0.805 is well above its floor of 0.667, confirming we are comfortably below sonic conditions; and the inlet density must come from steam tables at the actual condition — 7.6 kg/m³ corresponds to saturated steam near 15 bar, and if the steam were superheated the density would be lower and the required Kv correspondingly larger.
P6. A valve has an unbalanced 60 mm plug and must shut off against 25 bar. Packing friction is 700 N and Class IV seat load requires 1100 N. What actuator thrust is needed, and what is the alternative?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Plug area A = π/4 × 0.060² = π/4 × 0.0036 = 2.827 × 10⁻³ m². Unbalance force = 25 × 10⁵ × 2.827e−3 = 7 069 N. Total worst-case requirement = 7 069 + 700 + 1 100 = 8 869 N ≈ 8.9 kN, or about 900 kg of force. For a spring–diaphragm actuator with about 1 bar of usable signal pressure, that needs roughly 0.089 m² of diaphragm — a little over 330 mm diameter — which is large but not unreasonable for this valve size; at higher ΔP it quickly becomes impractical. The alternative is a balanced (cage-guided) plug, which ports pressure through the plug so the unbalance force largely cancels, reducing the requirement to friction plus seat load ≈ 1 800 N — a fifth of the original, allowing a much smaller and cheaper actuator with better dynamic response. The trade-off is the balance seal: it is a wearing part, and as it degrades the leakage rises, so a valve specified Class IV may drift toward Class II over a few years in service. The decision therefore hinges on whether tight shut-off is genuinely required for this duty (Page 13) — if it is, keep the unbalanced plug and accept the large actuator, or consider a piston actuator with engineered fail-safe. Also verify the fail-safe direction against this 7 kN force, since it either assists or opposes closure depending on flow direction (WE4).
P7. A loop runs at ωgc = 1.2 rad/s with 60° phase margin. The valve has Td = 0.4 s and a time constant of 1.5 s. Is it acceptable?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Budget: Δθ = PM(rad)/ωgc = (60 × π/180)/1.2 = 1.0472/1.2 = 0.873 s of allowable extra pure delay. Valve dead time cost: 57.3 × ωgc × Td = 57.3 × 1.2 × 0.4 = 27.5°. Valve lag cost: atan(ωgc·τv) = atan(1.2 × 1.5) = atan(1.8) = 60.9°. Total ≈ 88° against 60° available → NOT acceptable; the loop would be unstable. Note which term dominates: the 1.5 s lag costs more than twice what the dead time does, because at this crossover frequency ωτ = 1.8 puts the valve's pole right in the middle of the loop's action. Options: (1) speed the valve up — a volume booster or higher-capacity positioner relay, or a smaller actuator, targeting τv ≤ 0.1/ωgc ≈ 0.08 s for a negligible cost (a demanding but not impossible spec); (2) slow the loop — retuning to ωgc ≈ 0.2 rad/s brings the lag cost to atan(0.3) = 16.7° and the delay cost to 4.6°, which fits comfortably, but the loop is then six times slower; (3) check whether the valve's sluggishness is a fault rather than a specification — 1.5 s is slow for a small valve and may indicate an undersized air supply, a restricted line, or a positioner needing tuning. The general rule this illustrates: a valve's lag is often more damaging than its dead time, and both must be assessed against the loop's actual crossover frequency, not against a generic "fast enough" judgement.
P8. A linear-trim valve has an installed gain of 210 (m³/h per unit travel) at 25% travel and 40 at 85% travel. Comment, and state what a control engineer should do.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The gain ratio is 210/40 = 5.25 : 1 across the working range — far beyond the ~2:1 that a single PID tuning can accommodate. The shape (high gain when nearly closed, low gain when nearly open) is the classic signature of a linear trim in a low-authority system, distorted toward quick-opening behaviour. Predicted symptoms: the loop oscillates at low flow and is sluggish and possibly unable to reach setpoint at high flow; successive engineers will have retuned it in opposite directions; operators may run it in manual at one end of the range. What to do, in order. (1) Confirm by computing the authority β = ΔPvalve/ΔPtotal at maximum flow — it will be low, probably below 0.25, and that number is what justifies everything else. (2) Change the trim to equal percentage if the valve has a replaceable cage: its gain rises with flow, cancelling the falling ΔP, and typically brings the ratio under 1.5:1. This is the correct fix. (3) If the trim cannot be changed today, apply a characterisation curve in the digital positioner to synthesise equal-percentage behaviour — a legitimate mitigation, provided it is documented, because otherwise the valve no longer matches its own datasheet. (4) Alternatively gain-schedule the controller on flow or valve position, roughly inversely to the measured installed gain. (5) Consider raising the authority by fitting a smaller valve or removing parasitic system losses, which fixes the root cause and improves resolution — weigh the pumping-energy cost, and consider whether a VFD is the better answer overall. What not to do: retune. No fixed set of constants serves a 5:1 variation; each retune only selects which end of the range to be wrong at.
P9. A butterfly valve on hot water (pv = 1.2 bar a) has p₁ = 6 bar a, p₂ = 0.9 bar a. Diagnose, and explain why anti-cavitation trim would be the wrong purchase.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Run the tests in the correct order. FF = 0.96 − 0.28√(1.2/221) = 0.96 − 0.28 × 0.07369 = 0.96 − 0.0206 = 0.939. Flashing test first: is p₂ ≤ FF·pv? FF·pv = 0.939 × 1.2 = 1.127 bar, and p₂ = 0.9 bar, so p₂ < 1.127 → THIS IS FLASHING, not cavitation. The fluid leaves the valve as a two-phase mixture and the vapour never collapses. Why anti-cavitation trim is the wrong purchase: anti-cavitation trim works by staging the pressure drop so that no single vena contracta falls below vapour pressure — which prevents bubbles forming and collapsing. But in flashing, the outlet pressure itself is below vapour pressure, so vapour will exist at the valve outlet no matter how the drop is staged. No trim geometry can raise p₂. Worse, anti-cavitation trim has small passages and high velocities that the expanding two-phase mixture will erode rapidly, and the enormous volumetric expansion (liquid to vapour) can choke the small passages entirely. You would be buying an expensive component that cannot work and will fail quickly. What to do instead — design FOR the flashing: (1) an expanded outlet body to accommodate the volume expansion and keep velocity down; (2) hardened, erosion-resistant trim and body materials (Stellite, hardened stainless); (3) a straight downstream run of adequately rated pipe, with no elbow immediately after the valve where the droplets would impinge; (4) locate the valve so the two-phase mixture discharges somewhere designed for it, such as a flash drum; (5) if flashing must be avoided, the only route is to raise the downstream pressure above pv — a process change, not a valve change. And note the body choice separately: a butterfly on this duty is poor regardless, given high recovery and limited erosion tolerance.

Derivation 1 · The Sizing Equation from Bernoulli
Goal: show that Q = C√(ΔP/SG) follows from energy conservation, and see what C hides. Step 1 — Bernoulli between the inlet (1) and the vena contracta (vc), neglecting elevation and friction: p₁ + ½ρv₁² = pvc + ½ρvvc² With Avc ≪ Apipe, v₁ ≪ vvc, so: vvc = √( 2(p₁ − pvc)/ρ ) Step 2 — volumetric flow through the contracted area. The actual jet area is smaller than the geometric opening by a contraction coefficient, and there are losses, both absorbed into a discharge coefficient Cd: Q = Cd·A·vvc = Cd·A·√( 2(p₁ − pvc)/ρ ) Step 3 — relate the internal drop to the MEASURABLE drop. We can measure only p₁ − p₂, and some pressure is recovered between the vena contracta and the outlet. Define the pressure recovery factor: FL² = (p₁ − p₂)/(p₁ − pvc) so p₁ − pvc = (p₁ − p₂)/FL² Substituting and gathering every geometric and empirical term into one coefficient: Q = [ Cd·A·√(2)/FL ] · √( (p₁ − p₂)/ρ ) = C · √( ΔP / ρrel ) ∎ Step 4 — read what C contains. C bundles the flow area at that travel, the discharge coefficient, the contraction, and the unit-system constant. It is therefore measured, not calculated (IEC 60534-2-3), and it is a function of travel — which is the characteristic of Page 7. Step 5 — and where FL comes back. Note that FL appeared naturally in Step 3 as the ratio linking the internal and measurable pressure drops. That is exactly why the SAME factor governs choking: choking is decided by pvc reaching pv, and FL is the only thing connecting pvc to quantities we can measure.

What it teaches. The square-root law is not an empirical curiosity — it is conservation of energy with everything unmeasurable swept into one coefficient. Two consequences follow immediately and are examinable. First, because Q ∝ √ΔP, the gain of a valve as a flow element falls as ΔP rises: dQ/dΔP = C/(2√(ΔP·SG)), which is the same square-root nonlinearity that afflicts orifice flow measurement (A56) and is why DP transmitters need square-root extraction. Second, the derivation shows FL is not a bolt-on correction: it is the bridge between the pressure we can measure at the flanges and the pressure that actually exists inside the valve, which is why it governs both the sizing equation and the cavitation limit.

Derivation 2 · Why Equal Percentage Has Gain Proportional to Flow
Goal: derive the equal-percentage law from its defining property, then show its gain. Step 1 — the defining property. "Equal increments of travel produce equal PERCENTAGE changes in capacity." Written as a differential equation: dC/dh = k · C (the rate of change is proportional to the value) Step 2 — solve it. Separating variables: dC/C = k·dh → ln C = k·h + const → C = C₀·ek h Step 3 — apply the boundary conditions. At h = 1 (full travel) C = Cmax; at h = 0 the trim passes Cmax/R, where R is the inherent rangeability: C(0) = C₀ = Cmax/R C(1) = (Cmax/R)·ek = Cmax → ek = R → k = ln R Therefore: C(h) = (Cmax/R)·eh·ln R = Cmax·R(h−1) φ = C/Cmax = R(h−1) ∎ Step 4 — the gain. Differentiate, or simply read it off Step 1: dφ/dh = φ · ln R ∎ The gain at any point is proportional to the capacity at that point — which, at constant ΔP, means proportional to the FLOW. That was the design requirement all along; the exponential is merely its solution. Step 5 — sanity check the numbers for R = 50 (ln R = 3.912): h = 0.0 → φ = 0.020, gain 0.078 h = 0.5 → φ = 0.141, gain 0.553 h = 1.0 → φ = 1.000, gain 3.912 Gain varies 50:1 across the stroke — exactly the rangeability, as it must.

What it teaches. The characteristic is the solution of a design specification, not an arbitrary curve a manufacturer chose. Starting from "I want the gain to be proportional to the flow" and solving the resulting differential equation produces the exponential inevitably — and the rangeability R appears as the constant of integration fixed by the boundary condition. This also explains the neat identity in Step 4 that gain ratio equals flow ratio, which is the fastest way to answer any equal-percentage gain question in an exam. Finally, note the honest limitation: the derivation assumes constant ΔP, so it describes the inherent characteristic. Whether the resulting installed behaviour is useful depends entirely on whether the system's falling ΔP happens to cancel this rising gain — the subject of Derivation 3.

Derivation 3 · The Installed Characteristic in Closed Form
Goal: derive Q(h) for a valve in a real system, and show why authority decides the shape. Step 1 — the two governing relations. The valve passes Q = C·√(ΔPv/SG) and the system leaves the valve whatever head remains after its own losses: ΔPv = ΔPt − K·Q² (ΔPt = total available head, K = system resistance) Step 2 — eliminate ΔPv. Substitute the second into the first and square: Q² · SG = C²·(ΔPt − K·Q²) Q²·SG + C²·K·Q² = C²·ΔPt Q²·(SG + C²K) = C²·ΔPt Q = C · √( ΔPt / (SG + C²·K) ) ∎ Step 3 — examine the two limits. High authority (C²K ≪ SG, i.e. the valve dominates the resistance): Q ≈ C·√(ΔPt/SG) → Q ∝ C The installed characteristic REPRODUCES the inherent one. A linear trim gives a linear installed curve. ✓ Low authority (C²K ≫ SG, i.e. the pipework dominates): Q ≈ C·√(ΔPt/(C²K)) = √(ΔPt/K) → Q ≈ constant, independent of C Opening the valve further changes nothing — the curve has SATURATED. This is the flat top of the low-β curves on Page 8. Step 4 — why equal percentage rescues the low-authority case. Near saturation, Q depends on C only weakly, so to keep dQ/dh useful we need dC/dh to GROW as C grows — which is precisely dC/dh = C·ln R, the equal-percentage law. The two effects multiply to something roughly constant. Step 5 — relate K to the measured authority. At design flow Qd: K = (ΔPt − ΔPv,d)/Qd² and β = ΔPv,d/ΔPt so K = ΔPt(1 − β)/Qd² — authority and system resistance are two descriptions of the same fact.

What it teaches. A single line of algebra produces the whole of Page 8. The closed form Q = C√(ΔPt/(SG + C²K)) is worth memorising because it is directly computable — it is what the interactive demo evaluates — and because its two limits explain both design rules at once. Step 3's low-authority limit is particularly instructive: Q → √(ΔPt/K) contains no C at all, which is the mathematical statement of "the pipe is controlling, not the valve". That is why no trim characteristic can rescue a valve with β below about 0.1: you cannot shape a gain that has gone to zero. Step 5 closes the loop by showing that authority β and system resistance K carry identical information, so a sizing sheet that quotes one implicitly quotes the other.

Derivation 4 · The Choked-Flow Limit
Goal: derive ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv) and explain each factor. Step 1 — the physical limit. Flow increases with ΔP only while the vena contracta pressure can keep falling. Once pvc reaches the vapour pressure, the liquid boils there; the vapour cavity fixes the throat pressure and further reduction of p₂ cannot lower pvc or raise the velocity. Flow is CHOKED. Limiting condition: pvc = pv (to a first approximation) Step 2 — express pvc in measurable terms using the definition of FL from Derivation 1: FL² = (p₁ − p₂)/(p₁ − pvc) → p₁ − pvc = ΔP/FL² Step 3 — impose the limiting condition. Setting pvc = pv at the choking point: p₁ − pv = ΔPchoked/FL² ΔPchoked = FL²·(p₁ − pv) Step 4 — the empirical correction FF. Experiment shows the throat pressure at choking is slightly BELOW pv, because boiling is not instantaneous — the liquid sustains a metastable superheat, and the effect is stronger as the fluid approaches its critical point. IEC 60534 fits this with: FF = 0.96 − 0.28·√(pv/pc) giving the final form used in practice: ΔPchoked = FL²·(p₁ − FF·pv) ∎ Check the limits: pv ≪ pc (cold liquid) → FF → 0.96 pv → pc (near critical) → FF → 0.68 Step 5 — the gas analogue. The same reasoning with a compressible fluid gives choking when the throat reaches sonic velocity, at x = FγxT — where xT plays exactly the role FL² plays for liquids, and Fγ = k/1.4 corrects for the gas's specific heat ratio.

What it teaches. The whole of Page 5 is one substitution: express the unmeasurable throat pressure in terms of the measurable flange pressures using FL, then set the throat to vapour pressure. Everything else is empirical polish. Two points examiners probe. First, FL squared — the factor enters as a square because it was defined on pressure differences, which is why a modest difference in FL (0.90 vs 0.65) produces a near-doubling of allowable drop. Second, the distinction between choking and cavitation damage: this derivation locates the point where flow stops increasing, but bubbles begin forming and collapsing at pressure drops well below ΔPchoked. Passing the choking test is therefore necessary but not sufficient — the σ index against the vendor's incipient-damage limit is the check that protects the trim.

Seven Exam & Field Traps
  • 1
    Confusing Cv and Kv. A factor of 1.156 is a real valve size. State the convention every time, and check which one a vendor's curve uses before reading a travel off it.
  • 2
    Sizing at a single design ΔP. ΔP falls as flow rises (and further still with a centrifugal pump), so the maximum-flow case needs the largest Cv. Size at three flows and check the travel — the travel check, not the Cv, is the deliverable.
  • 3
    Using the actual ΔP when the flow is choked. Cap ΔP at ΔPchoked = FL²(p₁ − FFpv) or the calculation credits the valve with pressure drop that does no work — and check choking before sizing, not after.
  • 4
    Treating flashing as cavitation. Test p₂ against FFpv FIRST. If p₂ is below it the fluid flashes, no trim can prevent it, and anti-cavitation trim is both useless and quickly destroyed. Design for it instead.
  • 5
    Choosing the characteristic by habit. "Always equal percentage" is wrong at high authority, where it wastes half the stroke. Compute β at maximum flow and let it decide — then verify installed gain varies less than about 2:1.
  • 6
    Over-specifying shut-off and emissions. Class VI plus tight packing means high seat load and high friction, so the valve seals perfectly and controls badly. Use a separate isolation valve, and specify measurable dynamic criteria (deadband ≤ 0.5%, T86) instead of the phrase "good control".
  • 7
    Retuning a mechanical fault. A stiction limit cycle has amplitude ≈ stiction band × process gain and a period set by the integral time; reducing gain makes it slower and larger. Loop in manual, travel test, positioner diagnostics — then a work order.

Eight-Step Hands-On Ladder
  • 1
    Read a real valve datasheet end to end. Find the rated Cv, FL, xT, characteristic, leakage class, ANSI class, packing type, actuator size and bench set. Every line means something you now know.
  • 2
    Size a valve by hand, then check it in vendor software. Take a real duty from your plant, do the three-point calculation on paper, then run it through Fisher Specification Manager or equivalent. Reconcile every difference — that reconciliation is where the learning is.
  • 3
    Compute the authority of five valves on your plant from their datasheets and the pump curves. Predict which loops will have gain-variation problems, then check the loop performance data against your prediction.
  • 4
    Do a valve travel test. With the loop in manual, step the output ±0.5%, ±1%, ±2% and record actual position from the positioner. Measure the deadband. Do it on a valve you suspect and one you trust, and compare.
  • 5
    Capture a valve signature with a smart positioner (travel versus actuator pressure) and interpret the friction, bench set and seat load from its shape. Store it as the baseline.
  • 6
    Run the cavitation arithmetic on every high-ΔP liquid valve you can find — FF, ΔPchoked, σ, and the flashing test. You will almost certainly find one that is quietly destroying itself.
  • 7
    Attend a valve strip-down at a shutdown and read the damage: pitting downstream of the throat, polished directional wear, or a groove across the seat. Each tells a different story (Page 12).
  • 8
    Write a valve specification for one real duty, complete with three-point sizing, authority and characteristic justification, cavitation and noise checks, fail-safe verification against maximum unbalance force, and measurable dynamic criteria. Have it reviewed by an instrument engineer.
Self-Assessment — Do You Own This Subject?

Sizing

Can you size a valve at three flows for liquid, gas and steam, cap ΔP at the choked value, and verify travel at each case?

Severe service

Can you distinguish cavitation from flashing from choking with the actual arithmetic, and choose the right remedy for each — including knowing when a valve-side fix is impossible?

Characteristics

Can you compute valve authority, choose the trim from it, and predict the installed gain variation before the valve is bought?

Diagnosis

Shown a cycling loop, can you distinguish stiction from tuning from oversizing, verify actuator thrust and fail-safe action, and convert valve dynamics into lost phase margin?

When all four are true you can walk up to any control valve, read its datasheet against its duty, and say whether it is the right valve — before it fails. That is what separates specifying a valve from ordering one.
