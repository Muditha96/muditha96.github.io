🛠️Building a CNC Machine

Anyone can buy the parts. The reason most home-built and small-shop CNC machines never hold their advertised tolerance is that a machine tool is a single elastic loop, not a collection of components — and that loop is only ever as stiff as its softest link. This module builds one from first principles: the structure that carries the cutting force, the components that turn rotation into straight-line motion, the theory that says which of your choices actually matters, and the control system that decides whether the machine can follow the path you paid for. It follows one Moratuwa workshop's 1200 × 800 mm router from a specified ±0.05 mm to a measured ±0.35 mm, and back again — and shows why the most expensive component on the bill of materials was not the problem.

Cold Open — The Machine That Missed by Seven Times

A Moratuwa job shop built its own router to take on aluminium work. Every component was correctly specified against its own datasheet. The machine missed its tolerance by a factor of seven.

THE BUILD CORE

Twelve-person shop, mostly acrylic and plywood, sub-contracting its aluminium fixture plates out at a price it disliked. So it built its own 1200 × 800 × 200 mm moving-gantry router. Welded steel base, RHS gantry, profile rails, ballscrews, NEMA 23 steppers, a 2.2 kW air-cooled router spindle and a step/dir controller. Build cost LKR 1,180,000 and about 300 hours of a good fitter's time.

SPECIFIED DELIVERED positioning ±0.05 mm ±0.35 mm (7 × out) rapids 15 m/min lost steps above 10 cut in 6061 3 mm deep, 6 mm cutter chatters above 1 mm aluminium hours 120 h/month 0 Every component met its own datasheet. Nothing was broken, worn or mis-assembled. The machine was built exactly as drawn.

This is the ordinary way machine builds fail, and it is worth being precise about why: a machine tool is not a collection of components, it is a single elastic loop. The cutting force leaves the tool, travels through the spindle, the Z slide, the Y carriage, its drive, the gantry, the X carriages, the base and the table, and arrives back at the workpiece. Every one of those is a spring, they are in series, and the loop is only ever as stiff as its softest member. Specify each part separately and you will get exactly what this shop got.

PREDICT FIRST — 1 of 9
The loop contains seven springs. The base is 40 N/µm, the X drive 30, the Y carriage 25, the Z column 12, the spindle 8, the gantry 6 — and the Y-axis belt drive is 3 N/µm. What is the stiffness of the whole loop?
D — 1.24 N/µm, which is softer than the softest component. Springs in series add their compliances, not their stiffnesses: 1/k = Σ1/kᵢ = 0.125+0.0833+0.04+0.333+0.167+0.0333+0.025 = 0.8067 µm/N, so k = 1.240 N/µm. At the 400 N this machine makes in a 3 mm cut, the tool moves 323 µm relative to the work.

C is the intuition that gets closest and is still wrong: the softest link dominates but does not define — everything else adds on top, and here the other six between them contribute 59 % of the compliance. B is the parallel formula, correct only for springs sharing the load side by side, which these are not. A has no physical basis at all, yet it is what a bill of materials implicitly assumes when it lists parts without a loop analysis.

Notice which component is the villain: not the cheap Chinese spindle, not the base — the toothed belt on the Y axis, at 41 % of the total compliance. It cost LKR 4,200. This is the module in one number.
FAULT 1 — THE BELT CORE

The Y axis was belt driven because it was cheap, quiet and easy to fit. A 25 mm HTD-5M steel-cord belt over a 1.2 m span gives about 3 N/µm at the carriage. The same axis on a 20 × 10 ballscrew gives 76 N/µm — twenty-five times stiffer.

Effect on the loop: 1.240 → 2.056 N/um deflection 323 → 195 um Effect on the axis: ωn = √(k/m), m = 45 kg belt 3.0 N/um → ωn = 258 rad/s = 41.1 Hz usable bandwidth 13.7 Hz screw 76.2 N/um → ωn = 1301 rad/s = 207 Hz usable bandwidth 69 Hz 25.4 × the stiffness buys only 5.04 × the frequency — the square root, which is the single most-forgotten fact in machine dynamics.

A 41 Hz axis chatters in aluminium because the tooth-passing frequency of a 3-flute cutter at 12,000 rpm is 600 Hz and its sub-harmonics walk straight through the machine's own resonance. It also caps the servo loop at about 14 Hz, which is where the following error of page 12 comes from.

FAULT 2 — THE SLOT IN THE GANTRY CORE

The gantry was 100 × 100 × 4 RHS — a good choice. Then a full-length slot was cut in the underside so the cable chain could run inside it. That slot converts a closed section into an open one, and for torsion the difference is not a percentage.

Closed box 100×100×4: J = 3,538,944 mm⁴ Same tube, slit: J = 6,400 mm⁴ 553 × less torsional stiffness for exactly the same steel and the same weight Bending is barely touched (I falls 11 %). Torsion collapses.

The gantry is loaded in torsion because the tool sits 250 mm below the gantry's shear centre, so the cutting force arrives as a couple. Combined with a single-side ballscrew — the drive pushes one end of the gantry while the friction and cutting load resist at the other — the beam both twists and yaws.

That distance is the Abbe arm, and on a gantry router it is unavoidably large.

FAULT 4 — THE X BALLSCREW WHIPPED CORE

A 16 × 5 ballscrew was fitted over a 1300 mm bearing span. A rotating shaft has a whirl speed, and it goes as the inverse square of the length.

It was chosen because it was in stock.

They ran it at 8 m/min anyway. The screw howled, the nut's return path wore, and the whip itself put a rotating positional error into the axis. A ballscrew has three independent limits — whirl, buckling and the DN number — and length attacks all three.

The Machine Is One Elastic Loop — and Only the Softest Link Matters

This is the organising idea of the whole module. Everything on pages 3 to 15 is an attempt to make one link of this loop stiffer, and the only way to know which link to attack is to compute all of them.

THE STRUCTURAL LOOP, DEFINED CORE

The structural loop is the closed path of material that carries the cutting force from the cutting edge back to the point on the workpiece where it is being applied. It always closes: force cannot leave the machine. On this router the loop is tool → spindle → Z slide → Y carriage → Y drive → gantry → X carriages → X drive → base → table → fixture → workpiece → back to the tool.

Each element deflects under the same force F, and the deflections ADD: δtotal = δ₁ + δ₂ + ... = F/k₁ + F/k₂ + ... 1/kloop = Σ 1/ki (compliances add — springs in SERIES) This is the same algebra as resistors in parallel, capacitors in series, thermal resistances in a wall, and the effective bulk modulus of A13. It is worth recognising the pattern once and for all: whenever several things share the same effort and their responses add, the reciprocals add and the weakest element dominates.

Two consequences follow immediately and both are counter-intuitive enough to be worth stating as rules.

The loop is softer than its softest part

Seven springs of 3–40 N/µm give 1.24, not 3. Everything piles on top of the weak link. A machine can never be as good as its worst component — only worse.

Improving anything but the weakest link is nearly wasted

Doubling the 40 N/µm base to 80 moves the loop from 1.240 to 1.259 — a 1.5 % gain for a rebuilt base. Doubling the 3 N/µm belt moves it to 1.55, a 25 % gain.

THE COMPLIANCE SHARE — the number to compute first, every time sharei = (1/ki) / (Σ 1/kj) As built: Y belt 41.3 % gantry 20.7 % spindle 15.5 % Z 10.3 % Y carriage 5.0 % X drive 4.1 % base 3.1 % The share IS the priority list. It also tells you the ceiling on any single fix: making the belt infinitely stiff can only ever recover 41.3 % of the compliance, so the loop cannot exceed 1.240/(1−0.413) = 2.11 N/µm from that change alone, no matter how much you spend on it.

WHERE THE NUMBERS COME FROM STRETCH

You will rarely be given a component stiffness. Four sources, in descending order of trustworthiness:

  1. 1
    Measure it. Clamp a dial indicator or a capacitive probe between the two ends of the link, apply a known force with a spring balance or a dead weight, read the deflection. k = F/δ. Ten minutes per link, and it is the only number nobody can argue with.
  2. 2
    Catalogue data. Guide blocks, ballscrew nuts, bearings and toolholders all publish stiffness — usually as a table against preload class. Read the direction: a guide block has different radial, lateral and moment stiffnesses.
  3. 3
    Hand calculation. Beams, columns and closed sections are textbook. This is page 4 and it is accurate to about 20 % if the joints are good — which is the catch.
  4. 4
    FEA. Correct in principle and routinely wrong in practice, because the answer is dominated by the joint stiffness you assumed, not the parts. A bolted joint is not a weld and a weld is not continuous material.
THE JOINT PROBLEM — why FEA over-predicts machine stiffness A bolted joint carries load through the real contact area of two rough surfaces, which is a few per cent of the apparent area. Typical values: ground and bolted, well preloaded 200 – 800 N/µm per joint machined and bolted, ordinary 50 – 200 N/µm as-sawn faces, bolted 10 – 60 N/µm welded, full penetration effectively continuous epoxy-bedded and bolted 300 – 1000 N/µm A model that meshes two parts as bonded will report a machine 2 – 5 × stiffer than the one you build. Every bolted interface in the loop is another spring in the series. This is the main reason fabricated machines disappoint and cast ones do not.

It is also why bolting count and preload matter more than plate thickness on a fabricated machine, and why the traditional remedies — scraping mating faces, epoxy bedding, and torquing to a real spec rather than "tight" — are worth more than the next 5 mm of wall.

PREDICT FIRST — 2 of 9
Your loop is 2.0 N/µm and you need 4.0. A supplier offers a spindle upgrade that triples the spindle link from 8 to 24 N/µm. The spindle is currently 25 % of the compliance. Does it get you there?
B — about 2.5 N/µm. Total compliance is 1/2.0 = 0.500 µm/N, of which the spindle is 25 % = 0.125 (consistent with k = 8). Tripling the spindle's stiffness cuts its compliance to 0.0417, so the new total is 0.500 − 0.125 + 0.0417 = 0.4167 → k = 2.40 N/µm. A 20 % gain for a spindle.

D states the ceiling correctly — deleting the spindle's compliance entirely gives 1/0.375 = 2.67 N/µm — and that is the number to compute before asking the price. It is also why D is nearly the right answer: it correctly says 4.0 is unreachable, and for the right reason. A is the seductive one: "triple a quarter" sounds like it should roughly double the whole, and it does nothing of the sort, because you can only ever recover the share you started with. C confuses series with parallel.

The general result worth memorising: the best possible outcome from perfecting one link is knew = kold/(1 − share). Compute that first. If it is below your target, the link is the wrong one to buy, whatever the salesman's numbers say.

Structure I — Material, Specific Stiffness and the Damping Nobody Budgets For

The material argument for machine tools is almost never about strength. It is about stiffness per unit mass, damping, thermal behaviour and what you can actually buy in Sri Lanka on a Tuesday.

STRENGTH IS IRRELEVANT HERE CORE

A machine-tool structure at 400 N of cutting load sees stresses of a few MPa against a yield of 275. It will never break. It is chosen entirely on deflection, damping and dimensional stability — three properties that appear on no mild-steel certificate. This is the first thing to unlearn from a strength-of-materials course.

For a structure whose job is to be stiff for its own mass, steel and aluminium are effectively tied, and cast iron is 43 % worse than either. The reason aluminium extrusion machines are floppy is not the material; it is the section and the joints (page 4), and the fact that the extrusion is chosen for convenience rather than for its second moment of area.

Which specific stiffness applies depends on what limits you: a MOVING member limited by its own weight (a gantry beam) → E/ρ a STATIC member, mass irrelevant (a base bolted to a floor) → E alone a member limited by BUCKLING or plate stability → √E/ρ or E⅓/ρ The third case is why aluminium wins in aerospace and loses in machine tools: machine structures are stiffness-limited in bending and torsion, not buckling-limited, so the exponent that flatters aluminium never appears.
DAMPING IS THE PROPERTY THAT ACTUALLY DIFFERS CORE

Steel and aluminium tie on stiffness and both are terrible at damping. Cast iron is 2.5 × better, polymer concrete is 15 × better. That single number decides whether a machine chatters.

At resonance, the dynamic compliance is the static compliance divided by twice the damping ratio: |G(ωn)| = 1/(2ζk) — the "dynamic stiffness" is 2ζk steel weldment ζ = 0.002, k = 6 N/µm → dynamic stiffness 0.024 N/µm cast iron ζ = 0.005, k = 6 → 0.060 N/µm polymer concrete ζ = 0.030, k = 6 → 0.360 N/µm Fifteen times the chatter resistance from the same static stiffness. The stability lobe diagram of A42 scales directly with 2ζk, so the limiting depth of cut scales with it too.

This is why a 1960s cast-iron mill out-cuts a modern fabricated router of twice the static stiffness, and why the standard cheap fix — filling a hollow steel structure with sand, gravel, or epoxy granite — works so well. It adds almost no static stiffness and multiplies the damping by five or more, because energy goes into friction between the particles.

Sand filling

Cheapest possible. ζ from 0.002 to ~0.010. Must be dry and packed; seal the ends. Adds mass, which lowers ωn — fine on a base, bad on a moving gantry.

Epoxy granite fill

ζ to 0.015–0.025 and adds real stiffness. Mix graded aggregate with 8–12 % epoxy by mass; vibrate to compact. The realistic high-end option for a self-build.

Constrained-layer damping

A viscoelastic sheet sandwiched between the structure and a bolted-on cover plate. Shear in the layer dissipates energy. Cheap, retrofittable, and effective on plate panels that ring.

💼

“Why does the old machine cut better than the new one?” is a question you will be asked. The answer is almost always damping, and almost never stiffness — and you can demonstrate it in ten minutes with an accelerometer and a hammer, which is the ring-down test of A42.

Three defences, in order of cost. Homogeneity — build the machine from one material so it grows uniformly and the errors partly cancel; mixing an aluminium gantry on a steel base guarantees a bimetallic strip the size of your machine. Symmetry — put the screw on the neutral axis and the motors outside the loop, so growth moves the structure without rotating it. Soak and measure — run the machine for 30–60 minutes before the first accurate cut, and if you must chase the last 20 µm, measure the structure's temperature and compensate in the controller (page 14).

Structure II — Sections, and the Slot That Costs 553× the Torsional Stiffness

Material chooses itself in ten minutes. Section geometry is where a machine is actually won or lost, and torsion is where almost every self-build goes wrong.

THE TWO SECTION PROPERTIES CORE
BENDING deflection ∝ 1/(E I) I = second moment of area [mm⁴] cantilever δ = FL³/(3EI) simply supported δ = FL³/(48EI) both ends fixed δ = FL³/(192EI) — note the 64× span between them TORSION twist ∝ 1/(G J) J = torsion constant [mm⁴] θ = TL/(GJ) G = E/(2(1+ν)) = 79.3 GPa for steel For a CLOSED thin-walled section (Bredt's formula): J = 4Am² / ∮(ds/t) Am = area enclosed by the MID-LINE For an OPEN thin-walled section: J = (1/3) Σ bi ti³ — note t CUBED, and no enclosed area at all

Those last two lines are the whole page. A closed section carries torque as a shear flow running round the perimeter, and its stiffness scales with the square of the enclosed area. An open section cannot form that circuit; it resists twist only by each wall bending locally, and its stiffness scales with wall thickness cubed — a much smaller number.

And a gantry beam is loaded overwhelmingly in torsion, because the tool hangs 250 mm below the beam's shear centre and the cutting force therefore arrives as a couple. This is the geometry of every gantry router ever built, and it is why the cable-chain slot on page 1 destroyed the machine.

HOW TO CLOSE A SECTION YOU HAVE TO OPEN CORE

You often genuinely need the access. Four ways to keep the shear circuit, in descending order of effectiveness:

  1. 1
    Put the opening in the neutral place. Run the cable chain outside the beam on a bracket, or in a separate small channel welded to the outside. The main tube stays intact. Free.
  2. 2
    Bolt a continuous cover plate with enough fasteners. A cover plate restores the circuit only if the shear flow can cross the joint. Fastener pitch should be under about 10 × the plate thickness; at 4 mm plate that is a bolt every 40 mm, which people never do. At 200 mm pitch you have a decorative lid.
  3. 3
    Fit internal diaphragms. Plates welded across the tube every 200–300 mm restore much of the torsional stiffness locally and stop the section distorting into a parallelogram. Cheap, and standard practice on bridge girders for exactly this reason.
  4. 4

Configurations — and the Abbe Arm That Decides Your Accuracy Before You Start

The kinematic arrangement is chosen in the first hour of a design and cannot be changed afterwards. It fixes the Abbe arms, the moving mass, and the shape of the error field for the machine's whole life.

ABBE'S PRINCIPLE CORE

Ernst Abbe, 1890: the measuring scale must lie on the same line as the dimension being measured. Any offset between the axis of measurement and the axis of interest turns an angular error into a linear one, multiplied by the offset.

ε = θ × LAbbe θ = the angular error of the carriage (pitch, yaw or roll), radians LAbbe = distance from the guide/scale axis to the tool tip A very good profile-rail carriage holds about 20–50 µrad of pitch over its travel. At an Abbe arm of 400 mm that is: 30 µrad × 0.400 m = 12 µm — from a perfect drive and a perfect scale The angular error is the SAME whether your encoder reads to 1 µm or 1 nm. Resolution cannot see it and compensation cannot remove it, because it depends on where the tool is, not on where the axis is.

This is why a micrometer (anvil in line with the screw — Abbe-compliant) is more accurate than a vernier caliper (jaws offset from the scale — Abbe-violating) made to the same standard. And it is why a gantry router, whose tool hangs several hundred millimetres from every one of its guideways, has a hard accuracy ceiling that no amount of electronics will lift.

Pitch

Rotation about the axis across the travel. Multiplied by the vertical Abbe arm — usually the biggest one on a router.

Yaw

Rotation about the vertical. Multiplied by the horizontal offset. This is what a single-side gantry drive creates directly.

Roll

Rotation about the direction of travel. Multiplied by the lateral offset; usually smallest, and usually forgotten.

Where the doubling comes from is almost always a moment, not a force. MID-BAND RESONANCE — the other stepper failure, and it is worse A stepper is a spring: the rotor is held magnetically to the commanded position with a torsional stiffness, and with the reflected load inertia it forms a lightly damped oscillator, usually 100–250 Hz. Drive the step rate through that frequency and the rotor oscillates about its commanded position. If it swings more than 2 full steps it slips a pole and the machine loses position — at a speed WELL BELOW the torque-speed limit, and often at only one particular feedrate. Symptom: the machine loses steps at 1,800 mm/min but is fine at 1,200 and fine at 2,400. Cures: microstepping (the classic reason for it), mechanical damping on the shaft, avoiding the band, or closing the loop.

It does not buy positioning accuracy, and it never buys torque. The accuracy of the axis is set by the screw, the guide and the loop — page 16.

It also costs you speed on a step/dir controller, because the pulse rate is the product of microsteps and revolutions: at a 100 kHz step ceiling, 1/8 microstepping on a 10 mm lead gives 18.8 m/min and 1/32 gives 4.7 m/min. Page 13 returns to this.

The electrical cabinet is where most self-built machines become unreliable rather than inaccurate — and it is the one part of the build with a legal dimension.

The single most common failure in a home-built controller is a shared ground path: motor current returning through the same conductor as the step/direction reference, so every acceleration injects a voltage into the signal. The machine then loses steps under load, in a way that looks exactly like a torque problem and is not.

Star ground

One point where logic 0 V, drive 0 V and PE meet — normally at the PE bar. Never daisy-chain returns between drives.

Opto-isolation

Step, direction and enable through optocouplers or differential line drivers. Breaks the ground loop between controller and drives entirely.

Shielded motor cable

Screen bonded 360° at both ends for VFD and servo cables — this is the exception to the one-end rule, because the noise is capacitively coupled and high frequency.

WHY A VFD IS THE NOISIEST THING YOU WILL OWN An IGBT switches 540 V in about 50–100 ns. That is a dV/dt of roughly 5–10 kV/µs, and every metre of unscreened motor cable radiates it. • use screened 4-core motor cable (3 phases + PE inside the screen) • bond the screen with a 360° gland, not a pigtail — a 50 mm pigtail is an inductor and undoes most of the benefit • keep motor cable at least 200 mm from signal cable; cross at 90° • fit the recommended line filter AND keep it within 300 mm of the drive • earth the spindle body separately back to the PE bar Symptom of getting this wrong: the controller resets, the encoder count jumps, or the touch probe triggers, only when the spindle is running.

THE SAFETY CHAIN — AND THE STANDARD THAT GOVERNS IT CORE

A CNC machine is a machine under the Machinery Directive and its equivalents. The relevant type-C standard is ISO 16090-1:2022, Machine tools safety — Machining centres, milling machines, transfer machines, which superseded EN 12417 and consolidated EN 13128 and EN 14070 into one document.

What ISO 16090-1 actually requires of a build like this: • a guard that contains a broken tool at the maximum spindle speed (thickness is specified by material and energy — polycarbonate degrades with coolant and age and must be replaced on a schedule) • interlocked movable guards, with guard locking where run-down time exceeds access time • an E-stop to ISO 13850, category 0 or 1, that is HARDWIRED and does not rely on the controller's software • a safe stop for the spindle with a defined run-down time • protection against gravity-loaded axis fall — the Z brake • a risk assessment to ISO 12100 and a performance level determined per ISO 13849-1 (see A52) The E-stop must remove power from the motors through a contactor or a certified STO input — not by telling the software to stop. Software that is executing a move is exactly the software you cannot trust in an emergency.

The Z-axis brake

A ballscrew backdrives. Disable a vertical servo and the head falls. A spring-applied, electrically released brake is the standard answer: power fails, brake engages. It must be wired so that E-stop applies it.

STO — safe torque off

A hardware input on the drive that removes gate drive independently of the control loop, certified to a performance level. Modern servo drives have it; using it correctly is far better than switching motor power with a contactor.

💼

If you build a machine that anyone other than you will ever operate, the risk assessment and the E-stop architecture are not optional paperwork — they are the difference between an accident and a prosecution. Doing a proper ISO 12100 assessment on your own build is also the single best way to learn the method, because you cannot hide behind someone else's design decisions.

Control Architecture — What Actually Happens Between G-Code and a Step Pulse

Five layers, each with a different time constant, and the one that limits your machine is almost never the one you were told to buy.

THE FIVE LAYERS CORE
INTERPRETER parse G-code, modal state, offsets, cycles ↓ ~ms, not real-time LOOK-AHEAD / PLANNER blend corners, apply accel and jerk limits, ↓ decide the feed for every segment ~1–20 ms INTERPOLATOR split the path into position setpoints, ↓ one per cycle 0.5–10 ms, HARD real-time POSITION / VELOCITY LOOP PID + feedforward per axis 0.1–1 ms ↓ CURRENT LOOP inside the drive 10–100 µs Each layer runs faster than the one above and must NEVER miss its deadline. Hard real-time means a late answer is a wrong answer — an interpolator that is 2 ms late has commanded the axis to be somewhere it is not.

This is why "run it on a Raspberry Pi with Linux" is a question about the bottom three layers and not the top two. Parsing G-code late is invisible; producing a position setpoint late is a jerk in the motion. Every serious architecture answers this by putting the hard real-time work somewhere protected — a real-time kernel, a dedicated microcontroller, or an FPGA.

THE TRAJECTORY PLANNER CORE

Between the G-code and the interpolator sits the piece of software that decides how fast you may actually go. It is doing four things at once.

  1. 1
    Velocity profiling. Trapezoidal (constant accel) is simple and produces an acceleration step at every transition — which excites every resonance on page 8. S-curve / jerk-limited ramps the acceleration itself, at the cost of slightly longer moves, and is worth it on any machine with a low fn.
  2. 2
    Look-ahead. The planner must know about the corner before it arrives, or it will enter it too fast and have to stop. Depth matters: a planner looking 20 blocks ahead on a path of 0.05 mm segments can see 1 mm into the future, which at 3 m/min is 20 ms — not enough to decelerate.
  3. 3
    Corner blending. A sharp corner at constant feed needs infinite acceleration, so the planner either stops, or rounds the corner within a tolerance you specify (G64 P… in LinuxCNC, the "exact stop / continuous" modes elsewhere). That tolerance is a real geometric error you are choosing to accept.
  4. 4
    Constraint arbitration. Each axis has its own max velocity, acceleration and jerk. On a diagonal move, the planner must scale the whole vector so that no axis exceeds its own limit — which is why a 45° move is not √2 times faster than an axis move.

The Position Loop — Kv, Following Error and the Circle That Shrinks

Every axis lags its command. How much it lags is one number, that number is capped by the machine's natural frequency, and it shows up on the part as a shrunken circle.

Kv — THE ONE NUMBER CORE
A position loop with proportional gain behaves, to first order, as velocity = Kv × (position error) so at a steady feed the axis settles at a constant lag: following error e = v / Kv Two units are in daily use and confusing them is a factor of 16.7: Kv [s⁻¹] — SI, used in control texts Kv factor — m/min of feed per mm of error, used by machine builders Kv[s⁻¹] = Kv factor × 1000/60 factor 1 = 16.7 s⁻¹ → at 3 m/min the axis lags 3.00 mm factor 3 = 50.0 s⁻¹ → at 3 m/min 1.00 mm factor 8 = 133 s⁻¹ → at 3 m/min 0.375 mm A millimetre of lag on a machine holding 50 µm sounds impossible, and it is fine — provided every axis lags by the SAME proportion, because then the tool is simply behind along the path, not off it. The moment the axes differ, the lag becomes a contour error.

That is the key insight of this page and it is worth stating on its own: following error along the path costs you nothing; following error mismatched between axes costs you the part. Matching Kv across axes is therefore more important than making it large.

THE CEILING ON Kv CORE
You cannot raise Kv freely: the axis is a lightly damped second-order system at ωn (page 8), and Kv < 2 ζ ωn with ζ = 0.05–0.15 for a machine axis belt axis ωn = 258 rad/s → Kv < 51.6 s⁻¹ = factor 3.1 20×10 screw ωn = 1301 rad/s → Kv < 260 s⁻¹ = factor 15.6 and in practice you use about half of the limit for margin. The mechanical design has already set your control performance. No amount of tuning will give the belt axis the gain the ballscrew axis can carry, because the limit is √(k/m), not the software.

This is the same relationship as the hydraulic axis of A13 and the servo axis of A09, and it is worth recognising as a general law of motion control: bandwidth is bought with stiffness and paid for with mass, and the controller only gets to spend what the mechanics earned.

FEEDFORWARD — the free fix Feedback must SEE an error before it can correct it. Feedforward does not wait: it injects the velocity the axis is known to need. output = Kv·e + Kff·vcmd + Ka·acmd With Kff = 1.0 the steady-state following error goes to zero without touching the loop gain — and therefore without touching stability. What is left is the error during ACCELERATION, which the acceleration feedforward term Ka attacks in the same way. Velocity feedforward is the highest-value ten minutes in commissioning. It is also the reason a modern machine can run a Kv factor of 3 and still cut a good circle at 6 m/min.
THE CIRCLE THAT SHRINKS CORE
Command a circle of radius R at feed F. Each axis is commanded a sinusoid at ω = F/R. A first-order lag of bandwidth Kv attenuates it: amplitude = R / √(1 + (ω/Kv)²) ≈ R(1 − ½(ω/Kv)²) Δr = F² / (2 R Kv²) — the radius comes out UNDERSIZE F = 3 m/min, R = 50 mm: factor 1 → 90 µm factor 3 → 10 µm factor 8 → 1.4 µm F = 3 m/min, R = 10 mm: factor 1 → 450 µm factor 3 → 50 µm factor 8 → 7 µm F = 6 m/min, R = 50 mm: factor 1 → 360 µm factor 3 → 40 µm factor 8 → 5.6 µm Note F² and 1/R. Doubling the feed quadruples the error; halving the radius doubles it. Small circles at high feed are where machines are exposed — which is exactly what a bolt-hole pattern or a corner radius is.

And if X and Y have different Kv, they shrink by different amounts and the circle becomes an ellipse tilted at 45°. That is the single most recognisable pattern in a ballbar plot, and page 17 shows how to read it. Other classic signatures: backlash gives step discontinuities at the axis reversals (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°); stick-slip gives spikes at reversal; squareness error gives an ellipse tilted at 45° that does not change with feed — which is how you tell it apart from mismatched gain.

Errors that grow with F² are servo-loop errors; errors that are unchanged are geometric; errors that appear only at reversals are mechanical. Three plots, ten minutes, and you have partitioned the entire error budget.

Resolution is what your display shows. Accuracy is where the tool is. They are different numbers, they are set by different components, and confusing them is the most expensive misunderstanding in machine building.

WHERE TO PUT THE ENCODER CORE

Rotary, on the motor

Semi-closed loop. Measures the motor, not the table. Blind to backlash, screw error, thermal growth and deflection. Cheap, robust, and what 90 % of machines use.

Linear scale, on the axis

Fully closed loop. Measures the actual carriage. Sees screw error, backlash and thermal growth — and removes them. Costs 3–10 × the rotary and needs care against swarf and coolant.

Both — dual loop

Motor encoder closes velocity (fast, stable), scale closes position (accurate). The right answer, and it needs a controller that supports it — check before buying, per page 11.

THE LIMIT OF A LINEAR SCALE A scale measures where the CARRIAGE is. It cannot see: • deflection between the carriage and the tool tip (the loop, page 2) • angular error × Abbe arm (page 5) • tool deflection and spindle runout (page 15) • thermal growth of the structure between scale and tool On the page-1 machine after rebuild, fitting scales takes the non-deflection error terms from 34 µm to 21 µm — and the total from 119 µm to 116 µm, because deflection is 97 % of what is left. Fit scales when your budget is dominated by screw error and thermal growth. Do not fit them to fix a soft machine.
THE THREE COMPENSATIONS CORE
  1. 1
    Backlash compensation. The controller adds the lost motion on a reversal. It works only if the backlash is constant and repeatable — and it is not, because it varies with position, load and temperature. Backlash compensation is a way of living with a fault, not fixing one. Preload the nut instead. Symptom that it is being over-used: a step in the ballbar plot at each reversal that changes size with feedrate.
  2. 2
    Screw / pitch-error mapping. Measure the axis against a laser interferometer at, say, 25 mm intervals, store the error at each point, and interpolate. This genuinely removes systematic lead error — a C7 screw with a good map behaves like a C3 screw over the mapped range. The highest-value compensation there is, and it costs one afternoon with borrowed equipment.
  3. 3
    Thermal compensation. Put a temperature sensor on the screw and on the structure, and apply a scaling term. Industrial controls do this as standard; LinuxCNC and Centroid can. It is the only way to attack the 70 µm of page 3 without air conditioning — but it needs a model, and building that model means measuring the machine over a full warm-up. Expect a day.
WHAT COMPENSATION CAN AND CANNOT TOUCH can systematic, repeatable, position-dependent errors — screw lead error, squareness, straightness, thermal drift cannot anything that depends on the CUTTING LOAD — deflection, racking, tool pull-out, chatter because the controller does not know the force, and the error changes with every depth of cut and every tool The dividing line is whether the error is a function of position alone. If it is, map it. If it depends on force, you must fix the mechanics.

The Accuracy Budget — and Why a Tolerance Without a Force Is a Wish

Everything in this module arrives here. An error budget is the one document that tells you what the machine will actually do, which change is worth making, and when to stop spending.

THE THREE FAMILIES OF ERROR CORE

Geometric

Straightness, squareness, lead error, angular errors. Function of position only → measurable, repeatable, compensable.

Thermal

Growth of structure and screws. Function of position and time → compensable only with a model and sensors.

Load-induced

Deflection, racking, tool pull-out. Function of the cutting force → not compensable. Must be engineered out.

That third column is the one that matters and the one that gets left out, because it is the only one you cannot measure by moving the machine around with no tool in it. A machine measured with a laser at zero cutting force will pass a specification it cannot meet while cutting. This is not a trick: it is exactly what ISO 230-2 measures, and exactly why ISO 230-2 is not a machining tolerance.

PREDICT FIRST — 9 of 9
Your machine's budget is: deflection 114 µm, screw error 23, thermal 14, straightness 15, squareness 10, backlash 8, resolution 6. You have money for exactly one improvement. Which halves the total RSS?
C — nothing but the deflection matters. The RSS is √(114²+23²+14²+15²+10²+8²+6²) = 118.9 µm, and 114² is 92 % of the sum of squares. Delete every other term entirely — perfect screws, no thermal effect, perfect geometry, infinite resolution — and you get 114 µm. A 4 % improvement for an unlimited budget.

A takes it to 116.5, B to 118.1, D to 117.6. All three are real improvements to real errors, and all three are invisible. This is the whole reason to build the budget before spending: in a squared sum, anything smaller than about a third of the largest term contributes essentially nothing.

The rule that falls out and is worth carrying: work only on terms within a factor of ~3 of the largest one; ignore the rest until the ranking changes. It is the same logic as the compliance share of page 2, applied to errors instead of springs — and it is why experienced engineers seem to ignore most of the problems on a list. They have squared them.

Commissioning, ISO 230 — and the Machine Rebuilt

A machine is not finished when it moves. It is finished when you have measured it, written the numbers down, and can prove them again in a year.

THE COMMISSIONING SEQUENCE CORE
  1. 1
    Level and settle. Level the base with a precision level (0.02 mm/m), leave it a week, level again. A welded frame relieves stress for months; a machine squared on day one will not be square on day thirty.
  2. 2
    Rail alignment. Set the master rail straight with a taut wire, a straightedge or an autocollimator; set the second rail parallel to it with a dial indicator on the carriage. Parallelism error becomes preload (page 6) — feel for a carriage that gets tighter along the travel.
  3. 3
    Squareness. Cylindrical square or a large machinist's square and an indicator, or cut a large test square and measure the diagonals — the difference in diagonals over length L gives the squareness error directly. Shim or scrape until it is under 0.02 mm/m.
  4. 4
    Screw alignment. The screw must be parallel to the rails in both planes and the nut mount must not force it. Slacken the nut bracket, run the axis end to end, then tighten — if the motor current changes with position, the screw is fighting the rails.
  5. 5
    Tram the spindle. Indicate a large circle on the table. Untrammed spindles leave scalloped floors and are the most common cause of “my surface finish is bad”.
  6. 6
    Tune the loops. Velocity loop first, then position, then feedforward (page 12). Match Kv across axes before maximising any of them.
  7. 7
    Measure, and write it down. Everything below. A machine without a commissioning record cannot be diagnosed later, because there is nothing to compare with.

Kv = 33.3 s⁻¹, F = 33.3 mm/s. Δr = 33.3²/(2×8×33.3²)×1000 = 62.5 µm. At factor 6 (100 s⁻¹): 6.9 µm. Trap: tripling Kv divided the error by nine, because Kv is squared. This is the one place in the module where a gain improvement pays back better than linearly — and it is capped by 2ζωn, which is mechanical.
P9. A 3D path has 0.03 mm segments and you want 2.5 m/min. What block rate is needed? What does a 300 blocks/s controller actually give, and what segment length would fix it?tap to reveal
Required = (2500/60)/0.03 = 1,389 blocks/s. A 300 blocks/s controller gives 300×0.03 = 9 mm/s = 0.54 m/min. To reach 2.5 m/min at 300 blocks/s you need segments of 0.139 mm. Trap: the fix is a CAM setting, costs nothing, and takes thirty seconds — but only if you have diagnosed it. Otherwise you buy servos.
P10. A 200 kHz step generator, 5 mm lead, 200-step motor. Tabulate rapid speed and torque-per-microstep at 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32.tap to reveal
1/8 (1,600 cnt): 37.5 m/min, 19.5 % torque. 1/16 (3,200): 18.8 m/min, 9.8 %. 1/32 (6,400): 9.4 m/min, 4.9 %. Trap: both columns get worse together. Microstepping costs speed and incremental torque, and buys only smoothness. Choose the coarsest setting that stays out of the resonance band.
P11. A budget reads 68, 30, 22, 18, 12, 9 µm. Give worst case and RSS. What does halving the 68 achieve? What does deleting everything except the 68 achieve?tap to reveal
Worst case 159 µm, RSS 81.0 µm. The 68 is 70.5 % of the sum of squares. Halving it to 34 gives RSS 55.6 µm (−31 %). Deleting all five others gives 68.0 µm (−16 %). Trap: halving the biggest term beats perfecting everything else combined, and by a factor of two. This is the single most useful habit in error budgeting: square the list, then look only at the top.
P12. A carriage has 40 µrad of pitch error. The tool is 500 mm from the guideway. What is the Abbe error, and what Abbe arm would hold it to 10 µm?tap to reveal
ε = 40×10⁻⁶ × 500 = 20 µm. To hold 10 µm: L ≤ 10/40×10⁻⁶ mm = 250 mm. Trap: the only variables are the carriage's angular error and the geometry. No encoder, no scale and no compensation table can touch this, because the machine genuinely does not know the tool has rotated. It is fixed in the layout sketch or it is not fixed at all.

Derivations, Traps, Glossary and the MSc Practice Pack

Five results this module owns, eleven ways to get them wrong, thirty terms, and seven exercises worth about twenty-two hours on real hardware.

DERIVATION 1 — THE LOOP, AND THE CEILING ON ANY FIX CORE
Each link sees the SAME force F and their deflections ADD: δ = Σ δi = Σ F/ki = F Σ 1/ki ⇒ 1/kloop = Σ 1/ki Define the compliance share of link j: sj = (1/kj) / (Σ 1/ki) = kloop/kj Now improve link j from kj to kj' . The new loop is 1/k' = Σ1/ki − 1/kj + 1/kj' = (1/k)(1 − sj) + 1/kj' k' = 1 / [ (1 − sj)/k + 1/kj' ] and letting kj' → ∞ gives the ceiling: kmax = k / (1 − sj) This is the equation to compute before asking any price. A link at 30 % of the compliance can never give you more than 1.43× the loop, no matter what you spend, and a link at 5 % can never give more than 1.05×. The same algebra governs the effective bulk modulus of A13, thermal resistances in a wall, and Amdahl's law in computing. It is worth recognising as one idea with many costumes.
DERIVATION 2 — WHY A SLIT DESTROYS TORSION STRETCH
Closed section. Torque is carried by a shear flow q = τt constant round the perimeter (equilibrium of a thin wall with no end load). The torque about any interior point is T = ∮ q · r ds = q ∮ r ds = q · 2Am (Bredt's first relation) Strain energy per unit length U = ∮ (q²/2Gt) ds, and equating U = T²/(2GJ) gives J = 4Am² / ∮(ds/t) — note Am is SQUARED Open section. There is no closed circuit, so no shear flow can run round it. Each wall behaves as a thin rectangle twisting on its own, for which St Venant gives Ji = ⅓biti³, and they simply add: J = ⅓ Σ bi ti³ — note t is CUBED and there is no area at all The ratio. For a square tube of side a and wall t, with a ≫ t: Jclosed = 4(a−t)⁴/(4(a−t)/t) = (a−t)³t ≈ a³t Jopen = ⅓ · 4a · t³ = 4at³/3 Jclosed/Jopen ≈ 0.75 (a/t)² (valid for a SQUARE tube; a rectangular one needs the full formulae) For a = 100, t = 4: 0.75 × 25² = 469 against 553 exact For a = 200, t = 6: 0.75 × 33² = 833 — thinner walls, worse penalty The penalty scales with the SQUARE of the slenderness ratio a/t. Thin-walled sections — exactly the ones you want for stiffness per kilogram — are the ones a slot ruins most.
DERIVATION 3 — BALLSCREW WHIRL STRETCH
A rotating shaft is an Euler-Bernoulli beam. Free transverse vibration: EI ∂⁴y/∂x⁴ + ρA ∂²y/∂t² = 0 Separating variables with y = Y(x)eiωt gives Y'''' = β⁴Y with β⁴ = ρAω²/(EI). The boundary conditions quantise βL to a value λ: λ = π simple – simple λ = 3.927 fixed – simple λ = 4.730 fixed – fixed ωcr = (λ/L)² √(EI/ρA) For a solid round shaft, I = πd⁴/64 and A = πd²/4, so √(I/A) = d/4 ⇒ ωcr = (λ/L)² · √(E/ρ) · d/4 Whirl occurs when the shaft's rotation frequency equals this bending natural frequency — the shaft's own residual unbalance drives it at exactly its resonance, so the deflection grows without bound. Three readings: • ncr ∝ d/L² — length is punished quadratically, diameter helps linearly • √(E/ρ) is the same for all steels: alloying the screw changes nothing • the LEAD does not appear — whirl is a property of the shaft alone, which is why doubling the lead doubles the rapid for free (page 7)
DERIVATION 4 — THE CIRCLE THAT SHRINKS STRETCH
A proportional position loop is, to first order, a first-order lag of bandwidth Kv: G(s) = Kv/(s + Kv) Command a circle of radius R at feed F. Each axis receives a sinusoid of angular frequency ω = F/R. The magnitude response is |G(jω)| = 1/√(1 + (ω/Kv)²) so the achieved radius is R|G|, and for ω ≪ Kv the binomial expansion gives R|G| ≈ R(1 − ½(ω/Kv)²) Δr = R · ½ (ω/Kv)² = ½ R (F/R)²/Kv² = F²/(2 R Kv²) Four consequences, all testable in ten minutes with a ballbar: • the radius is always UNDERSIZE, never over • ∝ F² — doubling the feed quadruples the error • ∝ 1/R — small circles are worse, so corner radii are the exposure • ∝ 1/Kv² — tripling the gain divides the error by nine And if Kv,X ≠ Kv,Y the two axes shrink differently and the circle becomes an ellipse at 45°. Since a squareness error produces the same shape, distinguish them by running two feedrates: the servo ellipse grows as F², the geometric one does not move.
DERIVATION 5 — WHY REFLECTED INERTIA GOES AS lead² CORE
The screw converts θ to x with x = (lead/2π)θ. Write κ = lead/2π. Kinetic energy of the moving mass: T = ½ m v² = ½ m (κθ̇)² = ½(mκ²)θ̇² Comparing with T = ½Jθ̇² : Jreflected = m κ² = m (lead/2π)² Meanwhile the force-to-torque relation is linear in the lead: T = F κ / η = F (lead/2π)/η THE CENTRAL TRADE OF DRIVE DESIGN: doubling the lead → doubles the speed for a given rpm ✓ → doubles the torque demand ✗ → QUADRUPLES the reflected inertia ✗✗ and a reduction ratio N does the reverse: torque demand ÷ N, reflected inertia ÷ N², motor speed × N This asymmetry — linear one way, squared the other — is why gear reduction so often solves two problems at once, and why coarse leads so often create two.
ELEVEN TRAPS CORE
  1. 1
    Adding stiffnesses instead of compliances. Springs in a loop are in series. Seven links of 3–40 N/µm give 1.24, not 124 and not 17.
  2. 2
    Forgetting the drive in the loop. The Y belt was 41 % of the compliance and appears on no structural drawing. Anything that transmits the cutting force is a link.
  3. 3
    Losing the square root from k to ωn. A 25 × stiffness gain is a 5 × frequency gain. This is the single most common slip in machine dynamics.
  4. 4
    Believing an alloy steel is stiffer. All steels have E ≈ 210 GPa. Alloying buys strength and hardness, never stiffness, and machine structures are never strength-limited.
  5. 5
    Cutting a slot in a torsion member. Two to three orders of magnitude, for one saw cut, and it barely touches the bending stiffness you were probably checking.
  6. 6
    Sizing a screw on load rating. The binding limits are whirl, buckling and DN — and whirl goes as d/L², so length is the variable that matters most and is the one fixed by the machine layout.
  7. 7
    Quoting stepper holding torque. Holding torque is the torque at zero speed. What matters is the torque at your rapid, which back-EMF may have reduced to zero.
  8. 8
    Confusing resolution with accuracy. A 1 µm readout on a C7 screw is a precise display of a 52 µm error. And microstepping buys neither accuracy nor torque.
  9. 9
    Compensating a load-dependent error. Compensation tables are functions of position. Deflection is a function of force. Nothing in the controller knows the force.
  10. 10
    Blaming the servos for a block-rate limit. If the machine ignores the programmed F on 3D paths but obeys it on 2D, the controller is starving on short segments — a CAM setting, not a hardware fault.
  11. 11
    Quoting a tolerance with no cutting force. ISO 230-2 is measured at zero load. It is a real, useful, and completely different number from what the machine holds while cutting.

THE MSc PRACTICE PACK — SEVEN EXERCISES, ~22 HOURS STRETCH

Every one is done on real hardware and produces an artefact you can show someone. Together they are a portfolio.

  1. 1
    Measure a real loop. (4 h) Take any machine you can reach — a router, a mill, a 3D printer, a press. Clamp a dial indicator between the tool and the table and push with a spring balance at three forces. k = F/δ. Then break the loop down: indicator between spindle and Z, between Z and gantry, and so on. Deliverable: a compliance table with shares, a photograph of each measurement setup, and a ranked list of what to fix.
  2. 2
    Find the natural frequency. (3 h) Stick an accelerometer (a phone will do for a first pass) on the spindle nose and tap the tool with a soft hammer. FFT the ring-down. Compare with √(k/m) from exercise 1. Repeat at three positions along each axis. Deliverable: the spectra, the predicted-versus-measured table, and an explanation of any factor-of-two discrepancy — there will be one, and it is usually a joint.
  3. 3
    Section study. (2 h) Take the actual gantry or column of a machine you know. Compute I and J from measurements. Then compute what it would be with a slot, with 50 mm more depth, and in aluminium at equal mass. Deliverable: a one-page comparison with a recommendation and its cost.
  4. 4
    Drive audit. (3 h) For every axis: measure the screw length and diameter, look up the lead, and compute the whirl, buckling and DN limits with Tool 4. Then measure the actual maximum rapid the machine achieves. Deliverable: a table of computed versus achieved, and an explanation of every gap — motor, controller or screw.
  5. 5
    Torque and inertia. (3 h) Build the torque budget for one axis with Tool 5. Then verify it: command a hard acceleration and log the drive current (most servo drives will show it; a clamp meter on a stepper is a rough substitute). Deliverable: the budget, the measured peak, and the inertia ratio with a verdict.
  6. 6
    Ballbar or its poor cousin. (4 h) If you can borrow a ballbar, run ISO 230-4 at two feedrates and interpret the plot against the five signatures on page 17. If you cannot, cut a 100 mm test circle and a 100 mm square at two feedrates and measure them on a CMM or with a height gauge and pins. Deliverable: the plots or measurements, and a partition of the error into servo, geometric and reversal terms.
  7. 7
    Write the specification. (3 h) Using exercises 1–6, write the honest datasheet for that machine: positioning accuracy to ISO 230-2, plus a tolerance-versus-cutting-force table, plus the feedrate ceilings and which one binds, plus the limitations you would tell a customer. Two pages. Deliverable: the datasheet. Nobody practises this and it is the document that distinguishes an engineer from an enthusiast.
