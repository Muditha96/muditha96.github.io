What the Solver Actually Does

The colour plot is the last step of six. Understanding the other five is what separates a pass from a lucky guess.

THE PIPELINE
This single fact — displacement first, stress second — explains mesh convergence, singularities, and why nodal and element stresses differ.
The governing equation [K] {u} = {F} [K] global stiffness matrix — from geometry, material and element type {u} nodal displacement vector — the UNKNOWNS, one per degree of freedom {F} nodal force vector — from the applied loads What each step of the pipeline does to it meshing decides the SIZE of [K] — one row/column per DOF material scales [K] through E and ν fixtures REMOVE rows and columns (a fixed DOF is known, not solved) loads populate {F} solving inverts (factorises) [K] — the expensive step recovery ε = ∂u/∂x from the element shape functions, then σ = [D]ε Why a model must be restrained With no fixtures, [K] is SINGULAR — it has no unique inverse, because the body can translate and rotate freely. A free 3D body has 6 rigid body modes (3 translations + 3 rotations) that must all be removed. The solver reports this as an "insufficient fixtures" or "large displacement" error (Page 13).
THE FIVE ASSUMPTIONS OF LINEAR STATIC ANALYSIS

The entire CSWA-S exam lives inside these assumptions, so knowing them — and knowing when they break — is directly examinable.

  • 1Linear elastic material. Stress is proportional to strain by Hooke's law and the part returns to its original shape. Consequence: the solver will happily report 900 MPa in a steel with a 250 MPa yield — it does not know about yielding. A result above yield is not a prediction; it is a statement that the model has left its own validity.
  • 2Small displacements. The deformed shape is close enough to the original that [K] does not change. Breaks for: fishing rods, membranes, snap-fits, buckling, anything where deflection is a noticeable fraction of a dimension. Symptom: the excessive-displacement warning.
  • 3Static loading. Loads are applied slowly enough that inertia and damping can be ignored — no dynamics, no impact, no vibration. Rule of thumb: if the loading time is short compared with the part's natural period, this fails and you need a dynamic study.
  • 4Boundary conditions are constant and applied at once. Loads do not move, fixtures do not change, and the load path does not depend on deformation.
  • 5Contact, if any, is linear. Bonded contact keeps the problem linear and solves in one pass. No penetration contact does not — the contact area depends on the solution, so the solver iterates. That is why a no-penetration study takes far longer and sometimes fails to converge (Page 8).
Superposition — the property that follows from linearity If load A alone gives stress σᵀ and load B alone gives σᴸ, then A and B together give σᵀ + σᴸ, and 2×A gives 2σᵀ. Exam use: "the load is doubled — what is the new maximum stress?" In a LINEAR static study the answer is exactly twice. If the question involves no-penetration contact, plasticity or large displacement, superposition does NOT hold and the answer is "cannot be scaled".

Quick check: A static study reports a maximum von Mises stress of 640 MPa in a part made of AISI 1020 steel (yield 351 MPa). A colleague says “the FEA proves it fails”. What is wrong with both the model and the statement?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The statement is wrong because the model is no longer valid at that stress, so it cannot prove anything about what happens there. A linear static study assumes the material obeys Hooke's law indefinitely — stress rises in proportion to strain with no upper limit. The solver has no knowledge of the 351 MPa yield strength; it happily extrapolated the elastic line far past the point where the real material would have started to deform plastically. In reality, once the material yields it redistributes load: the peak stress is limited, strain increases locally, and the surrounding material picks up the load. The real peak stress cannot exceed the material's actual capability, so 640 MPa is not a prediction of anything — it is a number produced by an equation applied outside its domain. What the result DOES tell you. It says the part is loaded beyond its elastic limit somewhere, which is genuinely useful information: the design is either under-sized, mis-loaded or mis-modelled. So the correct statement is not “the FEA proves it fails” but “the linear elastic assumption is violated, so I cannot conclude anything quantitative about the peak, and I must investigate”. What to do next, in order. (1) Check whether the peak is real or a singularity (Page 10). If the 640 MPa sits at a sharp re-entrant corner, a point load or a fixture edge, it is a mathematical artefact that will keep rising as you refine the mesh, and the correct response is to model the fillet, spread the load, or ignore that location — not to redesign the part. This is the single most common cause of alarming numbers in student work. (2) Verify the inputs: units (Page 5 — a load entered in the wrong system is a factor-of-4.45 or 1000 error), the load magnitude, the fixture choice, and whether the material assignment is what you think it is. (3) Check the reaction forces against the applied load (Page 11) — if they do not match, the model is wrong before any stress question arises. (4) If the peak is real and away from singularities, then either redesign (more material, a larger fillet, a different section) or move to a study that can represent yielding — a non-linear static analysis with an elastic-plastic material model, which is outside CSWA-S scope and outside SOLIDWORKS Simulation Standard. For the exam, remember the phrasing: a linear static result above yield means the assumption of linearity has been violated. That is the answer the examiners are looking for — not “the part breaks”.

CHOOSING THE RIGHT ELEMENT
The aspect-ratio decision SOLID all three dimensions are comparable — blocks, brackets, housings, castings, shafts. The safe default. SHELL one dimension much smaller than the other two. Rule of thumb: thickness < 1/10 to 1/20 of the smallest in-plane dimension. Sheet metal, pressure-vessel walls, panels. Why it matters: meshing a 1 mm sheet with solids needs at LEAST 2-3 elements through the thickness, so elements must be ~0.3 mm across a 500 mm panel — millions of elements, and the solve will never finish. A shell mesh does it in seconds. BEAM length much greater than cross-section — roughly 10:1 or more. Weldment frames, trusses, pipe racks. A 40-member frame is a few hundred DOF as beams and several million as solids. SOLIDWORKS behaviour to know for the exam • Solid bodies → meshed with SOLID elements by default • Sheet metal & surfaces → meshed with SHELL elements by default (mid-surface for sheet metal; the thickness comes from the model) • Weldment structural members → treated as BEAMS by default; you can right-click and "Treat as Solid" (and vice versa) • A MULTIBODY part meshes each solid body separately; bodies are joined by component contact (bonded by default) — Page 8 • Mixed meshes (solid + shell + beam in one study) are allowed, and the connections between different element types must be defined

One subtlety that appears in exam questions: shell elements have rotational degrees of freedom and solid elements do not. A solid element node can only translate, so when a shell edge meets a solid face, the rotational stiffness has nowhere to go — which is why shell-to-solid connections need care, and why applying a moment directly to a solid face is not possible in the way it is on a shell or beam node. Moments on solids are applied through a remote load or a bearing/torque load that converts them into a distributed force pattern (Page 7).

DRAFT VS HIGH QUALITY — WHY IT MATTERS SO MUCH

Draft (linear) elements

Strain is constant within each element, so stress is constant too. A bending field cannot be represented: the element resists bending by shearing, which makes it artificially stiff — shear locking. Results under-predict both displacement and stress, sometimes badly. Edges are straight, so curved geometry is faceted.

High quality (parabolic) elements

Mid-side nodes give quadratic displacement, so strain varies linearly within the element and bending is captured properly. Edges curve to follow the geometry. Roughly 2.5× the DOF per element, and far more than 2.5× the accuracy — which is why it is the default and why you should almost never change it.

The examinable summary: draft quality is for a first look at a large model; high quality is for any result you intend to quote. If a question offers “switch to draft quality” as a way to improve accuracy, it is wrong; if it offers it as a way to reduce solution time, it is right but with the accuracy caveat.

Quick check: A 2 mm thick sheet-metal bracket, 400 × 250 mm, is to be analysed. A candidate meshes it with solid elements at a 5 mm global size, and the study runs but reports a stiffness far higher than the physical part. Explain, and give the correct approach.
Tap to reveal ↓A: At a 5 mm element size on a 2 mm sheet, there is less than one element through the thickness. The mesher will produce a single layer of tetrahedra spanning the full 2 mm, and possibly elements whose faces span several millimetres across a 2 mm wall — giving appalling aspect ratios (Page 4). A single layer of elements cannot represent a through-thickness bending strain gradient: the top of the sheet must go into tension and the bottom into compression, and with one linear or even one parabolic element through the thickness that variation is captured crudely at best. The consequence is exactly what was observed — the model is far too stiff, under-predicting deflection and mis-representing bending stress. Adding to it, tetrahedral elements are especially poor in bending compared with hexahedra, and draft-quality tetrahedra are worse still through shear locking. Why brute force is not the answer. To mesh this properly with solids you would need at least two to three elements through the 2 mm thickness, so an element size of roughly 0.7 mm, and reasonable aspect ratios then force a comparable size in-plane. Over 400 × 250 mm that is on the order of a million elements and tens of millions of degrees of freedom — a model that will exhaust memory or take hours, to answer a question that shell elements answer in seconds. The correct approach. Mesh it with shell elements. In SOLIDWORKS Simulation a sheet-metal body is meshed with shells by default, using the mid-surface with the thickness taken from the sheet-metal feature; for a surface body you assign the thickness in the shell definition. Shell elements carry six degrees of freedom per node including rotations, so bending is represented by the element formulation itself rather than by resolving the through-thickness gradient with a stack of solids. Check three things afterwards: that the thickness shown in the shell definition matches the model, that the shell orientation (top/bottom faces) is consistent so that top and bottom stress results are not scrambled, and that stresses are examined on both faces — in bending the extremes are on the surfaces, and looking only at the mid-surface or one side will miss the peak. The general rule to carry into the exam: geometry drives the element type. Three comparable dimensions means solid; one small dimension means shell; one long dimension means beam. Choosing the wrong family is not a small inaccuracy — it changes the answer by a large factor and, as here, in the unsafe direction.

MESH QUALITY — THE TWO CHECKS
Both are only worth checking where the answer is. A sliver element in an unstressed corner is harmless; one at the peak stress invalidates the number.

The practical routine: after meshing, run Mesh → Create Mesh Plot → Aspect Ratio and Jacobian, and look at where the bad elements are. Also read the mesh details panel — node count, element count, percentage of elements with aspect ratio less than 3, and percentage greater than 10. Those two percentages are the quickest single indicator of mesh health, and they are quotable in an exam answer.

H-ADAPTIVE VS P-ADAPTIVE — EXPLICITLY EXAMINABLE

h-method (h-adaptive)

Keep the element order fixed and make the elements smaller — “h” is the classical symbol for element size. More elements, more DOF, mesh regenerated each pass. This is what you do manually every time you refine a mesh, and h-adaptive automates it: the solver runs, estimates the error, refines where the error is high, and repeats until a target accuracy is met.

p-method (p-adaptive)

Keep the mesh fixed and raise the polynomial order of the elements — second order, third, up to fifth. The same elements represent a richer displacement field. No re-meshing, so it suits geometry that is hard to mesh; convergence is often very fast, but each pass is more expensive per element.

Exam-ready comparison h-adaptive p-adaptive changes element SIZE (smaller) element ORDER (higher) re-meshes yes, every pass no — mesh unchanged DOF growth many more elements more DOF per element best for localised stress concentrations, where you know WHERE to refine smooth fields, awkward geometry available static studies, solid mesh static studies, solid mesh, single-body parts only Both are available only for static studies, and both report a convergence graph you can quote as evidence that the result is mesh-independent (Page 10).

Defeaturing is a legitimate modelling skill, not cheating. Cosmetic fillets, small chamfers, tapped holes, logos and fasteners far from the region of interest add elements without adding information — and can prevent the mesh from being created at all. The rule is: simplify anything that does not carry load or shape the stress field near the region you are reporting. What you must not remove is the fillet at a re-entrant corner, because that fillet is the only thing standing between you and a singularity (Page 10).

Quick check: A part meshes successfully at a 10 mm global size and gives a peak stress of 82 MPa. The candidate applies a mesh control of 1 mm to the fillet where the peak occurs, re-runs, and gets 131 MPa. Which number should be reported, and what should be done next?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Neither number should be reported yet — two data points do not establish convergence. What the pair does tell you is that the coarse mesh was badly under-resolved: a 60 % jump on refinement means the 10 mm mesh had nowhere near enough elements across the fillet to represent the stress gradient, so 82 MPa was never a candidate answer. But a single refinement cannot distinguish between a result that is approaching a real value and one that will keep climbing forever. What to do next. Run at least two more refinements — say 0.5 mm and 0.25 mm on the same fillet — and look at the sequence. If the increments shrink (131 → 141 → 145 → 146, with changes of 7.6 %, 2.8 %, 0.7 %), the result is converging on a real stress concentration and the converged value, around 146 MPa, is what you report — ideally with a Richardson extrapolation and a note that the last change was under 1 %. If instead the increments stay roughly constant or grow (131 → 175 → 233 → 310), you are refining into a singularity, the value has no limit, and no mesh will ever give an answer (Page 10). The two diagnostic questions to ask about the location. First, is there actually a fillet there in the geometry, or is it a sharp re-entrant corner? A zero-radius internal corner is a mathematical singularity in linear elasticity: the stress genuinely tends to infinity, and the only fix is to model the real fillet radius. Second, is the peak at a fixture edge or a point/vertex load? Those are singularities too, for the same reason, and the answer is to represent the real support or load footprint rather than to refine. How much refinement is enough. A useful working target for a fillet is roughly four to six elements across the fillet's arc, with high-quality (parabolic) elements; for a hole, twelve to sixteen elements around the circumference. Achieve that with a local mesh control rather than a small global size, so the model stays solvable. And check the mesh quality at the peak (aspect ratio and Jacobian) before believing any of it — a refined mesh full of slivers is not an improvement. For the exam: if a question shows two stress values from two mesh densities and asks which to trust, the expected answer is that the finer mesh is closer but convergence has not been demonstrated — and that the correct procedure is successive refinement until the change is small.

The three facts about materials that get examined 1. Only E and ν affect the stress result in a linear static study. Changing the yield strength changes the factor of safety plot and NOTHING else — not the stress, not the displacement. 2. Stress in a statically determinate part is independent of E. A steel and an aluminium bracket of identical geometry under the same load have the SAME stress; only the displacement differs, by the ratio of the moduli (about 3×). Stress depends on E only where the structure is statically indeterminate or made of several materials. 3. Isotropic linear elastic is the only model CSWA-S uses. Orthotropic, hyperelastic and plastic models exist but are out of scope — and a question offering "non-linear material" as an answer inside a static study is testing whether you know that.

A practical exam note: if a material is not applied, the study will not run — SOLIDWORKS reports the material as undefined. If the yield strength is missing from a custom material, the study will run but the factor-of-safety plot cannot be produced against yield. That distinction — solving needs E and ν; FOS needs a strength — is worth remembering as a discriminator between plausible multiple-choice answers.

Quick check: A study returns a maximum displacement of 0.0000315. The candidate reports “0.00003 mm — essentially rigid”. What has probably gone wrong, and how would you catch it in five seconds?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The plot is almost certainly in metres, not millimetres, so the true displacement is 0.0315 mm — a thousand times larger than reported. The number 0.0000315 with a leading string of zeros is the signature of an SI (metre-based) result being read as though it were millimetres. It matters because “essentially rigid” and “three hundredths of a millimetre” lead to different engineering conclusions, and because in a multiple-choice question both 0.0000315 and 0.0315 will be offered. How to catch it in five seconds — three independent checks. (1) Read the legend. Every SOLIDWORKS result plot states its units at the top of the colour bar. This is the definitive answer and takes one glance; the habit of reading the legend before the number is the single most valuable exam reflex on this page. (2) Order-of-magnitude reasoning. Ask what deflection you would expect: a steel bracket under a modest load deflects in the range of hundredths to tenths of a millimetre; a plastic clip, millimetres; a large steel beam, several millimetres. A result of 3 × 10−5 mm — thirty nanometres, roughly a hundred atoms — is not a plausible structural deflection under any real load, so the number is telling you the units are wrong before you have checked anything. If your hand value is 0.03 mm and the plot says 0.0000315, the factor of 1000 identifies the unit system immediately. The general rule this illustrates: in FEA the arithmetic is never wrong — the solver does exactly what it was told. Essentially every “wrong answer” is an input or interpretation error, and the largest category is units. So build the reflex: read the legend, sanity-check the magnitude, and confirm with a hand calculation. Those three take under a minute and will catch the majority of the mistakes that separate a pass from a fail on the hands-on problems.

THE THREE RULES OF RESTRAINT
  • 1All six rigid-body modes must be removed — and no more. A 3D body has three translations and three rotations. Leave one unrestrained and the stiffness matrix is singular and the solve fails or reports enormous displacements. Restrain more than necessary and you have invented stiffness: the part is now supported by something that does not exist in reality, and both the deflection and the stress distribution are wrong — usually optimistically.
  • 2Fixtures generate reaction forces — check them. Result → List Result Force gives the reaction at any fixture. The vector sum of all reactions must equal the applied load, to within solver tolerance. If it does not, something is wrong with the model, and no stress number from it means anything (Page 11).
  • 3Fixtures are singularity sites. A fixed geometry restraint on a face produces an artificial stress concentration at its boundary, because the model transitions from “infinitely stiff” to “free” over zero distance. If the peak stress in your result is at the edge of a fixture, it is very probably an artefact — refine, and it will keep rising (Page 10). Report the stress in the region of interest, not at the restraint.
Symmetry — the cheapest accuracy in FEA, with conditions Requirements, ALL of which must hold: • the GEOMETRY is symmetric about the plane • the MATERIAL is symmetric • the LOADING is symmetric about the same plane • the FIXTURES are symmetric about the same plane What it buys: half the model → roughly half the DOF, and often much better than half the solve time; or a quarter model for two planes. What it costs if misused: a bending or torsional load that is ANTI-symmetric about the plane is completely suppressed — the model silently prevents the deformation that actually occurs, and the answer is not merely inaccurate but qualitatively wrong. Exam framing: "can symmetry be used here?" is answered by checking all four conditions, not just the shape of the part.
Quick check: A bracket is bolted to a wall through four holes and loaded at its free end. A candidate applies Fixed Geometry to the whole back face. What is wrong, what happens to the results, and what would you do instead?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Fixing the whole back face asserts that every point of that face is welded to an infinitely rigid wall — which is not what four bolts do. A bolted joint clamps the material local to each hole, leaves the face between the bolts free to lift or slide, and provides finite rather than infinite stiffness. Fixing the whole face therefore (1) over-stiffens the bracket, so the computed deflection at the free end is smaller than reality, often substantially; (2) redistributes the stress, removing the load concentration that genuinely occurs around the bolt holes — which is usually where the part actually fails; and (3) invents a stress concentration along the boundary of the fixed face, at the transition from fully fixed to free, which will grow without limit on mesh refinement and can be mistaken for the critical stress. The result is unsafe in the specific sense that matters: it under-predicts deflection and misses the real hot spot while displaying a false one. What to do instead, in increasing order of fidelity. (1) Fix the bolt-hole faces rather than the whole back face — the simplest improvement, and often enough. Better still, apply Fixed Geometry only to the annular washer footprint around each hole (split the face with a sketch if the geometry does not already have it), which is a fair representation of where a bolt head or washer actually clamps. (2) Add a Roller/Slider on the remainder of the back face so that the bracket cannot pass through the wall but can lift away from it — this captures the fact that the joint reacts compression but not tension. (3) The most faithful approach available in a static study: model the back face with no-penetration contact against a fixed plate or a Virtual Wall, and use bolt connectors with an appropriate preload (Page 8). That gives the real load path — compression through the interface, tension through the bolts — and produces the bolt forces as an output you can check against the joint design. For the exam, the expected reasoning is the first two: fix what is physically fixed, not what is convenient, and remember that an over-constrained model is stiffer and less safe than the real part. And whichever you choose, check the reaction forces sum to the applied load before believing any stress number.

Point loads and edge loads — the guaranteed singularity A force applied to a VERTEX is transmitted through zero area. Quick check: A pin passes through a 20 mm bore in a lug and carries 8 kN. A candidate applies a 8 kN force to the whole cylindrical face in the load direction. Why is this wrong, and what are two better options?
Tap to reveal ↓A: A uniform force on the whole bore applies load to the half of the hole the pin is pulling away from. Physically, a pin bears only on the half of the bore it presses against; the opposite half carries nothing and, if anything, separates. Spreading 8 kN uniformly over the full 360° therefore (1) halves the peak bearing pressure, because the load is shared over twice the area that is really in contact; (2) applies a spurious outward traction on the unloaded half, which suppresses the ovalisation the lug would really experience; and (3) misplaces the stress peak — in reality the maximum tensile stress appears at the sides of the hole, on the net section perpendicular to the load, and this model will not reproduce that pattern correctly. The result is non-conservative in the place that matters, because lug failure is normally either bearing failure at the contact or tension failure across the net section. Two better options. (1) Bearing load. This is the purpose-built tool: select the cylindrical face, give the direction and the total force, and SOLIDWORKS distributes it as a sinusoidal (or parabolic) pressure over the loaded half of the bore, zero at the edges of the contact arc and maximum at the load line. It takes the same time as the wrong method, needs no extra geometry, and produces both the correct bearing pressure and the correct net-section stress. This is the answer the exam is looking for. (2) Split the face and apply pressure or force to the loaded half only — less elegant, but it works, and it is worth knowing because it generalises to contact patches that are not cylindrical. Convert the force to a pressure over the projected area if the question asks for pressure: for a 20 mm bore in a 12 mm thick lug, the projected bearing area is d × t = 240 mm², so the mean bearing pressure is 8000/240 = 33.3 MPa, with the sinusoidal peak roughly 4/π times higher. The most faithful option, if the pin itself matters, is to model the pin and use a no-penetration contact set between pin and bore, or a pin connector between two lugs (Page 8) — that lets the contact area develop from the solution rather than being assumed. It costs a non-linear, iterative solve, so reserve it for when the bearing detail is the answer rather than a boundary condition. The principle: match the load's distribution to the physics, not just its total. Getting the resultant right while getting the distribution wrong is one of the most common ways an FEA model looks right and answers the wrong question.

The reason connectors exist is cost. Modelling twelve M10 bolts as solid geometry with threads and no-penetration contact turns a five-minute study into an overnight non-linear solve that may never converge, and adds no accuracy where it matters. A bolt connector captures the two things the assembly actually cares about — the clamping preload and the load path through the fastener — in a handful of degrees of freedom, and gives you the bolt force as a directly checkable output.

CHOOSING THE CONTACT TYPE — THE DECISION
  • 1Are the parts genuinely joined? Welded, brazed, bonded, moulded together, or an interference fit that will never separate under the load — use bonded. It is faster, always converges, and is the right physics.
  • 2Can the faces separate or slide under load? A bolted flange that can gap, a part resting on another, a pin in a clearance hole, anything where the contact area changes — use no penetration, and accept the non-linear solve.
  • 3Does the joint transmit tension? This is the crisp test. Bonded transmits tension; a real unglued interface does not — it can only push. If bonded contact is holding two faces together in tension that physically would separate, the model is wrong in a way that understates stress.
  • 4Is friction important? No penetration with a friction coefficient is available and makes the solve harder still. Use it only when the load path depends on friction; otherwise leave it frictionless, which is conservative for sliding.
  • 5Start bonded, then refine. A pragmatic workflow: run the whole assembly bonded to get a working study and a first answer, then change only the joints that matter to no penetration. Changing everything at once produces a model that does not converge and gives no clue why.
Why no-penetration costs so much The contact AREA is part of the answer, not part of the question: the solver must guess which nodes are touching, solve, check whether any have interpenetrated or gone into tension, adjust, and repeat. ⇒ the stiffness matrix changes between iterations ⇒ superposition no longer holds — you cannot scale the result ⇒ solve time typically 5-50× a bonded equivalent ⇒ convergence is not guaranteed If a no-penetration study will not converge: check the initial gap (faces should be touching or very nearly), switch to a direct solver, refine the mesh at the contact, reduce the load and step it up, or simplify to bonded where the joint is not the answer.
Quick check: A two-part clamp bolted around a shaft is analysed with default settings. The result shows tensile stress across the split line where the two halves meet, and the halves stay perfectly together. Is this right?
Tap to reveal ↓A: No — the default bonded contact has welded the two halves together, and a split clamp is not welded. The tell-tale is exactly what was observed: tension across the split line. Two unbonded faces in contact can transmit compression and, with friction, shear — but they cannot pull on each other. A model that shows tension across a joint that physically can only push has told you, in the clearest possible way, that the contact definition is wrong. What the error does to the answer. Because the halves cannot separate, the clamp behaves as a continuous ring rather than as two pieces held together by bolts. That makes it much stiffer than reality, so deflections are under-predicted; it removes the load path through the bolts, so the bolt forces are wrong or absent; and it moves the stress peak away from where it really occurs — typically at the bolt holes and at the inner bore near the split, where the real clamp opens slightly under load. The model is unsafe in the usual direction: it makes the design look better than it is. The correct set-up. (1) Define a local contact set of type No Penetration between the two mating faces at the split line, so they can separate but not interpenetrate. Add friction only if the load path genuinely depends on it. (2) Add bolt connectors for the clamping screws, with a realistic preload — this is essential, because without preload the halves have nothing holding them together and the study may show rigid-body motion or fail to converge. The preload also produces the compressive interface pressure that a clamp works by. (3) Represent the shaft: either model it and use no-penetration contact between shaft and bore, or, if the shaft is not the subject, apply the clamping action as a pressure or use a virtual wall. (4) Expect a non-linear, iterative solve and budget the time; if it will not converge, check that the mating faces start in contact, use a direct solver, and refine the mesh locally at the split line and around the bolt holes. (5) When it runs, check the outputs that only this model can give you: the bolt axial and shear forces against the fastener rating, and the contact pressure distribution at the interface — and confirm that the split line now shows compression, with a small gap opening at the outer edge if the clamp is working as clamps do. The general lesson, and a good exam habit: after any assembly study, look at the interfaces and ask whether anything is being held together by glue that should not be. Quick check: A grey cast iron pump housing shows a maximum von Mises stress of 95 MPa against a 150 MPa strength, giving FOS 1.6. Why might this be dangerously optimistic, and what should be plotted instead?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Grey cast iron is brittle, and von Mises is a ductile-material criterion — it is the wrong tool for this material. The von Mises (distortion energy) criterion assumes failure occurs by yielding through shear-driven plastic flow, which is how ductile metals behave. Brittle materials do not yield appreciably; they fail by crack propagation from the maximum tensile stress, and they are dramatically stronger in compression than in tension — grey cast iron typically three to four times stronger. Because von Mises is a scalar built from stress differences, it cannot distinguish tension from compression at all: a region at 95 MPa von Mises might be at 95 MPa tension, at 95 MPa compression, or in a biaxial state, and those have entirely different margins for this material. The reported 1.6 could therefore be comfortable, or the part could already be beyond its tensile capacity somewhere the von Mises plot does not highlight. What to plot instead. (1) 1st principal stress (P1) — the maximum tensile stress — compared against the material's ultimate tensile strength, since brittle materials have no meaningful yield point. This is the Maximum Normal Stress (Rankine) criterion, and it is the FOS criterion to select in the dialog. (2) 3rd principal stress (P3) against the compressive strength, to confirm the compressive regions are also within limits — usually they are, by a wide margin. (3) Better still for this class of material, use the Mohr-Coulomb FOS criterion, which takes separate tensile and compressive limits and handles the mixed states properly. (4) Look at where P1 peaks: in a casting it will typically be at a re-entrant corner, a core-print transition or a section change — and then apply the checks of Page 10 to establish whether that peak is real or a singularity at an unmodelled fillet. Two further cast-iron considerations worth stating. Castings have porosity, inclusions and residual stresses, and their properties vary with section thickness, so the design factor applied to a brittle casting is normally larger than for a wrought ductile part. And grey iron's tensile strength is the relevant limit even in a part that is mostly in compression, because it is the local tension — often bending on the far side of a boss or at a fillet — that starts the crack. The exam-ready rule: ductile → von Mises against yield; brittle → maximum principal against ultimate. A question that specifies cast iron, ceramic or concrete is almost always testing exactly this.

Convergence vs Singularity

The most important page in this guide. It is also the exam's stated skill: “determining if results are valid and make sense”.

THE TWO CURVES
Two data points cannot tell these apart. Three or four can, instantly — which is why convergence checking is a procedure, not an opinion.
The test Run at successively halved element sizes and look at the INCREMENTS: increments SHRINK, each roughly a constant fraction of the last → CONVERGING. Stop when the change is under ~2-5 %, and report the last value (or the extrapolation below). increments CONSTANT or GROWING → SINGULARITY. No amount of refinement will help. The location is a mathematical artefact, not a stress. Richardson extrapolation — the estimate of the true value With values σ₁, σ₂, σ₃ at element sizes h, h/2, h/4: r = (σ₂ − σ₁) / (σ₃ − σ₂) the increment ratio p = log₂(r) the observed order σ∞ ≈ σ₃ + (σ₃ − σ₂) / (r − 1) r > 1 → converging, and σ∞ is the estimated exact answer. r ≤ 1 → diverging — the formula returns nonsense, which is itself the diagnosis.

THE FOUR SINGULARITY SITES

Sharp re-entrant corner

A zero-radius internal corner. In linear elasticity the stress there is genuinely infinite — it is not a numerical error but a property of the mathematical model. Fix: model the real fillet. Every manufactured corner has one.

Point or edge load

Force through zero area. Stress = F/A with A → 0. Fix: spread the load over the true contact patch, or use a bearing or remote load. Results away from the point remain valid (Saint-Venant).

Fixture boundary

The edge of a fixed-geometry face — infinitely stiff meeting free, over zero distance. Fix: restrain what is physically restrained; use elastic support or contact against a real body.

Bonded contact edge

Where a bonded interface ends, the same infinite-stiffness discontinuity appears. Fix: no-penetration contact, a fillet or weld geometry, or simply do not report stress there.

Saint-Venant's principle — why singularities are survivable "The stresses produced by statically equivalent load systems differ appreciably only in the immediate vicinity of the load." Practically: beyond roughly ONE CHARACTERISTIC DIMENSION (a thickness, a width, a diameter) from a singular point, the stress field is correct regardless of how crudely the load or restraint was applied. ⇒ a point load is fine for finding the bending stress in a beam. ⇒ it is useless for the stress under the load. ⇒ on the exam: if the peak is AT the load or the fixture, look away from it and report the field stress — or say the peak is a singularity, which is often the answer being sought.
THE VALIDITY CHECKLIST
  • 1Do the reaction forces equal the applied load? Result → List Result Force. If not, the model is wrong — stop here (Page 11).
  • 2Does the deformed shape make sense at 1 : 1 scale? Bending where you expect bending, no rigid-body drift, no parts passing through each other.
  • 3Is the maximum displacement plausible? Compare with a hand calculation and with common sense — hundredths of a millimetre for a stiff steel bracket, not metres and not nanometres.
  • 4Where is the peak stress? If it is at a corner, a load point, a fixture edge or a bonded-contact edge, treat it as a singularity until proven otherwise.
  • 5Has convergence been demonstrated? Three or four refinements with shrinking increments, or an h-adaptive convergence graph.
  • 6Is the mesh quality acceptable at the peak? Aspect ratio and Jacobian, checked where the answer is (Page 4).
  • 7Do nodal and element stress plots agree? A large discrepancy means the mesh is too coarse.
  • 8Is the result below yield? If not, the linear assumption is violated and the number is not a prediction (Page 2).
Quick check: A candidate refines a mesh four times at a sharp internal corner and reports “the stress converged to 310 MPa at the finest mesh I could solve”. What is wrong with the statement, and what is the correct engineering response?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Nothing converged — the candidate simply ran out of computer. At a sharp re-entrant corner with zero radius, the elasticity solution has a genuine stress singularity: the theoretical stress tends to infinity as the corner is approached. Every refinement will return a higher number, and the “final” value of 310 MPa is not a property of the part — it is a property of the smallest element the machine could solve. Solve it on a bigger computer and you get a bigger number. The give-away is in the data the candidate already has: if the increments between successive refinements were not shrinking, the sequence was diverging, and calling the last point “converged” misreads the evidence. Why it matters practically. Reporting 310 MPa would condemn a part that may be perfectly sound, or — if the number happened to look acceptable — would provide false assurance. Either way the number cannot be used, and design decisions taken on it are unfounded. The correct response, in order. (1) Recognise it as a singularity and say so explicitly: sharp re-entrant corner, no convergence, stress unbounded. That statement is itself the professional answer, and on the exam it is very often the answer being tested. (2) Fix the geometry, because the real part has no sharp corner. Machined corners carry the cutter radius; cast and moulded corners have a draft fillet; welded corners have a weld toe radius; sheet-metal bends have a bend radius. Model the actual radius — even a small one, 0.5 mm or 1 mm — and the singularity disappears, because the stress at a finite-radius fillet is finite. Then refine to four to six elements across the fillet arc and demonstrate real convergence. (3) If the radius is genuinely unknown, bound the answer: run the study at two or three plausible radii and report the stress as a function of radius. That is honest, useful, and shows the designer exactly how much the fillet is worth — usually a great deal, since peak stress falls steeply with increasing radius. (4) If the corner is remote from the region of interest, apply Saint-Venant: state that the singularity is local, report the stress at least one characteristic dimension away, and note that the corner requires either a fillet or a separate fatigue assessment. (5) For fatigue or fracture questions, note that a sharp corner is not merely a modelling nuisance — it is a crack initiation site, and the right treatment is a fracture-mechanics or notch-sensitivity approach rather than a peak-stress reading. The one-line rule to carry into the exam: refinement that keeps increasing the answer is not convergence — it is a diagnosis.

Quick check: A hands-on exam problem gives a cantilever bracket and asks for the maximum von Mises stress, offering 33.3, 66.7, 148 and 296 MPa. Your study returns 38.9 MPa. How do you decide what to answer?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Do the hand calculation, then reconcile the difference — do not simply pick the nearest option. A beam-theory estimate for a cantilever is σ = Mc/I = FLc/I and takes under a minute. Suppose it gives 33.3 MPa. Your FEA says 38.9 MPa, which is 17 % higher — and that difference is informative, not a problem: real brackets are not ideal beams. The likely contributors are a fillet or a section change acting as a stress concentration, the local effect of the fixture, and the fact that a short, deep bracket carries shear as well as bending. A finite-element result modestly above simple beam theory is exactly what you should expect. So the answer is 33.3 MPa if the question is asking for the beam-theory value, and neither 33.3 nor the others if it wants your FEA number. This is where reading the question matters: a hands-on problem normally asks for the value from your study, with the options spaced far enough apart that the correct one is unambiguous. Here 33.3 is the nearest option to 38.9, and the others are 2×, 4.4× and 8.9× away — look at those multiples. The multiples are the real clue. 66.7 is exactly 2× 33.3 — the “per item vs total” error, or a load applied twice. 148 is about 4.45× — the lbf-entered-as-N error. 296 is about 8.9× — both mistakes together. A well-written exam builds its distractors from the specific errors it is testing for, so recognising the ratios tells you what mistake each option represents and confirms which one is the clean answer. Since your value sits closest to 33.3 and every other option corresponds to an identifiable blunder, answer 33.3 MPa with confidence. Before committing, spend two minutes on the standard checks: confirm the reaction force equals the applied load; confirm the units in the load dialog and the plot legend; check whether your 38.9 MPa peak is at a fillet in the body of the bracket (plausible and real) or at the fixed-face boundary (a singularity, in which case read the stress in the beam section instead and you will land closer to 33.3). That last check very often resolves the remaining discrepancy entirely. The habit to build: in a multiple-choice FEA question, the options are a diagnostic tool. Compute the hand value, look at the ratios between the options, and identify which error each represents — the correct answer is usually the one that no error explains.

ARCHETYPE 1 · THE SINGLE-PART BRACKET

“Apply the given material. Fix the mounting face. Apply a force of X to the top face in the direction of Y. Mesh with high quality, element size Z. What is the maximum von Mises stress / maximum resultant displacement / minimum factor of safety?”

What is being tested

Whether you can build a complete study unaided and read a value correctly. Almost no theory — pure fluency.

Where marks are lost

Units in the load dialog; using the specified element size rather than the default (the expected answer depends on the mesh); reading the colour bar instead of probing; missing that the question wants displacement in inches.

The check

Hand-calculate the bending stress from the overall geometry. Your FEA should be the same order and typically 10–30 % higher because of the fillets.

Speed tip

Apply material first, then fixtures, then loads — the order the tree is in. Use the exact mesh size given: the examiner solved it at that size and the tolerance band assumes it.

ARCHETYPE 2 · THE ASSEMBLY WITH CONTACT OR CONNECTORS

“Two plates joined by bolts / a pin through two lugs / a bracket bolted to a base. Define the contact as no-penetration between faces A and B. What is the resultant force on the pin, or the maximum stress in the lug?”

What is being tested

Contact sets, connectors, and the understanding that bonded is the default and usually wrong for these joints (Page 8).

Where marks are lost

Leaving global bonded contact in place; forgetting bolt preload so the model is under-constrained; not knowing that connector forces are read from Result → List Pin/Bolt Force; running out of time on a slow non-linear solve.

The check

The sum of the connector forces plus the interface reactions must equal the applied load. And the joint should show compression at the interface, not tension.

Time risk

No-penetration contact makes this the slowest problem. Set it up carefully once rather than re-running — a failed convergence costs the whole time budget.

ARCHETYPE 3 · THE MESH / CONVERGENCE OR MODIFICATION PROBLEM

“Run the study at element size A, then at size B. By what percentage does the maximum stress change?” or “Change the material to aluminium and the load to 2X. What is the new maximum displacement?”

Quick check: A hands-on problem says “mesh with a global element size of 5 mm, high quality, standard mesh”. The candidate uses the default curvature-based mesh at the default size and gets an answer 12 % away from every option. What happened, and what is the lesson?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The examiner specified the mesh because the expected answer depends on it. Stress is mesh-dependent — that is the whole content of Page 10 — so a problem that asks for a specific numerical value must pin down the mesh, or no unique answer exists. By ignoring the stated mesher and element size, the candidate solved a slightly different problem and landed between the offered options. The 12 % discrepancy is not an error in the software or in the candidate's understanding of FEA; it is the difference between two legitimate meshes, and it is exactly the size of gap that a well-set question uses to catch this. What to do. Re-mesh with the stated settings — standard mesh, high quality, 5 mm global size — and re-run. If the tolerance band still does not capture your value, work through the rest of the specification line by line: was the material the exact one named, were the fixtures on the faces stated, was the load per-item or total, were the units right? Exam problems are written so that following every instruction literally produces an answer inside the band. The lesson, and it generalises well beyond the exam. In a hands-on FEA question, every stated setting is part of the answer: the mesher type, the element size, the element quality, the material, the fixture faces, the load direction and the units. There is no scope for “I used a better mesh” — the question is not asking for the best analysis, it is asking whether you can reproduce a defined one. Read the problem statement twice and treat it as a specification, not a suggestion. Two practical habits that prevent this. First, before solving, write the specified settings on your scratch paper and tick them off as you enter them — the whole list takes twenty seconds and eliminates the commonest cause of near-miss answers. Second, if your value falls between two options rather than near one, do not guess: that pattern almost always means a setting was missed, and re-checking the specification is a far better use of two minutes than choosing the nearer number. And a genuine engineering point underneath it: the fact that a 12 % change comes from a mesh choice alone is precisely why professional reports state the mesh, and why a stress value quoted without its mesh and convergence evidence is not really a result at all.

Quick check: A study solves and reports a maximum resultant displacement of 4 200 mm on a 300 mm bracket, with the excessive-displacement warning. The candidate enables large displacement and re-runs. Assess.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Wrong response — the number is not a deflection, it is a symptom, and enabling large displacement treats the symptom. A 300 mm steel bracket cannot deflect 4.2 metres under any load it could survive; long before that it would have yielded, buckled and failed. A displacement fourteen times the size of the part is not a structural result at all, so running a slower non-linear solve on the same bad inputs will simply produce a slow, expensive, equally meaningless answer — and on the exam it will consume most of the remaining time. What it almost certainly is, in order of probability. (1) A missing or insufficient fixture. If the model is not fully restrained, part of it moves as a rigid body. The solver reports that motion as “displacement”, and the magnitude is governed by numerical conditioning rather than by physics — which is exactly why it comes out as an absurd number. Check that all six rigid-body modes are removed, and check that every body is connected: a floating body in a multibody part or assembly gives precisely this, often together with a zero-pivot warning. (2) A unit error in the load. A load intended as newtons entered where the dialog was set to something else, or a metre/millimetre mismatch, scales the answer by 1000 or more. The ratio between the reported and expected displacement usually names the factor. (3) A material error — no material, or a modulus entered in the wrong units, giving a part made of something a thousand times softer than steel. (4) A contact error — in an assembly, an interface that should transmit load is set to allow penetration, so parts pass through each other and the load path does not exist. The diagnostic sequence, which takes about two minutes. First, List Result Force at the fixtures and compare with the applied load; if the reactions are zero or wildly wrong, the fixtures or the load are the problem, and you have localised it immediately. Second, animate the deformed shape at 1 : 1: rigid-body motion looks like the whole part sliding or spinning, whereas real deformation looks like bending or stretching — the distinction is obvious on screen and instantly identifies case (1). Third, check the load dialog and the plot legend units. Fourth, confirm the material's E. When large displacement IS the right answer: when the fixtures, loads, materials and contacts are all verified correct and the part is genuinely slender and flexible — a leaf spring, a thin membrane, a snap-fit, a fishing rod — and the deflection is a real fraction of the geometry. That is a legitimate non-linear geometric problem, it lies outside CSWA-S scope, and recognising it as such is the answer the exam wants rather than solving it.

The four questions a static study cannot answer — examinable 1. "Will it buckle?" A slender column in compression can fail at a load far below its yield stress. A linear static study will happily report a comfortable stress and a comfortable factor of safety right up to and past the buckling load. Only a buckling study finds it. Pcr = π²EI/(KL)² is the hand check (Page 11). 2. "Will it resonate?" Static analysis has no concept of time or inertia. A frequency study gives the natural frequencies; the design rule is to keep them away from the excitation, typically by a factor of at least 1.5 to 2. 3. "What happens after it yields?" Linear static extrapolates the elastic line forever (Page 2). Load redistribution, permanent set and collapse need a non-linear study. 4. "How long will it last?" Fatigue failure occurs at stresses far below yield after enough cycles. A static factor of safety of 3 says nothing about a part that sees 10⁷ cycles.

Quick check: A 1.2 m long, 25 mm diameter steel rod is loaded in compression at 40 kN. A static study reports 81 MPa and a factor of safety of 3.1 against a 250 MPa yield. Is the design safe?
Tap to reveal ↓A: No — and the static study is incapable of telling you so. The stress calculation is perfectly correct: A = π(25)²/4 = 491 mm², so σ = 40 000/491 = 81 MPa, and 250/81 = 3.1. But a slender member in compression does not fail by yielding; it fails by buckling, at a load that depends on stiffness and length, not on strength. The hand check. I = πd⁴/64 = π(25)⁴/64 = 19 175 mm⁴. Taking pinned ends (K = 1), Pcr = π²EI/(KL)² = π² × 200 000 × 19 175 / 1200² ≈ 26.3 kN. The applied load is 40 kN — roughly 1.5 times the critical buckling load. The rod would collapse sideways long before anything approached yield, and it would do so suddenly. The critical stress at buckling is only about 53 MPa, well under a third of the yield strength, which is the signature of a slenderness-dominated member. Why the static study missed it entirely. Linear static analysis solves [K]{u} = {F} with the stiffness matrix built from the undeformed geometry, and it has no mechanism for the stiffness reduction that compression causes as a member begins to bow. Buckling is a stability problem — an eigenvalue problem — not a strength problem, and a static solve will return a comfortable, entirely valid, entirely irrelevant stress at any compressive load whatsoever. This is the single most dangerous blind spot in routine FEA, because the result looks reassuring. What to do. Run a buckling study, which returns a buckling load factor (BLF): the multiplier on the applied load at which instability occurs. A BLF above 1 means stable at that load; here it would come out around 0.66, immediately flagging failure. Design practice is to require a substantial margin — commonly a BLF of at least 2 to 4 — because the linear (Euler) prediction is optimistic: real members have initial crookedness, load eccentricity and imperfect end conditions, all of which reduce the actual capacity. Check the end fixity too, since K has an enormous effect: fixed-fixed (K = 0.5) quadruples Pcr to about 105 kN and makes the design viable, while fixed-free (K = 2) quarters it to 6.6 kN. The fixes, if it does buckle: increase I (a larger diameter or, far more efficiently, a tube — I ∝ d⁴ so material moved outward is worth far more than material added inward), shorten the effective length by adding an intermediate support, or improve the end restraint. Note that changing to a stronger steel does nothing at all: Pcr depends on E, which is essentially the same for every steel. The exam-ready summary: whenever a question involves a long, slender member in compression, the static factor of safety is not the answer — buckling is.

RECAP — THE EIGHT IDEAS

1 · Displacement first, stress second

Stress is obtained by differentiating displacement, so it converges more slowly and is always the less reliable number. Everything about meshing follows from this.

2 · Linear static has five assumptions

Linear material, small displacement, static loading, constant boundary conditions, linear contact. A result above yield means an assumption has been broken, not that the part fails.

3 · Geometry chooses the element

Three comparable dimensions → solid; one small → shell; one long → beam. High-quality parabolic elements by default — draft elements are artificially stiff.

4 · Fixtures are the biggest lie in most models

Fix what is physically fixed. Over-constraint makes the part stiffer and the answer optimistic, and fixture edges generate false peaks.

5 · Bonded is the default and often wrong

Bonded transmits tension. If an interface physically can only push, it needs no-penetration contact — and a non-linear solve.

6 · von Mises for ductile, P1 for brittle

von Mises is a scalar and cannot tell tension from compression. FOS is only as good as the strength in the material definition.

7 · Convergence is a procedure, not an opinion

Three or four refinements with shrinking increments. Increments that do not shrink mean a singularity, and no mesh will ever fix it.

8 · Verify with arithmetic

Reaction force equals applied load; hand calculation within about 10 %; symmetry gives symmetry; doubling the load doubles the stress. Thirty seconds, every time.

Final check 1: In one paragraph, explain why a linear static FEA result can be numerically exact and engineering nonsense at the same time.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because the solver answers the question it was asked, not the question you meant. Given a mesh, a material, a set of fixtures and a set of loads, it forms [K]{u} = {F} and solves it to machine precision — equilibrium is enforced exactly, and the arithmetic is never wrong. But every one of those inputs is a model of reality chosen by you: the fixtures assert something about the rest of the world, the loads assert a magnitude, a direction and a distribution, the material asserts linear elasticity without limit, the mesh determines how finely the stress field can be represented, and the contact definitions assert which parts are joined. Each of those can be wrong while the mathematics remains impeccable — a load in the wrong units, a face fixed that is really bolted at four points, bonded contact across a joint that can only push, a point load producing an infinite stress, a mesh too coarse to see the gradient, or a stress reported above yield where the linear assumption has already collapsed. The result is a number computed correctly from a description of a part that does not exist. This is why the professional discipline is not to trust the plot but to verify: check that the reaction forces equal the applied load, that the deformed shape at 1 : 1 makes physical sense, that the magnitude matches a hand calculation, that the peak is not at a singularity, and that refinement shows convergence. The colour plot is a hypothesis; the verification is what turns it into a result.

WE1 — The cantilever hand-check.
A steel cantilever, rectangular section 20 mm wide × 30 mm deep, projects 200 mm and carries 500 N at its free end. E = 200 GPa, yield 250 MPa. (a) Compute the maximum bending stress, the tip deflection and the factor of safety. (b) How significant is transverse shear? (c) What would you expect an FEA of the real bracket to give, and why?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) The three numbers. Second moment of area I = bh³/12 = 20 × 30³/12 = 20 × 27 000/12 = 45 000 mm⁴. Distance to the extreme fibre c = h/2 = 15 mm, so the section modulus Z = I/c = 3 000 mm³. Bending moment at the root M = FL = 500 × 200 = 100 000 N·mm. Bending stress σ = Mc/I = M/Z = 100 000/3 000 = 33.33 MPa. Tip deflection δ = FL³/(3EI) = 500 × 200³/(3 × 200 000 × 45 000) = 4.0 × 10⁹/2.7 × 10¹⁰ = 0.1481 mm. Factor of safety = 250/33.33 = 7.5. Note the units discipline: newtons and millimetres throughout gives N/mm² = MPa directly, with no conversion anywhere. (b) Transverse shear. For a rectangle the maximum shear stress is at the neutral axis: τmax = 3V/2A = 3 × 500/(2 × 600) = 1.25 MPa, which is 3.7 % of the bending stress. Two lessons follow. First, for a beam of this slenderness (L/h ≈ 6.7) bending dominates completely and shear can be neglected in a hand check — which is why the classical formulas ignore it. Second, the maximum shear and the maximum bending stress occur at different places: shear peaks at the neutral axis where bending stress is zero, and bending peaks at the surfaces where shear is zero. For short, deep beams (L/h below about 4) shear becomes significant and FEA will show a noticeably higher von Mises than simple bending theory. (c) What FEA will give, and why it differs. Expect a von Mises stress at the root somewhat above 33.3 MPa — typically 10–30 % higher on a real bracket — for four identifiable reasons. (1) The fillet or the fixture edge. If the bracket has a root fillet, it concentrates stress; if you fixed a face, the boundary of that face is a singularity (Page 10) and the peak there is not real. (2) Combined stress. von Mises combines the bending stress with the shear and any transverse components, so it is always at least the bending stress and usually a little more. (3) Local load effects. If the 500 N is applied at a vertex or edge, the stress near the load is singular; beyond one section depth from it, Saint-Venant's principle says the field is correct. (4) Beam theory assumes plane sections remain plane, which is violated near the support and near the load. How to use this in the exam: compute 33.3 MPa first, then run the study. If the FEA gives 36–43 MPa you have mutual confirmation and can answer with confidence. If it gives 148 MPa (4.45×) you have a lbf/N error; if it gives 66.7 MPa (2×) you have applied the load twice; if it gives 0.033 MPa or 33 300 you have a unit-system error. The hand calculation does not merely check the answer — the ratio between it and the FEA names the mistake.
WE2 — von Mises, Tresca and factor of safety.
At the critical node the principal stresses are σ1 = 120 MPa, σ2 = 45 MPa, σ3 = −30 MPa. Yield strength 250 MPa. (a) Compute the von Mises stress and the factor of safety. (b) Compute the Tresca equivalent and its FOS. (c) Which is right? (d) What happens under pure hydrostatic pressure?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) von Mises. σvM = √{½[(σ₁−σ₂)² + (σ₂−σ₃)² + (σ₃−σ₁)²]}. The differences are 120−45 = 75, 45−(−30) = 75, and −30−120 = −150. So σvM = √{½[75² + 75² + 150²]} = √{½[5 625 + 5 625 + 22 500]} = √{16 875} = 129.90 MPa. FOS = 250/129.90 = 1.925. (b) Tresca. The maximum-shear-stress criterion uses the largest difference between principal stresses: σ1 − σ3 = 120 − (−30) = 150 MPa (equivalently τmax = 75 MPa, half of it). FOS = 250/150 = 1.667. (c) Which is right. Both are legitimate yield criteria for ductile metals, and they bracket reality. Tresca is always the more conservative — here by 13.4 % on the factor of safety — because its yield surface is a hexagon inscribed inside von Mises's ellipse, so it predicts yielding at or before von Mises does. Experimental data on ductile metals generally sits closer to von Mises, which is why it is the default in FEA and in most design codes. Tresca is preferred where conservatism is required by code (some pressure-vessel and nuclear standards) and because it is simpler to apply by hand. The two agree exactly in uniaxial tension and differ most in pure shear, where the ratio is 2/√3 = 1.155: for σ1 = τ, σ2 = 0, σ3 = −τ, von Mises gives √3 τ = 1.732τ while Tresca gives 2τ. For the exam: von Mises unless the question says otherwise; Tresca if it asks for the maximum shear stress criterion or for the more conservative answer. (d) Hydrostatic pressure. Set σ₁ = σ₂ = σ₃ = −p. Every difference in the von Mises formula becomes zero, so σvM = 0 regardless of the magnitude of p, and Tresca also gives zero. This is not a defect but the central physical insight of the criterion: yielding in ductile metals is caused by distortion — change of shape — and pure hydrostatic pressure changes volume without changing shape. It is why a steel component can survive at the bottom of the ocean under enormous pressure, why hydrostatic pressure does not appear in the yield criterion at all, and why the von Mises stress is sometimes called the equivalent deviatoric stress. The practical corollary in FEA: a region showing high principal stresses but low von Mises is under near-hydrostatic loading and is not close to yielding — but if the material is brittle, that same region may be very close to failure, because brittle fracture is driven by the maximum principal stress (Page 9).
WE3 — Convergence or singularity?
Two locations are refined through four successively halved meshes. Location A gives 96.2, 104.8, 108.1, 109.3 MPa. Location B gives 96, 128, 171, 228 MPa. (a) Analyse both. (b) Estimate the true value where one exists. (c) What should be reported for each?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) Location A — converging. The increments are 8.6, 3.3 and 1.2 MPa, i.e. percentage changes of 8.94 %, 3.15 % and 1.10 %. Each increment is roughly a third of the one before, so the sequence is settling towards a limit. The last change being about 1 % is the usual practical criterion for declaring mesh independence. Location B — diverging. The increments are 32, 43 and 57 MPa — they are growing, and the percentage changes are 33.3 %, 33.6 % and 33.3 %, essentially constant. A constant fractional growth per halving of element size is the classic signature of a singularity: the stress is behaving like a power law with no finite limit, and every further refinement will multiply it again by about 1.33. (b) Extrapolating location A. The increment ratio r = (108.1 − 104.8)/(109.3 − 108.1) = 3.3/1.2 = 2.75. The observed order of convergence p = log₂(2.75) = 1.46, which is a plausible value for stress at a geometric feature with parabolic elements. Richardson extrapolation gives σ∞ ≈ σ₄ + (σ₄ − σ₃)/(r − 1) = 109.3 + 1.2/1.75 = 109.3 + 0.69 = 110.0 MPa. So the finest mesh is already within 0.6 % of the estimated exact answer. For location B the same formula gives r = 43/57 = 0.75, which is less than 1, and the extrapolation returns a nonsensical (negative-denominator) result — and that failure is itself the diagnosis: a ratio at or below 1 means no limit exists. (c) What to report. For location A: 110 MPa (or 109.3 MPa from the finest mesh), stated together with the evidence — four refinements, last change 1.1 %, extrapolated value 110.0 MPa. A stress quoted without its convergence evidence is not really a result. For location B: no stress value can be reported. The correct statement is that the location is a singularity, that the computed stress increases without limit under refinement, and that the cause must be identified — almost certainly a sharp re-entrant corner, a point or edge load, a fixture boundary, or the edge of a bonded contact (Page 10). The engineering response is to model the real fillet radius, distribute the load over its true contact area, or represent the support realistically, and then to re-run the convergence study on the corrected geometry. If location B is remote from the region of interest, invoke Saint-Venant's principle: report the stress at least one characteristic dimension away and note the singularity explicitly. The transferable procedure: always take at least three, preferably four, refinements; look at the increments rather than the values; compute the ratio; and let the ratio tell you whether you are converging on an answer or chasing infinity.
WE4 — Plate with a hole: the Kt cross-check.
A 100 mm wide, 10 mm thick plate with a 20 mm central hole carries 50 kN in tension. Yield 250 MPa. (a) Compute the nominal and peak stresses. (b) What Kt would you quote? (c) What mesh is needed? (d) An FEA returns 158 MPa — is that acceptable?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) The stresses. The net section — the material actually carrying load at the hole — is (W − d) × t = (100 − 20) × 10 = 800 mm², giving σnom = 50 000/800 = 62.5 MPa. The gross section is 100 × 10 = 1 000 mm², giving σgross = 50.0 MPa. With d/W = 0.20, the standard finite-width relation Kt = 3.00 − 3.13(0.2) + 3.66(0.2)² − 1.53(0.2)³ = 3.00 − 0.626 + 0.1464 − 0.0122 = 2.508. Hence σmax = 2.508 × 62.5 = 156.8 MPa, at the two sides of the hole on the net-section line. Factor of safety on the peak = 250/156.8 = 1.59, against 4.00 if you had (wrongly) used the nominal stress — and that difference is the stress concentration. (b) Which Kt to quote — the trap. Referred to the gross section the same physical peak gives Kt = 156.8/50.0 = 3.135. Both numbers are correct; they refer to different nominal stresses. Textbook charts and the formula above use the net section, so the answer to quote is 2.508 — but you must say which nominal it refers to, because 2.51 and 3.14 are both offered as options in exactly this kind of question. Note also that as d/W → 0 the formula tends to 3.00, the classical infinite-plate result, which is worth remembering as a check on any hole calculation. (c) The mesh. To resolve the gradient round the hole you need enough elements along the arc: a practical target is 12–16 elements around the full circumference, or equivalently an element size of about hole radius/4 = 10/4 = 2.5 mm at the hole, with high-quality (parabolic) elements. Achieve it with a local mesh control on the hole face rather than a small global size, so the rest of the plate stays coarse and the model stays fast. Then demonstrate convergence with one or two further refinements (Page 10) — and check aspect ratio and Jacobian at the hole, since that is where the answer is. (d) Is 158 MPa acceptable? Yes — it is 0.8 % above the analytical 156.8 MPa, which is excellent agreement and confirms both the model and the mesh. This is exactly why the plate-with-a-hole is the standard verification case: the answer is known independently, so it tests the analyst rather than the geometry. If instead the FEA had returned 125 MPa you would suspect an under-refined mesh (too few elements round the hole, so the peak is smeared); if it had returned 250 MPa you would suspect a singularity from a point load or fixture edge, or a units error. The habit worth forming: before running any model with a known feature — a hole, a fillet, a shoulder — look up or compute Kt and predict the peak. It takes a minute, it validates the mesh, and it converts the FEA from an oracle into an instrument you have calibrated.
WE5 — The unit trap, in full.
A cantilever 8 in long, section 0.75 × 1.5 in, carries 250 lbf at the free end. Material yield 36 ksi. (a) Solve in IPS. (b) Solve in MMGS and confirm they agree. (c) What happens if 250 is typed into a dialog set to newtons? (d) How would you catch it?
Tap to reveal ↓A:
(a) In IPS. I = bh³/12 = 0.75 × 1.5³/12 = 0.75 × 3.375/12 = 0.2109 in⁴; c = 0.75 in; M = FL = 250 × 8 = 2 000 lbf·in. Therefore σ = Mc/I = 2 000 × 0.75/0.2109 = 7 111 psi = 7.11 ksi, and FOS = 36/7.11 = 5.06. (b) In MMGS. Convert first: F = 250 × 4.44822 = 1 112.06 N; L = 8 × 25.4 = 203.2 mm; b = 19.05 mm; h = 38.1 mm. Then I = 19.05 × 38.1³/12 = 87 799 mm⁴; c = 19.05 mm; M = 1 112.06 × 203.2 = 225 970 N·mm; σ = 225 970 × 19.05/87 799 = 49.03 MPa. Cross-check: 7 111 psi × 0.00689476 = 49.03 MPa — identical, as it must be. Yield 36 ksi = 36 × 6.89476 = 248.2 MPa, so FOS = 248.2/49.03 = 5.06, the same in both systems. Physics does not care which units you use; only you do. (c) The trap. Typing 250 into a dialog still set to newtons applies a load 4.448 times too small. The stress becomes 49.03/4.448 = 11.02 MPa and the factor of safety becomes 248.2/11.02 = 22.5. Nothing warns you. No error appears, the study solves cleanly, the plot looks entirely normal, and the answer is comfortably conservative-looking — which is precisely why this error survives to the answer sheet and, in professional practice, to the drawing. The part is four and a half times more heavily loaded than the analysis believes. (d) How to catch it — four independent defences. (1) Read the unit dropdown before typing any number. Loads, material, results and document units are four separate settings and none of them has to agree with the others. This one habit prevents the error entirely. (2) Check the reaction force (Result → List Result Force) against the load you intended, converted by hand. A reaction of 250 N when you intended 250 lbf shows the discrepancy immediately, and a ratio of exactly 4.448 names the mistake. (3) Hand-calculate first. If you expect 7.1 ksi and the study says 1.6 ksi, the factor of 4.45 is unmistakable. (4) Look at the ratios between the multiple-choice options. A well-written exam offers 7.11 ksi alongside 1.60 ksi (the 4.45 error), 14.2 ksi (per-item doubling) and similar — the distractors are built from the specific mistakes being tested, so recognising the ratio identifies both the error and the right answer. The strategic advice: work in one system throughout — MMGS is the natural choice because N/mm² = MPa with no conversion — and do the unit conversions by hand, on paper, on the way in and on the way out, where you can see and check them, rather than trusting a dropdown you did not read.

P1. How many degrees of freedom does (a) a high-quality tetrahedral solid element have, (b) a draft-quality shell element, (c) a beam element, (d) a truss element?
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) 30. A high-quality (parabolic) tetrahedron has 10 nodes — 4 corners plus 6 mid-side — and each node of a solid element has 3 translational degrees of freedom, so 10 × 3 = 30. (b) 18. A draft-quality shell is a 3-node triangle, and shell nodes carry 6 DOF each (3 translations + 3 rotations), so 3 × 6 = 18. A high-quality shell has 6 nodes and therefore 36. (c) 12. A beam element has 2 nodes with 6 DOF each. (d) 6. A truss element has 2 nodes with only the 3 translations each — the rotational DOF are released, which is what makes it carry axial load only. The pattern to remember: solid nodes have 3 DOF (translations only), shell and beam nodes have 6 (translations plus rotations), truss nodes have 3. Then it is just node count × DOF per node. The engineering consequence worth stating: because solid elements have no rotational DOF, a moment cannot be applied directly to a solid node — that is why moments on solid bodies are applied through a remote load (Page 7) — and it is also why connecting a shell edge to a solid face needs care, since the shell's rotational stiffness has nothing to attach to.
P2. A study of a steel bracket gives a maximum displacement of 0.42 mm. The same geometry and loading in aluminium (E = 69 GPa) is run. Predict the new maximum displacement and the new maximum stress, and justify both.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Displacement: 0.42 × (200/69) = 1.22 mm — roughly three times larger. Displacement in a linear elastic body scales inversely with the elastic modulus, because [K] is proportional to E and {u} = [K]−1{F}. Aluminium is about 2.9 times less stiff than steel, so it deflects about 2.9 times further under the same load. Stress: unchanged at whatever the steel study gave. This is the part that surprises people. In a statically determinate structure — one where the internal forces follow from equilibrium alone — the load path does not depend on stiffness, so the same forces flow through the same sections and produce the same stresses regardless of material. A cantilever's root bending moment is FL whether it is made of steel, aluminium or rubber. Since σ = Mc/I contains no E, the stress is identical. The exceptions, which the question is really testing. Stress does depend on E when the structure is statically indeterminate and made of more than one material, because then the load distributes itself in proportion to the relative stiffnesses — a steel insert in an aluminium housing takes a larger share of the load than its area suggests. Stress also changes with material if the loading itself depends on deformation: thermal stress (which is proportional to EαΔT), preloaded or interference fits, contact problems where the contact area changes, and any case with prescribed displacement rather than prescribed force. And of course the factor of safety changes dramatically even when the stress does not, because aluminium's yield strength is far lower — so the aluminium bracket may be perfectly adequate on stiffness grounds and quite inadequate on strength. For the exam: if the question says “same geometry, same load, different material” on a simple part, the expected answers are displacement scales by E1/E2 and stress is unchanged.
P3. An assembly of three parts is analysed with default settings and no contact sets defined. Describe precisely what the solver assumes, and give two symptoms that would reveal it.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The solver applies the global contact condition, which defaults to BONDED, to every pair of touching faces. The three parts are therefore treated as if welded or glued together at every interface: no separation, no sliding, and full transmission of tension, compression and shear across each joint. Effectively the assembly has been analysed as a single solid body. The problem is linear and solves quickly, which is exactly why the error is comfortable and easy to miss. Symptom 1 — tension across an interface that can only push. Plot the normal stress or the principal stresses across a joint that is physically just two faces in contact. If material is being pulled across that interface, bonded contact is holding it together; a real unbonded joint would open. This is the definitive diagnostic and it takes one plot. Symptom 2 — the assembly is far too stiff. The maximum displacement will be noticeably smaller than expected from a hand calculation or from experience, because joints that would in reality slip, rotate or gap are behaving as continuous material. A related tell is that the stress at the joints looks smooth and low, with no concentration around bolt holes or contact edges — because the load is flowing through the whole interface rather than through the fasteners. Two more worth knowing: if there should be bolts or pins, the study reports no connector forces, because none were defined — a silent absence rather than an error; and if the parts were modelled with a small clearance rather than touching, bonded contact may not have been applied at all and a part may be floating, producing a zero-pivot or excessive-displacement message instead. The fix is to review Connections explicitly for every interface and decide, joint by joint, whether it is genuinely bonded (welded, brazed, adhesive, interference) or should be no-penetration, adding bolt or pin connectors with preload where fasteners exist (Page 8). The habit: in any assembly study, open the Connections folder before solving and again after — the default is a decision the software made for you, and it is usually the wrong one.
P4. A pressure vessel wall is 6 mm thick with an internal radius of 500 mm and 1.2 MPa internal pressure. (a) Compute the hoop and longitudinal stresses. (b) What element type should be used and why? (c) What von Mises stress would you expect?
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) The stresses. Check the thin-wall assumption first: r/t = 500/6 = 83, comfortably above the usual threshold of 10, so thin-wall theory applies. Hoop (circumferential) stress σh = pr/t = 1.2 × 500/6 = 100 MPa. Longitudinal (axial) stress σl = pr/2t = 50 MPa. The radial stress varies from −1.2 MPa at the bore to 0 at the outside and is negligible by comparison — which is precisely the thin-wall approximation. Note the factor of two: hoop stress is always twice longitudinal in a cylinder, which is why cylindrical vessels split along their length rather than around their circumference, and why the longitudinal weld is the critical one. (b) Element type: SHELL. With a 6 mm wall on a 1 m diameter vessel, one dimension is two orders of magnitude smaller than the others. Meshing with solids would need at least two or three elements through the 6 mm thickness, forcing an element size around 2 mm over a surface several square metres in area — millions of elements for no benefit. Shell elements carry the thickness as a property and represent the membrane and bending behaviour directly, solving in seconds. In SOLIDWORKS a surface body or a sheet-metal body meshes with shells by default; confirm the thickness in the shell definition and check the orientation so that top and bottom surface stresses are not confused. (c) The expected von Mises stress. Taking the three principal stresses as 100, 50 and approximately 0: σvM = √{½[(100−50)² + (50−0)² + (0−100)²]} = √{½[2 500 + 2 500 + 10 000]} = √7 500 = 86.6 MPa. Note that this is below the hoop stress — biaxial tension is less severe than uniaxial tension of the same magnitude, because the second tensile stress reduces the shear distortion. That is a useful sanity check on any pressure-vessel FEA: the von Mises should come out around 0.87 of the hoop stress in the plain cylindrical section. What FEA adds beyond the hand calculation: the discontinuity stresses at the ends, where the head or the flat closure restrains the shell's free radial growth and produces local bending that can substantially exceed the membrane stress — and at nozzles and supports. Those are exactly the places thin-wall theory cannot reach, and they are why the vessel is analysed rather than merely calculated.
P5. A candidate applies a 900 N force by selecting three faces and leaving the dialog on “per item”. What total load is applied, what will the reaction force show, and how would you correct it two ways?
Tap to reveal ↓A: 2 700 N is applied — three times the intended load. The “per item” setting means the value entered is applied to each selected entity, so three faces receive 900 N each. The stresses and displacements will all be exactly three times too large, and because linearity means everything scales together, the result looks entirely self-consistent: the deformed shape is right, the stress distribution is right, only the magnitudes are wrong. Nothing in the plot reveals it. What the reaction check shows. Result → List Result Force at the fixtures will report a total reaction of 2 700 N against the 900 N you intended. The ratio is exactly 3 — a clean whole number — and whole-number ratios in a reaction check almost always mean a per-item/total error or a load applied to more entities than intended. (Compare: a ratio of 4.448 means lbf/N, and 1 000 or 10⁶ means a length or stress unit mismatch.) This is why the reaction check is the first item on the verification ladder: it takes ten seconds and catches the entire class of input-magnitude errors at once. Correction, method 1 — change the setting. Edit the force, select the same three faces, and switch the option to Total. SOLIDWORKS then distributes 900 N across the combined area of the three faces in proportion to area. This is the right choice when the 900 N is a single physical load spread over a region — a pad, a bearing surface, a contact patch. Correction, method 2 — change the number. Leave it on “per item” and enter 300 N, so each face receives 300 N and the total is 900 N. This gives an equal load on each face rather than an area-weighted distribution, which is the right choice when the three faces represent three separate, nominally identical load introductions — three bolt pads, three feet, three rollers. The distinction matters if the faces differ in area: “Total” weights by area, “per item” does not, and the two give different stress distributions even when the resultant is the same. For equal faces they are identical. The exam-ready habit: after applying any load to more than one entity, read the dialog's per-item/total toggle deliberately, and then confirm with the reaction check before reading any result.
P6. A mesh convergence study gives 210, 268, 341, 434 MPa at four successively halved element sizes. State the diagnosis, the numerical evidence, and the correct action.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Diagnosis: a singularity. There is no converged answer and no amount of refinement will produce one. The numerical evidence. The increments are 58, 73 and 93 MPa — they are growing, not shrinking. As percentages of the preceding value they are 27.6 %, 27.2 % and 27.3 %, i.e. essentially constant. A constant fractional increase per halving of element size is the definitive signature: the stress is following a power law in element size with no finite limit. Computing the increment ratio r = 73/93 = 0.78, which is less than 1, confirms it — Richardson extrapolation requires r > 1 and returns nonsense here, and that failure is the diagnosis rather than a problem with the method. Extrapolating the pattern, the next refinement would give about 553 MPa, the one after about 704, and so on without limit. The correct action, in order. (1) Identify the site. It will be one of four things: a sharp re-entrant corner with zero radius, a point or edge load, the boundary of a fixed-geometry fixture, or the edge of a bonded contact region. Look at the plot and find which. (2) Fix the model, not the mesh. If it is a corner, add the fillet that the real manufactured part has — even 0.5 mm removes the singularity, because a finite radius gives a finite stress. If it is a point load, spread it over the true contact area or use a bearing or remote load. If it is a fixture edge, restrain what is physically restrained, or use no-penetration contact against a supporting body, or an elastic support. (3) Re-run the convergence study on the corrected model and demonstrate genuine convergence with shrinking increments. (4) If the singularity is remote from the region of interest, invoke Saint-Venant's principle: report the stress at least one characteristic dimension away, and state in the report that the local peak is a modelling artefact. What NOT to do: do not report 434 MPa, do not report “the finest mesh I could solve”, and do not refine further hoping it will settle. Also note the trap in the numbers: 434 MPa would exceed the yield strength of most structural steels, so a candidate might additionally conclude “the part fails” — compounding one error with another, since a linear result above yield is outside the model's validity anyway (Page 2).
P7. Which study type would you use to answer each: (a) will this shelf bracket sag more than 2 mm? (b) will this machine cover rattle at 50 Hz? (c) will this jack screw collapse sideways? (d) will this spring clip survive 10 million cycles? (e) will this snap-fit assemble without breaking?
Tap to reveal ↓A: (a) Static. A steady load and a stiffness question — exactly what linear static analysis is for. Read the resultant displacement (URES) and compare with the 2 mm criterion. Confirm the deflection is small relative to the geometry so the linear assumption holds, and check the reaction forces. (b) Frequency (modal). “Rattle at 50 Hz” is a resonance question, and static analysis has no concept of time or inertia. A frequency study returns the natural frequencies and mode shapes; it needs mass density in the material definition and requires no loads at all. The design rule is to keep natural frequencies away from the excitation, typically by a factor of at least 1.5 to 2 — so for a 50 Hz excitation you want the first mode below about 33 Hz or above about 75 Hz. If it is close, the mode shape tells you where to add stiffness or a support. (c) Buckling. A slender member in compression fails by instability at a load that may be far below yield, and a static study will report a comfortable stress right through the buckling load (Page 14). A buckling study returns the buckling load factor — the multiplier on the applied load at which instability occurs. Require a substantial margin (commonly 2 to 4), because the linear Euler prediction is optimistic relative to real members with initial crookedness and eccentric load. Hand-check with Pcr = π²EI/(KL)². (d) Fatigue. Failure under repeated loading occurs at stresses well below yield, so a static factor of safety says nothing about life. A fatigue study needs an S-N curve for the material and builds on one or more completed static studies to define the load cycle. Note that fatigue is driven by the alternating stress and is sensitive to stress concentrations and surface finish — so the fillet you were tempted to defeature (Page 4) is now the whole answer. (e) Non-linear. A snap-fit undergoes large deflection, its stiffness changes as it deforms, it involves contact whose area evolves, and the material may yield locally — three of the five linear-static assumptions broken at once. This requires a non-linear study (SOLIDWORKS Simulation Premium) and is outside CSWA-S scope. The exam-ready mapping: time, inertia or vibration → frequency or dynamic; slender member in compression → buckling; repeated loading → fatigue; large deflection, plasticity or evolving contact → non-linear; everything else steady → static.
P8. A part is fixed at one end and loaded at the other. The maximum von Mises stress reported is 512 MPa, located on the edge of the fixed face. The material is 6061-T6 aluminium (yield ~275 MPa). Work through the interpretation.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Two independent problems are present, and both must be addressed. Problem 1 — the location says singularity. The peak is on the edge of a fixed-geometry face, which is one of the four classic singularity sites (Page 10). At that edge the model transitions from infinitely stiff to completely free over zero distance, producing a stress that rises without limit as the mesh is refined. The 512 MPa is therefore almost certainly not a physical stress at all but an artefact of the boundary condition. Test it: refine the mesh locally at that edge two or three times. If the value keeps climbing with non-shrinking increments, it is confirmed as a singularity. Problem 2 — the value exceeds yield, so the model is outside its own validity. A linear static study extrapolates Hooke's law indefinitely; at 512 MPa in a material that yields at 275 MPa, the assumption of linear elasticity has been broken and the number is not a prediction of anything (Page 2). Even if the peak were real, you could not quote it. The interpretation to write down. “The reported peak of 512 MPa occurs at the boundary of the fixed face and is a boundary-condition singularity; it does not converge under mesh refinement and cannot be quoted. In addition it exceeds the material's yield strength, so the linear elastic assumption does not hold at that location.” That sentence is the professional answer and, on an exam, is very often what is being tested. What to do next. (1) Improve the restraint model. Fix what is physically fixed — the bolt footprints rather than a whole face — and consider a roller/slider on the remainder so the part can lift away, or no-penetration contact against a supporting body, or an elastic support with a realistic stiffness (Page 6). Any of these spreads the transition over a finite distance and removes the artificial peak. (2) Report the stress in the body of the part, at least one characteristic dimension away from the restraint, where Saint-Venant's principle guarantees the field is correct. That value is what should be compared with the 275 MPa yield. (3) Hand-check the bending stress from the overall geometry to confirm the body stress is plausible (Page 11). (4) If, after all this, a real stress still exceeds yield, then the design or the load is genuinely inadequate and the answer is to change the part — more section, a larger fillet, a different alloy — or to run a non-linear study that can represent yielding. The general lesson: always ask where the peak is before asking how big it is. The location often invalidates the magnitude.
P9. A study must be run on a symmetric part with symmetric loading. Explain what symmetry buys, what conditions must hold, and give a case where a geometrically symmetric part must still be modelled in full.
Tap to reveal ↓A: What it buys. Modelling half the part halves the degrees of freedom, and because direct-solver cost grows faster than linearly with DOF, the solve is usually rather more than twice as fast; memory demand falls correspondingly, which can be the difference between a model that fits and one that does not. Quartering with two planes of symmetry compounds the saving. Equally valuable, the saved capacity can be spent on a finer mesh in the region of interest, so symmetry often buys accuracy rather than merely speed. The conditions — all four must hold about the same plane. (1) The geometry is symmetric. (2) The material is symmetric. (3) The loading is symmetric. (4) The fixtures are symmetric. Candidates check the first and forget the other three, which is the whole content of this question. On the cut face you apply a symmetry fixture, which restrains translation normal to the plane (and, on shells, the two out-of-plane rotations) — physically asserting that material on the cut plane cannot move across it, which is exactly what the missing half would enforce. The case where symmetry must not be used. A geometrically symmetric part under anti-symmetric loading, most commonly torsion or a lateral bending load applied out of the symmetry plane. Consider a symmetric shaft loaded in torsion about its axis: the geometry is perfectly symmetric about a longitudinal plane, but the loading is not — material on one side rotates one way and on the other side the opposite way. A symmetry fixture would prevent exactly the deformation that occurs, and the model would return a stiff, low-stress, entirely wrong answer with no warning at all. The same applies to any load with a component that reverses across the plane. Two further cases worth knowing: buckling and frequency studies on symmetric parts, where a symmetric model can only find symmetric modes — and the critical mode is often anti-symmetric, so the study silently misses the lowest buckling load or the first natural frequency. That is a genuinely dangerous omission and the reason many analysts refuse to use symmetry for stability and modal work. And cyclic symmetry (impellers, flanges, discs) needs the circular-symmetry fixture rather than a plane symmetry, with the same caveat about non-symmetric modes. The rule to state in the exam: symmetry is valid only when geometry, material, loading and restraints are all symmetric about the same plane — and never assume it for torsion, for anti-symmetric loading, or for buckling and frequency studies.
P10 — Synthesis. A steel lifting lug, 12 mm thick, with a 25 mm pin hole 40 mm from a welded base, must carry a 15 kN vertical load through a pin. Yield 250 MPa; a factor of safety of at least 2 is required. Design the complete study: elements, fixtures, loads, contacts, mesh, verification and reporting. Then state your expected answer before running it.
Tap to reveal ↓A:
1. Hand calculation first — always, and especially here. Two failure modes must be checked before any software is opened. Bearing at the pin: projected area = d × t = 25 × 12 = 300 mm², so mean bearing stress = 15 000/300 = 50 MPa; the sinusoidal peak is roughly 4/π higher, about 64 MPa. Tension across the net section above the hole: if the lug is, say, 70 mm wide, the net area each side of the hole is ((70 − 25)/2) × 12 = 270 mm² per side, 540 mm² total, giving σnom ≈ 15 000/540 = 27.8 MPa; with a stress concentration around the hole of Kt ≈ 2.5 (Page 12) the peak is roughly 70 MPa. Bending at the weld: the 15 kN acts 40 mm from the base, so M = 600 000 N·mm; with a 70 × 12 mm section, Z = 12 × 70²/6 = 9 800 mm³, giving σ = 61 MPa. So I expect a peak somewhere in the 60–90 MPa range and a factor of safety around 3 — comfortably above the required 2. Writing this down before running is what lets me recognise a wrong answer. 2. Element type: solid, high quality. All three dimensions are comparable (12 mm thick, tens of millimetres in plan), so shells are not appropriate. Parabolic tetrahedra by default. 3. Fixtures. The lug is welded to a base, so Fixed Geometry on the welded face is defensible — a weld genuinely does restrain all degrees of freedom. But I expect a stress singularity at the edge of that face (Page 10), so I will report the stress in the body of the lug and at the hole, not at the fixture boundary, and I will model the weld fillet radius if the geometry has one. If the base plate is flexible, a better model includes it with bonded contact at the weld. 4. Load: bearing load on the hole, 15 kN vertical — not a uniform force on the whole bore, which would load the half the pin pulls away from (Page 7). If the exam requires the pin to be modelled, use no-penetration contact between pin and bore, or a pin connector between two lugs, and accept the non-linear solve. 5. Contacts: none needed for a single-body lug; if the base plate is included, bonded at the weld, and no-penetration between pin and hole if the pin is modelled. 6. Mesh. Curvature-based, high quality. Global size around 4 mm, with a local mesh control of about 2.5 mm on the hole face to give 12–16 elements around the circumference (radius/4), and a similar control on the weld fillet. Check aspect ratio and Jacobian at the hole and the fillet, since that is where the answer is. 7. Verification, in order. Reaction force at the fixture must equal 15 kN vertically, with negligible horizontal components. Deformed shape at 1:1 must show the lug bending about the base, with no rigid-body drift. Maximum displacement should be a few hundredths of a millimetre — anything in metres or nanometres is a unit error. Peak stress should be at the hole sides or the base fillet, both physically sensible; if it is on the fixed-face boundary, that is a singularity. Compare with my 60–90 MPa expectation. 8. Convergence. Refine the hole and fillet controls twice more (1.25 mm, 0.6 mm) and confirm the increments shrink to under about 2 %; report the converged value with that evidence. 9. Reporting. Peak von Mises with its location and the converged mesh stated; factor of safety = 250/σpeak against the required 2; the bearing stress at the pin against the material's bearing allowance; and a note that the weld itself requires a separate weld-strength check, since FEA of the parent metal says nothing about weld quality. 10. What is outside this study. A lifting lug is safety-critical and cyclically loaded, so a real design would also require a fatigue assessment, a shock/dynamic factor on the lifting load (commonly 1.5–2×), proof-load testing, and compliance with the applicable lifting-equipment standard. Saying so is part of the engineering answer even though none of it is in the FEA.

Derivations & Traps

Four results to reproduce from memory, and the seven mistakes that cost the most marks.

DERIVATION 1 · THE VON MISES CRITERION FROM DISTORTION ENERGY
The physical premise. Ductile metals yield by shear-driven plastic flow. Hydrostatic pressure changes VOLUME but not SHAPE, and experiment shows it does not cause yielding. So the criterion must depend only on the part of the stress state that changes shape. Split the stress into two parts. hydrostatic (mean) σᵐ = (σ₁ + σ₂ + σ₃) / 3 deviatoric sᵢ = σᵢ − σᵐ (note s₁ + s₂ + s₃ = 0) Total strain energy per unit volume for principal stresses: U = (1/2E)[ σ₁² + σ₂² + σ₃² − 2ν(σ₁σ₂ + σ₂σ₃ + σ₃σ₁) ] Volumetric part (from the hydrostatic component alone): Uᵛ = (3(1−2ν)/2E) σᵐ² = ((1−2ν)/6E)(σ₁+σ₂+σ₃)² Distortion energy = total − volumetric. Subtracting and collecting: Uᵈ = ((1+ν)/6E) [ (σ₁−σ₂)² + (σ₂−σ₃)² + (σ₃−σ₁)² ] Calibrate against a tensile test. At yield in uniaxial tension, σ₁ = σy, σ₂ = σ₃ = 0, so Uᵈcrit = ((1+ν)/6E) · 2σy² Yield occurs when Uᵈ reaches Uᵈcrit: (σ₁−σ₂)² + (σ₂−σ₃)² + (σ₃−σ₁)² = 2σy² Defining the von Mises equivalent stress as the σ that would produce the same distortion energy in uniaxial tension: σvM = √( ½[ (σ₁−σ₂)² + (σ₂−σ₃)² + (σ₃−σ₁)² ] ) ∎ Three checks that fall straight out uniaxial σ,0,0 → √(½[σ²+0+σ²]) = σ ✓ (so it is directly comparable with σy) hydrostatic p,p,p → all differences zero → 0 ✓ pure shear τ,0,−τ → √(½[τ²+τ²+4τ²]) = √3 τ so yielding in shear at τy = σy/√3 = 0.577σy, against Tresca's 0.5σy — the 2/√3 = 1.155 ratio.
DERIVATION 2 · THE BENDING FORMULA σ = Mc / I
Assumptions. Plane sections remain plane and normal to the neutral axis; material is linear elastic; deflections are small; loading is in a plane of symmetry. Step 1 — geometry of deformation. A beam bent to radius R: a fibre at distance y from the neutral axis stretches from length Rθ to (R+y)θ, so ε = yθ / (Rθ) = y / R strain varies LINEARLY with y Step 2 — Hooke's law. σ = Eε = E y / R stress also linear in y Step 3 — equilibrium of forces. No net axial force: ∫ σ dA = (E/R) ∫ y dA = 0 ⇒ ∫ y dA = 0 i.e. the neutral axis passes through the CENTROID of the section. Step 4 — equilibrium of moments. M = ∫ y σ dA = (E/R) ∫ y² dA = (E/R) I where I = ∫ y² dA is the second moment of area. Hence M / I = E / R = σ / y the engineer's bending equation Step 5 — maximum stress at the extreme fibre y = c: σmax = M c / I = M / Z, Z = I / c Why this matters for FEA. Everything here is a hand-check you can do in thirty seconds, and every assumption is one that FEA does NOT make — which is exactly why the two disagree near supports, near loads, at section changes and for short deep beams. The DIFFERENCE between the hand value and the FEA is engineering information, not an error. Irect = bh³/12 Icirc = πd⁴/64 Itube = π(D⁴−d⁴)/64 ⇒ depth is CUBED: doubling h multiplies I by 8 and Z by 4.
DERIVATION 3 · WHY PARABOLIC ELEMENTS BEAT LINEAR ONES
Setup. Within an element, displacement is interpolated from nodal values by shape functions Nᵢ: u(x) = Σ Nᵢ(x) uᵢ Linear (draft) element. Two nodes along an edge → N linear → u(x) = a + bx ε = du/dx = b — CONSTANT strain within the element σ = Eε = constant — constant stress too Parabolic (high quality) element. Three nodes along an edge (two corner + one mid-side) → N quadratic → u(x) = a + bx + cx² ε = du/dx = b + 2cx — strain varies LINEARLY σ = Eε — stress varies linearly Consequence 1 — bending. In pure bending, strain varies linearly across the depth. A linear element CANNOT represent that with one element through the thickness: it must approximate bending by shearing, producing spurious shear strain energy. The element therefore appears much too stiff. This is shear locking, and it under-predicts both displacement and stress — an unsafe error. Consequence 2 — geometry. Mid-side nodes let element edges CURVE, so a hole or fillet is followed accurately instead of being faceted. Faceting introduces its own artificial stress concentrations. Consequence 3 — convergence rate. With element size h, the error in stress falls roughly as linear elements e ∝ h¹ parabolic elements e ∝ h² So halving the element size roughly halves the error with linear elements and QUARTERS it with parabolic ones. Parabolic elements cost about 2.5× the DOF per element and buy far more than 2.5× the accuracy. ⇒ use high quality by default; use draft only for a first look.
DERIVATION 4 · RICHARDSON EXTRAPOLATION & THE CONVERGENCE TEST
Setup. For a convergent scheme the computed stress at element size h differs from the exact value by a power-law error term: σ(h) = σ∞ − C hp C, p constants; p = observed order Take three results at h, h/2, h/4: σ₁ = σ∞ − C hp σ₂ = σ∞ − C (h/2)p = σ∞ − C hp 2−p σ₃ = σ∞ − C hp 4−p Form the increments: σ₂ − σ₁ = C hp (1 − 2−p) σ₃ − σ₂ = C hp 2−p (1 − 2−p) Their ratio eliminates C and h: r = (σ₂ − σ₁) / (σ₃ − σ₂) = 2p ⇒ p = log₂ r And the exact value follows: σ∞ = σ₃ + (σ₃ − σ₂) / (r − 1) ∎ The diagnostic power of r r > 1 increments shrinking → CONVERGING; σ∞ is meaningful r ≈ 1 increments constant → no convergence r < 1 increments growing → DIVERGING — a SINGULARITY; the formula returns nonsense, and that IS the answer Worked (WE3): 96.2, 104.8, 108.1, 109.3 increments 8.6, 3.3, 1.2 → r = 3.3/1.2 = 2.75 → p = 1.46 σ∞ = 109.3 + 1.2/1.75 = 110.0 MPa, and the finest mesh is already within 0.6 % of it.
SEVEN TRAPS
  • 1Units. 250 lbf typed into a newton field is 4.448× too small; metres with newtons gives stresses 106 out; an answer wanted in psi and given in MPa is simply wrong. The plot never warns you. Read the dropdown before typing, and read the legend before reporting.
  • 2Per item vs total. Three faces selected with “per item” applies three times the load. The reaction-force check catches it in ten seconds, and a clean whole-number ratio is the signature.
  • 3A linear result above yield is not a failure prediction. The solver extrapolated Hooke's law past the material's validity. The correct statement is that the assumption has been violated — not that the part breaks.
  • 4von Mises cannot tell tension from compression. It is a scalar built from differences. For brittle materials — cast iron, ceramics, concrete — plot the 1st principal stress against the ultimate strength instead.
  • 5Default bonded contact welds your assembly together. The tell is tension across a joint that can only push. Bonded is stiffer, hides the fastener load path, and is optimistic.
  • 6Refinement that keeps raising the stress is not convergence. Growing or constant increments mean a singularity — a sharp corner, a point load, a fixture edge, a bonded-contact edge. No mesh will ever fix it; the geometry or boundary condition must change.
  • 7Static analysis is blind to four failure modes: buckling, resonance, fatigue and post-yield collapse. A comfortable factor of safety says nothing about any of them, and a slender member in compression is the dangerous case because the static result looks perfectly reassuring.

Two honourable mentions. Symmetry with anti-symmetric loading — a symmetric part in torsion, or a buckling or frequency study on a symmetric model, will silently suppress exactly the mode that matters. And changing the yield strength does not change the stress: in a linear static study only E and ν affect the solution, so editing the yield alters the factor-of-safety plot and nothing else — a favourite discriminator in multiple-choice questions.
