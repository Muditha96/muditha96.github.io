Cold Open — Four Loops, and Nobody Trusted Any of Them

A KNIT-FABRIC DYEING AND FINISHING PLANT, SEETHAWAKA CORE

165,000 kg of knitted fabric a month. Fabric arrives grey, is dyed in pressurised jet machines, is squeezed, dried, and finally heat-set in a stenter — a 24 m gas-fired oven that pulls the fabric through on pins at 18 to 32 m/min and fixes its final width and shrinkage. Four automatic control loops decide whether that fabric is sellable. On the day this story starts, three of the four were in manual and the fourth had a setpoint deliberately set 6 °C above the recipe.

Nothing was broken. Every instrument read. Every valve stroked. Every controller executed its algorithm exactly as written. The plant was losing LKR 2,106,990 every month and had been for seven months — LKR 14.75 million — because four different, entirely ordinary control-loop misunderstandings were each doing precisely what the textbook says they will do.

💼

This is the most valuable page in the primer for your career, and it is deliberately the first. Three of these four faults are found by looking at a trend for ninety seconds. None of them needs a differential equation. An engineer who can walk a plant and read four trends is immediately, visibly useful — long before he can derive a transfer function.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

The stenter's zone-3 temperature controller has proportional action only — its integral time was left at 9999 s (effectively off) after a controller upgrade. It sits perfectly steady, but 5.9 °C below setpoint whenever the line runs fast, and exactly on setpoint when the line runs slow. What is wrong with it?

Nothing is faulty. This is the defining behaviour of proportional-only control, and it has a name: offset. A pure proportional controller computes its output from the error and nothing else. If the error is zero, its contribution is zero. So the only way it can hold the burner at the 62 % it needs at high line speed is to sit at an error that produces 62 %. Remove the error and you remove the output. The gap is not a symptom — it is the mechanism.
stenter zone 3, measured by step test on page 12: process gain K = 1.45 %PV per %OP span 0-250 °C proportional band PB = 25 % → controller gain Kc = 100/25 = 4.0 extra burner demand needed at 32 m/min vs 18 m/min: ΔOP = 11.0 % exact steady-state offset (derived properly on page 18): offset = K · ΔOP / (1 + K · Kc) = 1.45 × 11.0 / (1 + 5.80) = 15.95 / 6.80 = 2.3456 % of span = 5.86 °C the rule of thumb everyone quotes, ΔOP/Kc = 11.0/4.0 = 2.75 % = 6.88 °C, over-estimates it by (1 + K·Kc)/(K·Kc) = 1.172× — see page 7.
Option (2) is the trap that catches most people, and it is half right: raising Kc genuinely does shrink the offset. But this loop's ultimate gain — the gain at which it begins to oscillate forever — is Ku = 5.0. It is already running at 80 % of the gain that destabilises it. There is no gain left to spend. The correct answer is not more proportional; it is a different mode, and page 8 introduces it.
THE FOUR FAULTS, AS THEY LOOKED ON THE MORNING SHIFT CORE
⚠ D1 — STENTER ZONE 3 · “it always runs a bit cold”

Proportional-only after a controller migration. Steady, silent, 5.9 °C low at 32 m/min and on setpoint at 18 m/min. Operators compensated by entering a setpoint 6 °C above the recipe — which then over-sets the fabric on slow orders. Residual shrinkage out of spec on 2.1 % of output → 3,465 kg/month reprocessed at LKR 92/kg = LKR 318,780/month.

⚠ D2 — DYE VESSEL 4 · “it overshoots every batch”

Heat-up to the 130 °C fixation hold. The steam valve sits saturated at 100 % for 90 minutes — and 25 of those minutes happen after the liquor has already passed 130 °C, because anti-windup was never enabled. Every batch overshoots by 17.9 °C and spends 110 min above setpoint. Reactive dyes fix irreversibly during that excursion → shade variation, 14 re-dyes/month at LKR 58,000 = LKR 812,000/month.

⚠ D3 — JET DYER 4 LIQUOR FLOW · “it cannot be tuned”

A steady cycle, 6.1 % of span peak-to-peak, period 1.32 min, with a square-shouldered PV and a sawtooth OP. Three engineers detuned it in turn, ending at a gain of 0.25. The cycle got slower and slightly bigger (3.5 min, 6.2 %). Cause: a valve repacked after a leak, packing over-tightened, 6.2 % stiction band. Cost: uneven levelling, +8 min per batch × 420 batches = 56 h/month at LKR 12,500/h = LKR 700,000/month.

⚠ D4 — LEVELLING-AGENT DOSING · “the process is just slow”

Sensor mounted 14 m downstream of injection in a 100 mm line at 0.9 m/s (15.6 s of transport), plus a 30 s filter added to “smooth the reading”, plus a 2 s controller task contributing 1 s. Total dead time 46.6 s against a 40 s process — θ/τ = 1.16, dead-time dominant. 92.8 % of that dead time was installed by engineers, not by physics. Operators added a 9 % chemical safety margin: LKR 1.674/kg × 165,000 = LKR 276,210/month.

Read that list again and notice what it does not contain. No failed instrument. No burnt-out valve. No PLC fault. No network problem. Four correct devices, four correct programs, and four engineers who did not know what the controller in front of them was actually doing. What Transfers — You Have Been Doing Control Theory Since Year One

THE WATT GOVERNOR IS A COMPLETE PID LOOP, IN BRASS CORE

Two flyballs on a spindle geared to the engine shaft. Speed up, centrifugal force flings them out, the sleeve rises, the linkage closes the steam throttle, the engine slows. It is a closed loop with a measurement, a comparison, a controller, a final element and a process — and it was doing this a century before anybody could write down what it was doing.

Every single box in a modern control loop has a brass ancestor here. Learn the ancestor first; the modern name will then feel like a translation rather than a new idea.

And the governor has the defect too. A Watt governor always runs the engine slower under load than at no load — because the balls must sit lower to hold the throttle open, and sitting lower is a lower speed. Steam engineers called it droop. Instrument engineers call it offset. It is fault D1, in 1788. The nineteenth-century cure was a second mechanism that slowly wound the whole spring assembly — an isochronous governor. That mechanism is integral action, and it is page 8.

THE THREE THINGS THAT DO NOT TRANSFER CORE
  • 1
    A mechanical governor is instantaneous; a plant loop is not. The flyballs know the shaft speed now. A thermocouple in a thermowell knows the temperature of ninety seconds ago, and a sample point 14 m downstream knows the concentration of sixteen seconds ago. Every delay between the valve moving and the measurement noticing is stored-up trouble, and page 5 is entirely about it. It is the reason control is hard and mechanism design is not.
  • 2
    You can change the controller with a keyboard, which makes it the first thing everyone blames. The valve costs money to fix, the piping costs a shutdown to change, the sensor position needs a welder. Retuning is free. So four times out of five the free thing gets adjusted and the actual fault survives. Three of the four faults in this plant were “fixed” by retuning at least once.
  • 3
    Feedback can make things worse. A stiffer spring on a valve always makes it stiffer. A higher gain on a controller makes the loop faster, then makes it oscillate, then makes it destroy the machine. There is a number — the ultimate gain — beyond which more effort produces less control. Nothing in statics prepares you for a parameter that helps up to a point and then reverses sign.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You are driving at a steady 60 km/h and the road tilts uphill. You press the accelerator further and hold 60 km/h exactly. Which control mode is your foot performing?

Integral — and this is the cleanest intuition for it that exists. The defining question is: what is your throttle position when the error is zero? A proportional foot would have to keep a permanent speed error to keep the extra throttle; it would settle at, say, 57 km/h and stay there. Your foot does not. It arrives at zero error and a new, larger throttle opening — which means the output no longer depends on the present error at all. It depends on the history of the error. That is exactly what integral action is: an output that accumulates while error persists and holds its value when error disappears.
the three instincts, in one line each: P “how far off am I now?” acts on the present I “how long have I been off, and by how much?” acts on the past D “how fast am I heading off?” acts on the projected future Your foot does all three. Coming up to a red light you lift off before the light is even close (D). Going up the hill you push harder and stay there (I). Overtaking, you push in proportion to the gap (P).
You also do the fourth thing that no PID controller does unaided: you look out of the windscreen and see the hill coming. That is feedforward, and it is page 4.

The Vocabulary — Six Words, Used Precisely, For Ever

THE SIX WORDS CORE

Instrument engineers are ruthless about these six. Use them loosely in a commissioning meeting and you will be corrected within thirty seconds — not out of pedantry, but because the entire diagnosis of a misbehaving loop is a question of which of these six is wrong.

SP — Setpoint

The value you want, in engineering units. Entered by an operator, by a recipe, or by another controller (cascade, page 14).

PV — Process Variable

What the sensor says is happening, in engineering units. Also called the controlled variable, CV. Never the same thing as the truth — see B04.

e — Error

e = SP − PV. Always that way round in this primer and in ISA usage. Sign matters: positive error means the PV is below where you want it.

MV / OP — Manipulated Variable, Output

The single quantity the controller is allowed to move, 0–100 %. Usually valve demand, drive speed reference or heater duty.

FCE — Final Control Element

The physical thing that converts OP into an effect: valve + actuator + positioner, VFD + motor, SCR + heater. Half the loop lives here — page 13.

DV — Disturbance Variable

Anything else that moves the PV and that you are not allowed to touch: feed rate, ambient temperature, raw-material change, line speed. The load.

Say them as a sentence and they stop being jargon: “The controller compares the SP with the PV, and moves its OP to the final control element so that the error goes to zero despite the disturbances.” Everything else in control — every standard, every algorithm, every tuning rule, all of A10, A57 and A58 — is a refinement of that sentence.
THE ONE THAT IS ALWAYS ASKED IN INTERVIEWS: WHICH VARIABLE IS “MANIPULATED”? CORE

A loop is named after what it controls, not what it moves. A “level control loop” controls level (the CV/PV) by manipulating a valve on the outlet flow (the MV). Get this wrong on a drawing and you will design a loop that controls the wrong thing.

CONTROLLER ACTION — DIRECT AND REVERSE, AND WHY IT IS ALWAYS THE FIRST CHECK CORE

The controller must know which way to push. This is a single configuration bit, and getting it wrong does not produce a slightly worse loop — it produces a loop that drives itself hard against a limit and stays there.

The memory hook that never fails: Ask ONE question: “If I open the valve, does the PV go up or down?” valve open → PV UP (heating, filling) the controller must be REVERSE valve open → PV DOWN (cooling, draining) the controller must be DIRECT Note the deliberate awkwardness: a HEATING loop needs a REVERSE controller. Action and valve fail-mode must be decided together — never one without the other. Page 11.
💼

On a commissioning job, controller action is the first thing you check and the last thing anyone documents. Two minutes with a P&ID and a valve nameplate, before the loop is ever put in auto, saves the afternoon where the tank overflows and everyone blames the tuning.

TWO MORE WORDS YOU WILL MEET ON DAY ONE STRETCH

Bias / manual reset

The output the controller produces when the error is zero. On a P-only controller it is a number you set by hand (hence “manual reset”) and it is what integral action later replaces — automatically and continuously. Page 7.

Range vs span

Range is 0–250 °C; span is the 250 °C width. Every tuning constant a controller holds is dimensionless — % of output per % of span — which is why changing a transmitter's range silently re-tunes the loop. Page 11.

That last point deserves a sentence of its own, because it is one of the most common real faults in industry and it is invisible: if the transmitter is re-ranged from 0–250 °C to 0–400 °C, one degree is now a smaller percentage of span, so the controller sees a smaller error for the same physical deviation, so the effective loop gain falls by 250/400 = 0.625 without a single tuning parameter having been touched. The loop becomes sluggish and nobody can say why.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A cooling-water valve controls a reactor's temperature. The valve is air-to-open. During a shutdown it is replaced with an air-to-close valve of the same size, because that is what stores had. Nothing else is changed. What happens when the loop is put back in auto?

The sign of the loop is inverted, and feedback that was negative becomes positive. The controller is direct acting (cooling): PV rises → OP rises. With the original air-to-open valve, more OP meant more cooling water and the temperature came down. With the air-to-close valve, more OP now closes the valve. So temperature rises → OP rises → cooling stops → temperature rises further → OP goes to 100 % → valve fully shut.
the loop sign is a PRODUCT of three signs, and it must come out negative: sign(controller action) × sign(valve action) × sign(process) = NEGATIVE before: direct (+) × air-to-open (+) × cooling (−) = − stable after: direct (+) × air-to-close (−) × cooling (−) = + runaway Two of the three signs changed sign? Fine. One? Disaster. This is why swapping a valve for “the same size one in stores” is a change that requires a management-of-change review, not a storeman's judgement.
Option (3) is a genuine and important distinction: the new valve does fail safe in a different direction — on air failure it now opens fully and floods the reactor with cooling water, which for a cooling service is usually the correct fail state. The valve is arguably better. It is the untouched controller-action bit that is wrong. Fail-action and controller action are one decision, made together.

Open Loop, Closed Loop, Feedforward — What Feedback Buys and What It Costs

THREE WAYS TO GET A PROCESS TO A NUMBER CORE

Open loop hopes. Closed loop reacts. Feedforward anticipates. Real plants use the second and the third together, with the second trimming the third.

WHAT CLOSING THE LOOP ACTUALLY BUYS YOU CORE

Disturbance rejection

1 / (1+K·Kc)

A disturbance that would move the PV by 100 units moves it by 1/(1+K·Kc) of that, at steady state, under P control. This is the value of feedback, in one fraction.

Insensitivity to the process

The plant can change — a fouled heat exchanger, a worn pump — and the loop absorbs it. Open loop cannot.

Zero offset (with I)

Integral action drives the steady-state error to exactly zero, permanently, for any constant disturbance. No open-loop scheme can promise that.

D1's loop, numerically: K = 1.45, Kc = 4.0 without control, that 11 % load change moves the PV by K × ΔOP = 1.45 × 11.0 = 15.95 % of span = 39.9 °C with the P-only controller closed: 15.95 / (1 + 1.45×4.0) = 15.95 / 6.80 = 2.35 % = 5.86 °C The loop is already doing 85 % of the job. The famous “fault” is the 15 % it cannot do. Keep that proportion in your head — it is why plants run for years on badly configured loops without anyone noticing.
AND WHAT IT COSTS — THE THREE PRICES OF FEEDBACK STRETCH
  • 1
    The error must happen first. Feedback is definitionally reactive. The line speed changes, the temperature falls, then the controller responds. Everything a feedback loop does is late by at least the process's own dead time. This is not a tuning deficiency — it is arithmetic, and no controller ever built escapes it.
  • 2
    It can be unstable. Push the gain far enough and the correction arrives out of phase with the error and adds to it. An open-loop system cannot oscillate; a closed-loop one can, and can destroy machinery doing it. Every tuning rule that exists is a way of buying performance while staying a defined distance from that cliff.
  • 3
    It hides the problem. A well-tuned loop makes a deteriorating process look fine — right up to the day the valve reaches 100 % and the loop runs out of muscle. Trend the OP, not just the PV. A PV that has been flat for six months while the OP has crept from 45 % to 88 % is a heat exchanger about to be cleaned in an emergency.
💼

That third point is the single highest-value maintenance idea in this primer, and it costs nothing to implement. Ask your SCADA to trend controller outputs on a monthly chart. Every slow degradation in the plant — fouling, wear, leakage, filter blockage, valve erosion — shows up as a drifting OP long before it shows up as a process problem.

FEEDFORWARD — THE RIGHT ANSWER TO D1, AND ITS ONE WEAKNESS STRETCH

The stenter's dominant disturbance is line speed, and line speed is already measured, to the millimetre, because the drive controls it. So there is no need to wait for the temperature to fall: when the line speed goes from 18 to 32 m/min, add 11 % to the burner demand at the same instant. The feedback controller is then left with only the model's error to clean up — a far smaller job.

FF is just a straight line fitted to the plant, then added to the OP: OP = FF(speed) + PID(error) from the step tests: OP_ff = 0.786 × speed[m/min] + 47.9 (%) at 18 m/min → 62.0 % at 32 m/min → 73.1 % Δ = 11.0 % Feedforward's virtues: Feedforward's one fatal weakness: • acts before the PV moves • it is open loop. It never checks. • no stability limit If the model is wrong, or the burner • handles measured disturbances efficiency drifts, FF is confidently of any size wrong for ever. Therefore they are always used together: FF does the big, fast, predictable part; feedback trims the remainder and guarantees zero steady-state error. A10 and A58 build this out properly, including dynamic (lead-lag) FF.

The Process Comes First — Three Numbers, One Stopwatch

YOU CANNOT TUNE WHAT YOU HAVE NOT MEASURED CORE

Before any of P, I or D means anything, you need three numbers about the process — not the controller. Get them with one experiment: put the loop in manual, step the output by a known amount, and watch the PV with a stopwatch. Everything else in this primer is arithmetic on those three numbers.

K — process gain

ΔPV / ΔMV

How much the PV moves, per unit of output, once everything has settled. Dimensionless if both are in % of span. “How strong is my valve?”

τ — time constant

63.2 %

How long the process takes to travel 63.2 % of the way to its new value, once it has started moving. “How heavy is my process?”

θ — dead time

nothing

The interval after the step during which absolutely nothing happens. Transport, mixing, sampling, scanning. “How blind am I?”

The two-point method. Two stopwatch readings and a final value give you the entire model that every tuning rule in the world is built on. This is the actual stenter test.

THE TWO-POINT METHOD — memorise these three lines and you can commission 1 put the loop in MANUAL, wait for a genuinely steady PV 2 step the output by ΔMV (5–15 % — big enough to see, small enough to be safe) 3 record the time to reach 28.3 % and 63.2 % of the total PV change τ = 1.5 × ( t63 − t28 ) θ = t63 − τ K = ΔPV[% span] / ΔMV[%] Why 28.3 %? Because it is 1 − e^(−1/3), i.e. the point reached at τ/3 after the dead time. Two equations, two unknowns, no curve fitting, and it is immune to the thing that ruins the eyeball method: you never have to guess where the response “starts”, which is exactly where noise lives.
θ/τ — THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RATIO IN CONTROL CORE

Neither τ nor θ alone tells you whether a loop will be easy or awful. Their ratio does, and it does so almost perfectly. This one number decides how much gain you may use, how fast the loop can be, whether derivative helps, and whether you should be considering something cleverer than PID at all.

SELF-REGULATING VS INTEGRATING — TWO SPECIES OF PROCESS CORE

Self-regulating

Step the output and the PV goes to a new steady value on its own. Temperature, flow, pressure in a leaky vessel, composition. The process has its own internal negative feedback (heat loss rises with temperature). Described by K, τ, θ. Most loops.

Integrating (non-self-regulating)

Step the output and the PV ramps for ever. Level in a tank with a fixed-speed outlet pump, pressure in a sealed vessel, position of an axis. There is no steady state to measure, so there is no K and no τ — only an integrating rate K* in %span per minute per %OP.

An integrating process is tested differently and tuned differently: step the output by ΔMV, measure the resulting slope of the PV: K* = (d PV/dt) / ΔMV in % span per minute per % output Example: dye vessel level, +10 % on the fill valve gives a ramp of 4.2 % span per minute → K* = 0.42 %/min per % The critical practical differences: • an integrating process needs no integral action to reach setpoint — the process itself integrates. Adding lots of I is what makes level loops cycle endlessly, and it is the classic level-loop mistake. • it has no offset under P-only control, ever. • averaging level control deliberately uses very LOW gain, letting the level wander across the tank to protect the downstream flow. Tight level control is usually a design error, not an achievement.

On/Off Control — The Simplest Loop, and Why It Cycles For Ever

THE THERMOSTAT, AND WHY IT NEVER SITS STILL CORE

A domestic thermostat has exactly two outputs: on and off. There is no gain, no reset, no rate. It is the whole of control theory reduced to one comparison — and studying it properly earns you the single most important insight in this primer, because everything that goes wrong in a proportional loop is already visible here in a cruder form.

The behaviour is a limit cycle: the PV never settles, it oscillates for ever between two values. And here is the part almost everybody gets wrong — the size of that oscillation is not set by the hysteresis band you configured. It is set by the dead time.

Why, in four lines, with no calculus: 1 The heater switches OFF the instant the PV crosses SP + h/2. 2 But the heat already in transit keeps arriving for θ seconds. 3 So the PV keeps climbing for θ seconds after the decision, at whatever rate it was climbing: overshoot ≈ θ × (dPV/dt). 4 The same happens on the way down, at the falling rate. peak-to-peak ≈ h + θ·(rise rate) + θ·(fall rate) Set h to zero and the cycle does not disappear. It gets faster, and the amplitude stops falling once the dead-time terms dominate. Hysteresis buys you contactor life, not accuracy.

Proportional — The Float Valve, the Band, and the Gap That Will Not Close

WHAT PROPORTIONAL ACTUALLY DOES CORE

Stop switching. Instead, open the valve in proportion to how wrong things are. Small error, small correction; big error, big correction. That is the whole idea, and it is written in one line.

OP = bias + Kc × e where e = SP − PV, both in % of span Two names for the same setting, and you must be fluent in both: Kc controller gain, dimensionless — % output per % of span PB proportional band, in % — PB = 100 / Kc PB has a beautifully physical meaning, and it is worth saying out loud: PB is the amount the PV must change to drive the output through its entire 0-to-100 % travel. PB = 25 % on a 0–250 °C span → 25 % of 250 = 62.5 °C So the burner goes from fully off to fully on across 62.5 °C. Outside that 62.5 °C window the controller is saturated and is, for the moment, an on/off controller with extra steps. Small PB = high gain = aggressive. Large PB = low gain = gentle. The two move in opposite directions, and mixing them up is the single most common cause of somebody making a loop worse while trying to help.

Three proportional bands through the same setpoint. Every line passes through the bias point — the band only changes the slope. Off the sloped section the controller is fully open or fully shut.

THE BIAS, AND WHY OFFSET IS UNAVOIDABLE CORE

Set e = 0 in the equation and the output is bias and nothing else. So a proportional controller can only produce exactly one output at zero error — the one you configured. If the plant needs any other output, the loop must sit at whatever error produces it. That is not a defect to be tuned away. It is the equation, rearranged.

DERIVATION — three lines of algebra, no calculus (the exam favourite) process, at steady state: PV = K · (OP + d) d = the load, in %OP controller: OP = bias + Kc · (SP − PV) the bias was set so that at d = 0 the loop sits exactly on SP. substitute and collect PV: PV (1 + K·Kc) = SP (1 + K·Kc) + K·d offset = PV − SP = K · d / (1 + K · Kc) Three consequences, all worth memorising: 1 offset ∝ the size of the load change. No load change, no offset. This is why D1 was on setpoint at 18 m/min and 5.9 °C low at 32. 2 offset ∝ 1/(1+K·Kc). Doubling the gain roughly halves the offset, but never reaches zero, at any finite gain. 3 the popular shortcut ΔOP/Kc ignores the “1 +” and therefore always over-estimates, by the factor (1+K·Kc)/(K·Kc). At the stenter that is 6.80/5.80 = 1.172×: the shortcut says 6.88 °C, the truth is 5.86 °C.

Integral — The Mode That Finishes the Job, and the One That Eats Plants

THE ONE-SENTENCE DEFINITION CORE

Integral action means: while an error exists, keep moving the output in the direction that reduces it — and when the error reaches zero, stop moving and stay where you are. That is it. That is the entire mode, and it is why it removes offset: its output is no longer a function of the present error, so zero error no longer implies zero output.

OP = bias + Kc · e  +  (Kc/Ti) · ∫e dt (ISA standard form) ∫e dt is nothing more frightening than “error × how long it lasted, added up”. On a controller executing every 0.5 s it is literally: accumulator = accumulator + e × 0.5 every scan Ti, the integral or RESET time, has a physical meaning you can time with a watch: it is how long integral action takes to repeat the proportional contribution. hold a constant error of 1 % of span, Kc = 2, Ti = 60 s: t = 0 s P contributes 2 %, I contributes 0 %, total 2 % t = 60 s P contributes 2 %, I contributes 2 %, total 4 % ← one repeat t = 120 s P contributes 2 %, I contributes 4 %, total 6 % t = 300 s I contributes 10 %, total 12 % Short Ti = fast, aggressive reset. Long Ti = slow, gentle reset. Ti = 9999 s means “effectively off” — which is fault D1.

WINDUP — FAULT D2, AND THE MOST EXPENSIVE MISTAKE IN THIS PRIMER CORE

Integral action has one catastrophic failure mode, and every plant meets it. The controller accumulates error whether or not its output can do anything about it. Once the valve is already at 100 %, more output is meaningless — but the accumulator does not know that, and keeps growing.

Fault D2. Nothing here is broken. The controller is executing its algorithm perfectly, on an error it was powerless to act on for 34 minutes.

D2, in one paragraph: Dye vessel 4: τ = 2,600 s, full-power steady 160 °C, ambient 30 °C, SP 130 °C on a 0–200 °C span, Kc = 1.5, Ti = 900 s, anti-windup OFF. The liquor reaches 130 °C at 65 min. The steam valve is saturated at 100 % for 90 min — so for 25 minutes AFTER the setpoint was reached the valve is still wide open. The accumulator has grown to the equivalent of 133 % output; 33 % of demand exists that the valve can never deliver and that must now be cancelled by NEGATIVE error before the valve moves. Result: overshoot 17.9 °C, 110 min above SP + 0.5 °C. With anti-windup clamping alone: 7.2 °C Clamping + 1.2 °C/min setpoint ramp + retune (Kc 8, Ti 200 s): overshoot 2.1 °C, heat-up 89.5 min — still inside the 90-minute recipe window. Reactive dyes fix irreversibly during an excursion; 14 re-dyes a month became none.
THE THREE CURES, IN ORDER OF PREFERENCE CORE
  • 1
    Anti-windup clamping (integral limiting). The controller freezes the accumulator whenever the output is at a limit and the error would push it further into that limit. Standard on every modern controller, usually a tick-box called anti-reset windup, integral limit or clamping. Cheap, effective, and switched off far more often than anybody realises. Check it first.
  • 2
    External reset / back-calculation. Rather than integrating its own output, the controller integrates towards the actual position of the final element, read back from the positioner or from the slave loop. It therefore physically cannot wind up beyond what the valve can do. This is what makes cascade safe (page 14) and it is the right answer whenever a positioner feedback exists.
  • 3
    Setpoint ramping. Do not ask for something the plant cannot deliver. Ramp the setpoint at a rate the process can actually follow — for D2, 1.2 °C/min against a maximum heating rate that falls from 3.0 °C/min cold to 0.92 °C/min near setpoint. The error stays small, the valve never saturates, and windup cannot occur because the condition for it never arises. This is the cure that also improves the product, because dye uptake depends on the ramp rate as well as the final temperature.
💼

Batch plants — dyeing, brewing, food, pharma, heat treatment — live and die by cure 3. If you can walk into a batch plant and convert three “overshooting” recipes into ramped setpoints with anti-windup enabled, you will have paid your salary for the year in a fortnight, and you will have done it without touching a single tuning constant.

Derivative — The Anticipator Nobody Uses, and Why That Is Mostly Right

WHAT DERIVATIVE ACTUALLY DOES CORE

Derivative action does not care where the PV is. It cares how fast it is moving. It is the mode that says: “you are not there yet, but at this rate you will overshoot — ease off now.” It is the door closer's dashpot, the driver lifting off before the corner, and the reason a good crane operator stops a load without swinging it.

D contribution = Kc · Td · d(PV)/dt (note: PV, not error — see below) Td, the derivative or RATE time, has a physical meaning too: it is how far into the future the controller extrapolates the current trend. Td = 20 s means “act now on where the PV will be in 20 seconds if it carries on at its present rate”. Consequences that follow immediately: • a steady PV, however wrong, produces zero D contribution. Derivative can never remove offset and never reaches setpoint. • D lets you use a HIGHER Kc for the same stability, because it supplies phase LEAD — it is the only mode that gives phase back. • and it multiplies the slope of the measurement — which is exactly what measurement noise consists of.
THE NOISE PROBLEM, WITH A NUMBER CORE

This is the whole reason derivative is switched off in most industrial loops, and it is worth doing the arithmetic once so you never have to argue about it again.

Take a completely ordinary measurement noise: ±0.3 % of span, at 1 Hz. Its rate of change is not small — a sine of amplitude a at frequency f has a peak slope of 2πfa: d(PV)/dt peak = 2π × 1 Hz × 0.3 % = 1.885 % of span per second With Kc = 1.45 and Td = 20 s, the D term's contribution is 1.45 × 20 × 1.885 = 54.7 % of output, oscillating at 1 Hz The valve is being asked to swing across half its travel, once a second, by noise that is one third of one percent of span. The positioner cannot do it, the actuator tries, the packing wears, and the loop looks “unstable”. The standard cure is a derivative filter — a first-order lag of Td/N on the derivative path, with N typically 8 to 10. That caps the D term's high-frequency gain at N × Kc instead of infinity: capped contribution = N × Kc × 0.3 % = 10 × 1.45 × 0.3 = 4.4 % of output a 12× reduction. Every real PID has this filter. If you ever see the textbook Kc·Td·de/dt with no filter, you are reading mathematics, not a controller — an unfiltered derivative has infinite gain at infinite frequency and is not physically realisable.
DERIVATIVE ON PV, NOT ON ERROR — THE “DERIVATIVE KICK” CORE

If derivative acted on the error, then changing the setpoint — an instantaneous step — would produce an infinite rate of change of error, and therefore a violent output spike. Operators call it a kick or a bump, and it is how valves get destroyed by an operator typing a new number.

D on error — wrong

A 10 °C setpoint change on a 250 °C span produces a theoretically infinite spike, limited in practice only by the execution period. On a 0.5 s task with Kc 1.45, Td 20 s: 1.45×20×(4 %/0.5 s) = 232 % — the valve slams full open for one scan.

D on PV — correct

The PV cannot step. Its rate of change is bounded by physics, so the D term stays sane. Every industrial controller does this by default, and it changes nothing about disturbance rejection — because during a disturbance the setpoint is not moving anyway.

The same reasoning is applied more gently to the proportional term as setpoint weighting (often called “b”, or a two-degree-of-freedom controller): the P term acts on b·SP − PV with b between 0 and 1, so setpoint changes are softened while disturbance rejection is untouched. If your controller has a “setpoint filter” or an “SP lead-lag” parameter, that is what it is for. A57 and A58 develop this properly.

Reading a Trend — Six Shapes, Six Accusations

THE SKILL THAT LOOKS LIKE MAGIC CORE

An experienced control engineer walks up to a trend screen, looks for about twenty seconds, and says what is wrong. This is not intuition and it is not experience-you-cannot-teach. It is a decision tree with six branches, and you can have all six by the end of this page.

Two rules first, because without them the tree gives wrong answers. Rule one: always trend the OP alongside the PV. The PV tells you the symptom; the OP tells you who did it. A number, not “fast” or “slow” — because the period compared with the loop's natural period Pu is the single most informative measurement you can make without touching anything.

Stop tuning immediately and go and find it — it is usually another loop, a batch operation, or a compressor cycling. Nobody who does this test first ever wastes an afternoon tuning a loop that was being disturbed from somewhere else. Almost everybody who skips it does, at least once.
💼

Learn to state a diagnosis as “the period is 3.6 minutes, Pu for this loop is about 1.4 minutes, the PV has square shoulders and the OP is a sawtooth, so this is the valve and not the tuning”. That sentence, delivered in front of a production manager who has been told for six months that the loop “cannot be tuned”, is worth more to your career than any certificate.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Six flow loops in one plant area all begin cycling with the same 4.2-minute period, starting on the same day. Each was tuned differently and each has a different valve. Where do you look?

A shared period is a shared cause. The period of a loop's own oscillation depends on its own τ, θ and tuning — all different here. For six loops to agree to four significant figures, they must be being driven by one thing they all share. The usual suspects, in the order to check them:
1 the common header — steam, air, cooling water, or the supply pressure controller for it, cycling at 4.2 min and modulating every downstream flow through Δp 2 a compressor or chiller loading and unloading on a 4.2 min cycle 3 a batch operation upstream with a 4.2 min step in it 4 a shared utility valve, or one large loop whose own oscillation is being exported The diagnostic is beautiful and free: put ONE of the six in manual. If it keeps cycling, the loop is innocent, and so are the other five. Then trend the header pressure on the same time axis and the answer is usually visible in ten seconds. This pattern — one badly tuned supply loop exporting its oscillation to a dozen innocent downstream loops — is one of the most common and most expensive control problems in industry, and the standard reaction (retune the twelve victims) makes the plant permanently worse. The Loop You Build Before You Tune — Ten Things, In Order

TUNING IS THE LAST STEP, NOT THE FIRST CORE

A tuning constant is a number that scales a relationship. If the relationship itself is broken — wrong direction, wrong range, a valve with no authority, a sensor in the wrong place — then no value of that number is right, and the search for it is what people mean when they say a loop “cannot be tuned”. Every one of the four faults in this plant survived multiple tuning attempts.

The professional order of work, and it is never negotiable: 1 the process is it capable of doing what is being asked? 2 the measurement is it right, fast enough, and in the right place? 3 the final element can it move, smoothly, over the needed range? 4 the configuration direction, ranges, units, limits, anti-windup 5 only now: the tuning constants Reverse that order and you will spend a career adjusting numbers that were never the problem. Published surveys of industrial control-loop performance put the share of oscillating loops whose oscillation is caused by the valve — stiction, deadband, hysteresis — at roughly 20 to 30 %, and detuning is precisely the wrong response to every one of them.

The re-ranged transmitter

The missing square root

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A steam valve on a heat exchanger normally sits at 14 % open and the loop is twitchy and hard to tune. The plant proposes a bigger actuator and a faster positioner. What is actually wrong?

The valve is two sizes too big, and everything else follows from that. Valves are routinely over-sized: the process engineer adds margin, the piping engineer rounds up to the line size, and the result is a valve doing its entire job in the bottom fifth of its travel.
why 14 % is a bad place to live, in four separate ways: 1 loop gain. If the valve wide open would pass roughly seven times the flow this service actually needs, then every 1 % of travel does about seven times as much as it would on a correctly sized valve. The process gain K the controller sees is inflated by that same factor, so Kc must be cut by it — and a loop running on a small fraction of an over-sized valve is exactly the loop that feels “twitchy and hard to tune”. 2 resolution. A pneumatic actuator resolves perhaps 0.3–0.5 % of full travel. On a valve using only 14 % of its stroke, 0.4 % of full travel is 2.9 % of the travel actually in use — the controller cannot ask for anything finer than that, no matter what it computes. 3 stiction. Break-away friction is a fixed force set by the packing. As a fraction of the tiny movements needed near the seat it is huge, which is why over-sized valves and stiction limit cycles travel together — and why D3 appeared on a valve, not in a controller. 4 non-linearity. Near the seat the installed characteristic is at its least linear and the trim at its most erosion-prone. The gain the controller sees changes as the operating point moves, so one set of tuning constants cannot be right across the range. The real fixes are all mechanical: a reduced trim, a smaller valve, or two valves in split range. The proposed bigger actuator and faster positioner would improve items 2 and 3 a little and do nothing whatever about items 1 and 4, which are the dominant ones. A59 sizes valves properly.

Tuning Without Laplace — One Dial, Two Formulas, Twenty Minutes

THE IDEA THAT MAKES TUNING SIMPLE CORE

Every tuning method ever published is answering the same question: how fast do you want the closed loop to be? Ask for speed and you spend robustness. Ask for robustness and you give up speed. Modern practice makes that trade-off an explicit, single number you choose — called λ (lambda) — instead of a hidden consequence of a formula you copied.

λ is the closed-loop time constant you are asking for. Choose it as a multiple of the process's own dead time θ, because θ is the thing you cannot beat: λ = θ aggressive. Fast, tight, roughly 1.6× gain margin. Use on flow, and where a small overshoot is harmless. λ = 2θ balanced. The sensible default for most loops. λ = 3θ robust. Slow, smooth, tolerates a process that changes. Use on anything whose gain varies with production rate, on temperature loops feeding product quality, and any time you will not be there to watch it. Then the two formulas — this is the whole of PI tuning: Kc = τ / ( K · (λ + θ) ) Ti = min( τ ,  4 · (λ + θ) ) (These are the SIMC rules — Skogestad's simplification of internal model control. They are the current industrial default, they degrade gracefully when your model is wrong, and they need nothing but K, τ and θ.) If you want PID rather than PI, add: Td ≈ θ/2 and only if the measurement is genuinely clean (page 9).

One dial. Move λ right for a loop you can leave alone, left for a loop you will watch. Everything else is arithmetic.

ZIEGLER–NICHOLS — WHY YOU WILL BE TAUGHT IT AND WHY YOU SHOULD NOT USE IT STRETCH

The 1942 Ziegler–Nichols rules are the ancestor of every tuning table, and they will be on your exam. They are also, by modern standards, far too aggressive for most plant loops — and it is worth understanding exactly why, because the reason is a design objective that nobody would choose today.

Two ZN methods, both still quoted: Closed loop (ultimate) raise Kc with I and D off until the loop oscillates with constant amplitude. That gain is Ku, that period is Pu. Then: P Kc = 0.50 Ku PI Kc = 0.45 Ku Ti = Pu/1.2 PID Kc = 0.60 Ku Ti = Pu/2 Td = Pu/8 Open loop (reaction curve) from a step test, using τ, θ and K directly: PI Kc = 0.9τ/(Kθ) Ti = 3.33θ On the stenter, Ku = 5.0 and Pu = 82.7 s, so ZN-PI gives Kc = 2.25 Ti = 68.9 s against SIMC at λ = θ: Kc = 1.45 Ti = 94.5 s ZN asks for 55 % more gain and 27 % faster reset. Why? Because ZN was designed for quarter-amplitude damping — each overshoot a quarter of the one before. On a 1942 chart recorder that looked decisive and returned to setpoint quickly. It also corresponds to a gain margin of roughly 1.7 and leaves the loop visibly oscillatory, which on modern plant means valve wear, product variation, and no tolerance at all for a process whose gain changes with production rate. And the closed-loop method has a second problem nobody mentions on a slide: you have to deliberately drive a production plant into sustained oscillation to find Ku. That is rarely acceptable and sometimes unsafe. Relay auto-tuning — where the controller substitutes a small on/off relay for a few cycles and infers Ku and Pu from the resulting limit cycle — is the modern way to get the same two numbers safely, and is what the “autotune” button on most controllers actually does.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You tune a loop beautifully at full production rate. Six weeks later the plant is running at 40 % rate for a slow season and the same loop oscillates. Nothing was changed. Which λ should you have used, and why?

λ = 3θ, and the reasoning generalises to almost every plant loop you will meet. Process gain, time constant and dead time are all rate-dependent. Halve the throughput of a heat exchanger and its residence time doubles — so τ and θ both roughly double while K rises. The controller does not know any of this happened.
what actually changes when a plant slows down: residence time ∝ 1/flow → τ and θ both rise transport dead time ∝ 1/velocity → θ rises the most heat-transfer / mixing gain → K usually rises So the loop's effective gain K·Kc rises while its phase margin falls — two changes both in the wrong direction, at once. That is why a loop tuned at 100 % rate so often cycles at 40 %. Three legitimate answers, in increasing order of sophistication: 1 tune at the worst operating point and accept sluggishness elsewhere — which in practice means choosing λ = 3θ 2 gain scheduling: two or three tuning sets, selected by rate 3 fix the underlying non-linearity — correct valve characteristic, ratio control on flow, or a model-based scheme (A58) Option 4, retuning every time, is not an engineering answer. It is a standing task that will be dropped the first week nobody has time, and the loop will then be left on whichever setting was last convenient.

The Valve Is Half the Loop — Stiction, Deadband, and Fault D3

THE CONTROLLER SENDS A NUMBER. THE VALVE DECIDES WHAT HAPPENS. CORE

Why stiction produces a cycle and deadband alone does not — the mechanism, in five steps, and it is worth being able to recite: 1 the PV is a little low; the controller's integral term begins to ramp the output up. A pure deadband stops the cycle instead of sustaining it, because the valve stops inside the band rather than jumping through it. That is the difference between an annoying loop and a permanently cycling one, and it is why the word “stiction” is used so precisely in this trade.

THE CURES, AND WHAT EACH ONE IS WORTH STRETCH

Fit or repair a positioner

Turns the valve into its own fast closed loop with stem-position feedback. Typically reduces effective stiction by an order of magnitude. Cheapest large improvement available in process control. Not appropriate on very fast loops where the positioner's own dynamics interfere — historically the flow-loop exception, though modern digital positioners are fast enough for most.

Repack correctly

Graphite packing over-tightened after a leak is the classic cause — and it is exactly what happened on jet dyer 4. Live-loaded packing with a Belleville stack maintains sealing force without the maintenance fitter's judgement, and is worth specifying on any valve in a control service.

Right-size the valve

Move the normal operating point into 30–70 % travel where friction is a smaller fraction of the movements required and the installed characteristic is most linear. A reduced trim is far cheaper than a new valve.

What NOT to do

Do not detune. Lowering Kc lengthens the period and typically leaves the amplitude unchanged or slightly larger — you have made the cycle slower and the loop useless. Do not add a filter; it hides the evidence and adds dead time. Do not add derivative; it will amplify the jumps.

Manual, Auto, Cascade — How Loops Are Stacked

THE THREE MODES EVERY CONTROLLER HAS CORE

MANUAL

The operator sets the output directly. The algorithm is not running. Used for commissioning, step testing, and every emergency. The controller must keep tracking so it can be handed back cleanly.

AUTO

The algorithm runs against a setpoint entered locally — by an operator or a recipe. The normal state of a working loop.

CASCADE

The setpoint comes from another controller's output. The loop is now a slave. Its own operator setpoint field is greyed out.

A plant's control health can be read from one number: the fraction of loops sitting in manual. Every loop in manual is an operator doing by hand what a machine was bought to do, and it is nearly always a symptom — a loop that oscillates, a valve that sticks, tuning nobody trusts. Three of the four loops in this dyeing plant were in manual, which is why the plant was losing two million rupees a month.

BUMPLESS TRANSFER — the feature you notice only when it is broken Going MANUAL → AUTO, the controller must not move the valve at the instant of transfer. To do that it pre-loads its integral accumulator with whatever value makes its computed output equal the output the operator currently has: I := OP_manual − bias − Kc·e at the moment of transfer If this is missing or broken, the valve jumps at the moment of transfer, the operator sees a bump, and from then on nobody in that control room will ever put that loop in auto again — regardless of how well it is tuned afterwards. One bad transfer poisons a loop for years. The same tracking is what makes cascade safe: a slave that is not in cascade must keep telling its master what output it is actually at, so the master can track it and hand over without a bump. External reset (page 8) is the mechanism that does both jobs at once.
CASCADE — THE MOST USEFUL STRUCTURE IN PROCESS CONTROL CORE

One controller's output becomes another's setpoint. The master (outer, slow, the thing you care about) tells the slave (inner, fast, the thing that misbehaves) what to do. The slave absorbs its own disturbances before the master ever sees them.

The steam header dips. Without cascade, the vessel temperature must fall before anything happens. With cascade, the flow controller has already corrected before the temperature moved at all.

THE THREE RULES OF CASCADE — all three must hold or it makes things worse 1 the slave must be faster than the master, by a good margin. The usual rule of thumb is at least 3×, preferably 5–10× faster (compare the two τ values, or the two Pu values). A slave no faster than its master adds lag and destabilises both. 2 the slave must see the disturbance first. Cascade only pays if the inner loop encloses the thing that misbehaves — header pressure, valve non-linearity, fuel calorific value. 3 tune the slave first, in auto, alone. Then close the master around it. Never the other way round, and never both at once. Tuning order and modes on handover: slave in AUTO, master in MANUAL → tune slave slave in CASCADE, master in AUTO → tune master and confirm the slave tracks when the master is not in auto, or the transfer will bump.
FOUR MORE STRUCTURES YOU WILL MEET IN A DYEING PLANT STRETCH

Ratio control

Keep B in a fixed ratio to A, where A is uncontrolled (“wild”). Dye-to-liquor, chemical-to-fabric, air-to-fuel. Implemented as a feedforward multiply with a feedback trim on the resulting quality. D4's dosing loop should be a ratio loop — dose per kg of fabric, not a concentration chased after the fact.

Split range

One controller, two final elements over different parts of the output span: 0–50 % opens the cooling valve, 50–100 % opens the steam valve. Beware the gain discontinuity at the crossover and the deadband people insert there to stop both running at once.

Override / selector

Two controllers compete for one valve and a low- or high-selector decides. “Control temperature, unless pressure exceeds the limit.” The unselected controller must have external reset or it winds up while it is being ignored — the classic override bug.

Cascade to a drive

The commonest modern cascade is a process loop whose output is a VFD speed reference. The drive's own internal speed loop is the slave, and it is typically a hundred times faster than the process — so rule 1 is satisfied automatically. See A08.

Rule 2 restated as a question you can ask in ten seconds: “what disturbance does the inner loop see that the outer one would only find out about later?” If there is no good answer, there should be no cascade.

The Digital Reality — and the Dead Time You Installed Yourself

A DIGITAL CONTROLLER IS A SAMPLED CONTROLLER CORE

Everything so far has quietly assumed the controller is watching continuously. It is not. It wakes up every T seconds, reads the input, computes, writes the output, and goes back to sleep. Between those moments the plant is unobserved and the output is frozen. That sampling is not free, and its cost is measured in the only currency control has: dead time.

a controller with execution period T adds, on average, T/2 of dead time Why T/2 and not T: a disturbance can arrive at any moment within the interval, uniformly. On average it waits half an interval to be seen. (The zero-order hold on the output adds the other half of the story; T/2 is the standard, and adequate, engineering figure.) Practical rules, worth memorising as a set: task period T ≤ τ/10 comfortable T ≤ τ/4 absolute limit, degraded phase margin Nyquist anything above 1/(2T) Hz will alias — it will not be filtered out, it will reappear as a slower, completely fictitious oscillation in your trend integral Ti should be comfortably larger than T; the discrete integral is a sum, and a sum with only a few terms per time constant is a coarse approximation And a warning about PLCs specifically: the task period you configured is not always the period the PID actually runs at. If the PID sits in the main program scan rather than a periodic task, its execution interval is the scan time — which varies with program branching and comms load. A PID whose ∫e dt assumes a fixed dt while dt actually wanders is a PID whose integral time is quietly wrong and quietly variable. Put PID blocks in a periodic task. Always. B03 covers scan behaviour in detail.

FILTERS — WHEN THEY ARE RIGHT, AND WHEN THEY ARE A CONFESSION STRETCH

A filter is right when…

the noise is genuinely in the measurement — electrical pickup, turbulence, surface splash — and it is fast compared with the process. A filter of τf ≤ τ/20 costs almost nothing in phase and can double the usable derivative gain.

A filter is wrong when…

the “noise” is the process actually moving. Filtering a real oscillation does not stabilise it; it blinds the controller to it, adds dead time, and makes the underlying problem harder to find later. D4's 30 s filter was 75 % of the loop's time constant.

The test to apply before adding any filter: Is the movement I want to remove faster than anything the process can physically do? yes → it is measurement noise. Filter it, with τ_f ≤ τ/20, and record what you did and why. no → it is the process. Filtering it is hiding evidence. Find out what is moving it. And the arithmetic that makes the point, on D4: τ = 40 s, filter 30 s → τ_f/τ = 0.75, fifteen times the τ/20 limit. Cutting it back to a defensible τ/20 = 2 s takes the loop's total dead time from 46.6 s to 18.6 s and raises the gain it could carry by 2.5× — one number typed into one field, at zero cost, on a Tuesday afternoon. Deleting the filter entirely would give 16.6 s and 2.8×.
💼

Ask, at your next plant: “can we get a list of every filter time constant configured in the control system?” Almost nowhere can. Producing that list, comparing each filter against its loop's τ, and deleting the indefensible ones is a genuinely valuable, low-risk, high-visibility project that a junior engineer can own end to end.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A pressure loop with τ = 8 s runs in a PLC's main program scan, which averages 40 ms but rises to 180 ms whenever the SCADA polls. The loop is mostly fine but occasionally “lurches”. What is happening?

The loop is being retuned, several times a second, by network traffic. Note that option (2) is a red herring: 180 ms against τ = 8 s is T = τ/44, comfortably inside the τ/10 rule. The absolute period is fine. The variation is not.
a discrete PID accumulates I := I + Kc · e · dt / Ti If the block uses a CONFIGURED dt while the real interval is different, the effective integral time is scaled by the ratio: Ti_effective = Ti × (dt_configured / dt_actual) configured dt = 40 ms, actual 40 ms → Ti as intended configured dt = 40 ms, actual 180 ms → Ti_eff = Ti / 4.5 integral 4.5× too fast Derivative is worse, because it divides by dt: the D term is scaled by dt_configured/dt_actual as well, and any noise in dt itself appears directly in the output. The cure is one line of engineering and it is not negotiable: put every PID in a PERIODIC task whose period matches its dt parameter, and make sure that task's execution time has margin. Most modern PLC PID blocks can also measure their own elapsed time and use it — but only if you enable that option, and it is rarely on by default. This is one of the most common and least diagnosed faults in PLC-based process control, precisely because it is intermittent and correlates with something — network load — that nobody trends alongside the loop.

PROBLEM SET — TWELVE QUESTIONS. TAP EACH TO REVEAL. CORE

Q1   A heating loop uses an air-to-close (fail-open) steam valve. What controller action is required, and why?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Direct acting. The rule “heating needs reverse” assumes an air-to-open valve, where more output opens the valve. An air-to-close valve inverts that: more output closes it and reduces heat. Multiply the signs — controller × valve × process — and the product must be negative: direct (+) × air-to-close (−) × heating (+) = −. Correct.

Q2   A transmitter is re-ranged from 0–100 °C to 0–200 °C. Nothing else changes. What has happened to the loop, and what must you do?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: A given physical temperature error is now half as many % of span, so the controller sees half the error and produces half the output change. The effective loop gain has halved. To restore the original behaviour, double Kc (halve PB). Ti and Td are unaffected — they are times, not gains. Note that nothing on the operator's screen changes, which is why this is one of the hardest faults to find retrospectively.

Q3   A loop cycles with a period of 40 s. Its process has τ = 50 s and θ = 10 s, giving Pu ≈ 38 s. What is your first action?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: The period is essentially Pu, so this is a gain problem, not a reset problem and not the valve. Halve Kc and change nothing else. Then re-test with a small load change. Resist the temptation to adjust Ti at the same time — if you move two parameters you cannot attribute the result to either.

Q4   Why does a proportional-only controller on a level loop have no offset, when the same controller on a temperature loop does?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Because level is an integrating process. The level stops changing only when inflow exactly equals outflow, and that balance can be achieved at any level. The process supplies the integration that the controller lacks, so the steady state is reached with zero error. A self-regulating process such as temperature has a unique output for each steady PV, so the controller must sit at an error to produce a different output. This is also why adding lots of integral to a level loop makes it cycle: you have put two integrators in series.

Q5   A controller is configured with reset expressed in repeats per minute. You want an integral time of 2.5 minutes. What do you enter?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: 0.4 repeats/minute (1/2.5). Enter 2.5 by mistake and integral action is 6.25× too fast (the ratio 2.5/0.4), which will produce a slow rolling oscillation with a period of several times Pu. Always confirm the units before typing — the parameter is a reciprocal, so the error is quadratic in the mistake, not linear.

Q6   A flow loop's PV is a clean square wave and its OP is a triangular sawtooth, period 4 min, on a process with Pu ≈ 8 s. Detuning has been tried. What do you do next, and what do you refuse to do?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Period is 30× Pu with the classic stiction signature. Do: put the loop in manual and run the 0.5 % step test to measure the stiction band; then repack the valve or fit a positioner. Refuse to: detune further (it lengthens the period without shrinking the amplitude), add a filter (it hides the evidence and adds dead time), or add derivative (it amplifies the jumps).

Q7   A dryer temperature loop has τ = 300 s and θ = 20 s, with K = 0.8. Give PI settings for a robust tuning, and state the resulting proportional band.

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Robust means λ = 3θ = 60 s, so λ+θ = 80 s.
Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)) = 300/(0.8 × 80) = 4.69  →  PB = 100/4.69 = 21.3 %
Ti = min(τ, 4(λ+θ)) = min(300, 320) = 300 s, Td = 0.
Sanity: θ/τ = 0.067, firmly lag-dominant, so a high gain is expected and correct — a narrow proportional band is not automatically aggressive.

Q8   The same dryer, but a maintenance fitter installs the thermocouple in an over-size thermowell with an air gap, raising the sensor lag to 180 s. Nothing else changes. What must happen to the tuning?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: The sensor lag is now in series with the process, and a second large lag converts mostly into apparent dead time in the two-point fit. Simulating the 300 s + 180 s cascade with 20 s of transport gives t28 = 267 s, t63 = 530 s, hence τ = 394.5 s, θ = 135.5 s — θ/τ has gone from 0.067 to 0.343, from lag-dominant to difficult. At λ = 3θ: Kc = 394.5/(0.8×542) = 0.91, PB = 110 %, Ti = 395 s — the loop has lost 81 % of its usable gain. The right answer is not to retune. It is to fit the thermowell correctly: a five-minute installation error cost more performance than any tuning could ever recover.

Q9   Six loops in one area start cycling with the same period on the same day. One of them is put in manual and keeps cycling. What have you proved, and what do you do?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: You have proved that this loop is not the source — its output was frozen and the PV still cycled, so something external is driving it. Combined with the shared period, the cause is a common upstream service: a header pressure loop, a compressor or chiller cycling, a shared utility valve, or a batch operation. Trend the candidate upstream tags on the same time axis and find the one whose oscillation leads the others. Retuning any of the six victims makes the plant worse.

Q10   Why is derivative action normally taken on the PV rather than on the error, and what is the cost of getting it wrong?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Because a setpoint change is a step, and the derivative of a step is a spike. On error, a 10 °C setpoint change on a 250 °C span in a 0.5 s task with Kc = 1.45 and Td = 20 s gives 1.45×20×(4 %/0.5 s) = 232 % of output for one scan — the valve slams. On PV there is no spike, because a physical process variable cannot step. Disturbance rejection is unaffected, since the setpoint is not moving during a disturbance.

Q11   A cascade is proposed: master = tank level (τ effectively integrating), slave = outlet flow (τ ≈ 3 s). Is this a good cascade? What if the slave were the outlet temperature (τ ≈ 120 s)?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Level over flow is the textbook cascade and an excellent one. The flow slave is orders of magnitude faster than the level master (rule 1), and it absorbs exactly the disturbances the master should never see — pump curve changes, downstream pressure, valve stiction and the valve's non-linear characteristic (rule 2). Level over temperature is nonsense: the slave is slower than useful, it does not enclose the level's disturbances, and manipulating temperature does not control level at all. The inner variable must be one that (a) responds fast and (b) directly influences the outer one.

Q12   You are handed a loop described as “impossible to tune”. List, in order, the first five things you do — before entering a single number.

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: 1. Trend PV and OP together for a few cycles, and write the period down as a number. 2. Put it in manual with the output frozen — does the cycle stop? If not, the loop is innocent; go upstream. 3. Read the configuration: mode, controller action, Ti and its units, Td, anti-windup, output limits, filter constants, transmitter range. 4. Run the 0.5 % valve step test. 5. Do a proper step test and compute K, τ, θ and θ/τ.
Only after all five do you compute Kc and Ti — and by then you will usually have found that the answer was never a tuning constant. In this plant it was a tuning constant exactly once out of four.

DERIVATION 1 — OFFSET, AND WHY INTEGRAL KILLS IT STRETCH
Steady state, self-regulating process, everything in % of span / % of output. Let d be a load expressed as the equivalent output the plant now needs. process: PV = K (OP + d) P controller: OP = bias + Kc (SP − PV), with bias chosen so PV = SP at d = 0 PV = K bias + K Kc SP − K Kc PV + K d PV (1 + K Kc) = K bias + K Kc SP + K d and at d = 0, PV = SP ⇒ SP (1 + K Kc) = K bias + K Kc SP ⇒ K bias = SP PV − SP = K d / (1 + K Kc) (1) Now the same with integral. At a genuine steady state the accumulator has stopped changing, and an integrator's input must therefore be zero: d(∫e dt)/dt = e = 0 at steady state, by definition of steady state There is no algebra to do. If the loop settles at all, it settles with e = 0, for any finite constant d, and for any Kc and Ti that keep it stable. That is the whole argument, and it is why integral action is not “better proportional” — it is a structurally different promise. Two riders that examiners like: • it holds only for a constant disturbance. Against a ramping load a PI loop retains a constant error; you would need a second integrator. • it holds only if the output does not saturate. A loop pinned at 100 % has an error it cannot remove, and page 8's windup is what happens next. • equation (1) also reads as the disturbance-rejection ratio: the loop shrinks a load upset by the factor 1/(1+K·Kc). At the stenter, 1/6.80 = 0.147 — it removes 85.3 % of the upset with P alone.
DERIVATION 2 — WHY 28.3 % AND 63.2 %, AND WHY TWO POINTS BEAT ONE STRETCH
A first-order process with dead time, responding to a step at t = 0: PV(t) = ΔPV · [ 1 − e^(−(t−θ)/τ) ] for t ≥ θ, and 0 before that. Pick two fractions F₁ and F₂ and let t₁, t₂ be the times they are reached: t₁ = θ + τ · ln(1/(1−F₁)) t₂ = θ + τ · ln(1/(1−F₂)) Subtract, and θ cancels — which is the entire point of the method: t₂ − t₁ = τ [ ln(1/(1−F₂)) − ln(1/(1−F₁)) ] Choose F₂ = 1 − e^(−1) = 0.632 → ln term = 1 F₁ = 1 − e^(−1/3) = 0.283 → ln term = 1/3 t₂ − t₁ = τ(1 − 1/3) = (2/3)τ ⇒ τ = 1.5 (t63 − t28) and then θ = t63 − τ Why not just use t63 and eyeball where the response starts? Because the start is exactly where the response is flattest and where noise is largest, so “when did it begin?” is the least repeatable measurement on the whole trend. The two-point method never asks. Its robustness comes from the cancellation above, not from clever fitting. Other tangent and two-point constructions exist (Ziegler–Nichols' inflection tangent, the 35.3/85.3 % pair, Sundaresan–Krishnaswamy). They differ by a few per cent and all of them beat guessing the start point.
DERIVATION 3 — THE ULTIMATE GAIN AND PERIOD, FROM τ AND θ ALONE RESEARCH
This is the one place a little trigonometry earns its keep. Feed the loop a sine of angular frequency ω. Each element shifts its phase: dead time θ contributes a lag of ωθ radians — proportional to ω, and unbounded. This is why dead time is so destructive. lag τ contributes a lag of arctan(ωτ), which can never exceed π/2 no matter how fast you go. Sustained oscillation needs a total lag of π radians (180°) at a loop gain of exactly 1. So the ultimate frequency ωu solves ωu θ + arctan(ωu τ) = π (solve numerically — two lines of code) and the ultimate controller gain follows from making the magnitude unity: Ku = √(1 + ωu² τ²) / K Pu = 2π / ωu Worked, for the stenter (K = 1.45, τ = 94.5 s, θ = 22.5 s): ωu = 0.07596 rad/s → Pu = 82.7 s → Ku = 5.00 and for the jet dyer flow loop (K = 1.0, τ = 6 s, θ = 2 s): ωu = 0.8785 rad/s → Pu = 7.15 s → Ku = 5.37 Notice what this gives you: Pu and Ku without ever destabilising the plant. One step test, two lines of arithmetic, and you know both the gain you must not exceed and the period a tuning-induced oscillation would have — which is the number page 10's whole diagnostic tree rests on. Note also the limiting case τ → 0 (pure dead time): arctan → 0, so ωuθ = π and Pu = 2θ, with Ku = 1/K. A pure dead-time process cannot tolerate a loop gain above 1 at all — which is the mathematical statement of “you cannot control what you cannot see yet”.
DERIVATION 4 — THE ON/OFF AMPLITUDE, AND WHY THE BAND BARELY MATTERS STRETCH
Let the process be heating towards a full-power value R with time constant τ, under on/off control about a setpoint S with band h and dead time θ. At the moment the controller switches OFF (PV = S + h/2) the PV is still rising, at the rate the process had at that instant: rise rate = (R − S)/τ and it keeps rising for θ more seconds overshoot above the upper limit ≈ θ (R − S)/τ At the moment it switches ON (PV = S − h/2) the PV is still falling toward ambient at rate S/τ, and keeps falling for θ: undershoot below the lower limit ≈ θ S/τ peak-to-peak ≈ h + θ[ (R−S) + S ] / τ = h + θR/τ Test it against the simulation on page 6: h = 1.0, θ = 12 s, R = 135 °C, τ = 300 s: estimate = 1.0 + 12×135/300 = 1.0 + 5.40 = 6.40 °C simulated 6.43 °C ✓ Two conclusions worth carrying out of this: 1 the amplitude is h plus a term that does not contain h at all. Setting h = 0 leaves θR/τ = 5.40 °C, and buys you an infinite switching rate for a 16 % improvement. 2 the term θR/τ is the whole argument for modulation. Halving θ halves it; nothing you do in the controller touches it. 3 duty cycle at steady state is simply S/R — here 100/135 = 74.1 %, against 73.2 % simulated (the difference is the cycle's asymmetry).
DERIVATION 5 — WHERE THE SIMC FORMULAS COME FROM, WITHOUT LAPLACE RESEARCH
The idea in one sentence: decide what you want the closed loop to look like, then work out what controller produces it. Ask for a closed loop that responds to a setpoint change like a first-order lag of time constant λ, delayed by the process's own θ (which you cannot avoid, so you do not try). The controller that delivers that, for a first-order-plus-dead-time process, turns out to be a PI controller whose integral time cancels the process lag: Ti = τ (the integral action is chosen to cancel the process's dominant lag — this is why Ti = τ keeps appearing) Kc = τ / ( K (λ + θ) ) Read the gain formula physically and it stops being a formula: • more process lag τ → more gain allowed (a heavy process forgives) • more process gain K → less controller gain needed (they multiply) • more θ, or more λ → less gain (blindness and caution cost the same) The one modification that makes it work in practice: Ti = min( τ , 4(λ + θ) ) Without the min(), a process with a very long τ — a big tank, a slow oven — gets an enormous Ti, and the loop takes for ever to remove a load disturbance even though its setpoint response looks perfect. Capping Ti at 4(λ+θ) trades a little setpoint smoothness for a large improvement in disturbance rejection, which is the job in a plant. On the stenter τ = 94.5 < 4×45 = 180, so the cap does not bite; on a 40-minute vessel with θ = 20 s it bites hard, and it is the difference between a loop that works and one that is merely stable. Everything above has a rigorous frequency-domain derivation, which is exactly what A57 does. You do not need it to tune a plant. You do need it to design one.
TEN EXAM AND INTERVIEW TRAPS CORE
  • 1
    “A heating loop needs a reverse-acting controller.” Only with an air-to-open valve. State the valve action or the answer is incomplete. The examinable form is the sign product: controller × valve × process must be negative.
  • 2
    Small PB is high gain. PB = 100/Kc. Candidates routinely say “increase the proportional band to make it more responsive”, which is exactly backwards.
  • 3
    Offset = ΔOP/Kc is an approximation. The exact result is K·ΔOP/(1+K·Kc). If the question gives you the process gain K, it wants the exact one — that is why K was given.
  • 4
    Integral time units. Minutes/repeat and repeats/minute are reciprocals. If a question quotes “reset” in repeats per minute, invert before using any tuning formula.
  • 5
    Derivative acts on PV, not error, in every real controller. Any answer that produces an infinite spike on a setpoint change has used the textbook form, not the industrial one.
  • 6
    τ and θ are not interchangeable. A slow process is easy; a blind one is hard. The examinable quantity is always the ratio θ/τ, never either alone.
  • 7
    An integrating process has no K and no τ. Asking for “the time constant of a level loop” is a trick question; the answer is an integrating rate in %span per minute per %output.
  • 8
    Windup needs saturation, not just a big error. A controller with a huge error but an unsaturated output is not winding up. State the condition, not just the symptom.
  • 9
    A limit cycle that survives detuning is not a tuning problem. Quote the period against Pu. This distinction — controller versus final element — is the most commonly asked practical question in an automation interview.
  • 10
    Cascade requires the slave to be at least 3–5× faster, and to see the disturbance first. Both conditions, every time. A cascade that satisfies only the first is a slower loop with two sets of tuning constants.
