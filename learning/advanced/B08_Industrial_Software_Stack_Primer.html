📊The Industrial Software Stack

PLC, HMI, SCADA, historian, MES, ERP. Six words that get used as if they were interchangeable, by people who are quietly certain the other five mean something else. They are not layers of the same product — they are six different answers to six different questions, separated by roughly a thousand-fold in time constant at every step. This primer builds the whole stack from the bottom, one honest question at a time, and then follows a single number — “how many bottles did we make?” — all the way from a photo-eye to a board pack, watching it change meaning four times on the way. Built around four real faults at a beverage plant in Kelaniya that cost LKR 1.57 million a month for nine months — LKR 14.1 million in all — not one of which was a missing piece of software — and were closed for LKR 630,000, which the plant earned back in 12 days. Not one rupee of that bought new software.

Cold Open — Four Faults, and Not One Missing Program Among Them

A BEVERAGE PLANT, KELANIYA CORE

Three PET filling lines. Line 2 fills 400 ml still water and flavoured water at a nameplate 24,000 bottles per hour, scheduled 20 hours a day, 26 days a month — 520 scheduled hours. Availability runs at 85 %, so the filler is actually turning for 442 hours, and while it turns it averages 22,000 bottles per hour. The photo-eye on the filler outfeed therefore counts 9,724,000 bottles a month, and that is the number on the daily report, on the shift board, in the MES, and in the slide the plant manager takes to Colombo.

The warehouse receives 9,434,380.

Nobody is stealing bottles. Nobody has miscounted. Both numbers are correct — they are answers to two different questions, asked by two different systems, and the plant had never noticed that it possessed two. That is the first of four faults, and it is the shape of every fault in this primer: not a missing system, not a broken program, but an unstated disagreement about what a number means, sitting on a boundary between two layers that nobody owns.

⚠ D1 — LEVEL 1 → LEVEL 3 · “good count” means two things

The MES takes “good bottles” from a photo-eye on the filler outfeed. Downstream, the labeller rejects 1.8 % and the case-packer inspection rejects a further 1.2 % — both after the counting point. Compounded, 1 − (0.982 × 0.988) = 2.978 % of everything counted never reaches a pallet. Planning commits to the filler number, so every month the plant is 289,620 bottles short of its own promise and buys the gap back with 13.17 hours of weekend running at LKR 41,500/h. LKR 546,328/month.

⚠ D2 — LEVEL 2 · the historian threw the evidence away

Final-rinse conductivity, span 0–200 µS/cm, release limit 10 µS/cm. The historian was commissioned on vendor defaults: compression deviation 1.0 % of span = 2.0 µS/cm. The whole decision lives in a band 10 µS/cm wide, so the archive's resolution is one fifth of the question being asked of it. Three product holds in six months could not be investigated because the trend was a straight line through the event. One hold — two hours of Line 2 — was destroyed. The standing countermeasure is a precautionary extra rinse on every CIP: 6 min × 3/day × 26 = 7.8 h of line time a month, forever. LKR 522,738/month.

⚠ D3 — LEVEL 2 → LEVEL 4 · the spreadsheet in the middle

There is no interface between the floor and ERP. At end of shift a supervisor reads fourteen numbers off HMI screens into a paper sheet; next morning a clerk keys them into Excel, and the Excel is keyed into ERP. 234 report transcriptions a month × 14 fields, at a manual re-entry error rate of about 0.55 % per field, gives 1 − 0.994514 = 7.43 % of reports carrying at least one wrong number — 17.4 corrupted reports and 18.0 wrong fields a month. Two people spend three days a month reconciling. And the 08:30 meeting discusses data that is on average 14.5 hours old, so a line problem runs one shift longer than it should. LKR 314,400/month.

⚠ D4 — LEVEL 3 → LEVEL 4 · backflush at standard

ERP consumes materials by backflush: when a work order is confirmed it deducts the bill-of-materials quantity plus a standard scrap allowance. Labels: standard 2.5 %, actual 4.1 %. The book therefore under-consumes 150,950 labels every month against a cover of only four days — 1,510,952 / 150,950 = 10.0 months before the shelf is empty while the book still says “fine”. It emptied in month ten, two months before the annual physical count would have caught it: 6.5 h of line stoppage and 1.6 million labels by air. LKR 186,563/month.

Look hard at the last line, because it is the argument of this entire primer. The plant had a PLC, an HMI, a SCADA package, a historian, an MES module and an ERP. Every one of them worked exactly as sold. Three of the four faults lived in the space between two systems, and the fourth lived in a configuration screen nobody had opened since commissioning. This is what industrial software actually fails at — not computation, but agreement.

Note the ranking too, because it contradicts the folklore. The fault with the worst reputation — ERP backflush, the one everybody blames — is the smallest number on the sheet at 11.9 %. The fault that sounds like a joke — a spreadsheet — is nearly twice as expensive. And the largest single line, the precautionary rinse at LKR 323,700 a month, is not a data cost at all in the ledger: it is booked as production time. Bad data almost never appears in the accounts as bad data. It appears as overtime, as scrap, as a longer cycle, as a buyer's private hedge. Learning to see it is a skill in itself, and it is why this subject is worth nineteen pages.

💼

Ask an interviewer “where does your good-count come from, and how far downstream is it measured?” and watch what happens. In most plants nobody in the room knows, and the two people who think they do will disagree. The engineer who can walk a number from a photo-eye to a board pack — naming every system it passes through, every place it is aggregated, and every place its definition quietly changes — is doing the work of a manufacturing systems engineer or a digital-transformation lead. That role pays well above maintenance-engineer scale in Sri Lanka, in the Gulf and everywhere else, and it requires no programming at all. It requires exactly the map in the next sixteen pages.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Fault D2 again. The historian's compression deviation is 1.0 % of a 0–200 µS/cm span, and every decision about releasing product lives between 2 and 12 µS/cm. The plant's proposed fix is to double the sample rate from one point every 2 seconds to one every second. What will that achieve?

Nothing at all — and it will cost disk for the privilege. A historian applies two independent filters. Exception decides what the collector even sends; compression decides what the archive keeps. Both are amplitude tests, not time tests: a new point is discarded if the stored line already predicts it to within the deviation band. Sampling faster simply offers more points to the same filter, and the filter rejects more of them. The worst-case reconstruction error is bounded by the deviation setting and by nothing else — here ±2.0 µS/cm on a 10 µS/cm decision, so a reading archived as 9.0 could have been anything from 7.0 to 11.0, which is to say the archive cannot answer the only question ever asked of it. The fix is one number in one configuration screen: set this tag's deviation in engineering units of the band that matters (0.1 µS/cm), not as a percentage of a span chosen by the transmitter's range card. Option 3 contains the trap that catches most people — compression does also have a maximum-time rule, but that rule only forces extra points in; it never rescues resolution.

The Five Levels — and the Thousand-Fold Gap That Creates Each One

WHY THERE IS A HIERARCHY AT ALL CORE

The stack is not a hierarchy of importance, or of cleverness, or of price. It is a hierarchy of time. A layer exists at a given level because there is a class of question that must be answered on that time scale — and because answering it on that time scale makes it physically impossible to answer the questions one level up or one level down.

A safety interlock must act in 10 milliseconds. A production schedule must be settled for next week. Nothing sensible can do both, and the reason is not effort or budget: a system that guarantees a 10 ms response must be simple, deterministic, and must throw away its history to stay that way, while a system that plans next week must hold enormous history and can afford to think for a second or two. Every property of every box in this primer — what it stores, what it forgets, what it can promise, what it costs, who is allowed to change it — is downstream of its time constant.

The stack is a logarithmic time axis wearing a costume. Each level is separated from its neighbour by about three decades — which is why no product has ever successfully spanned two of them.

ISA-95 AND PURDUE — TWO MODELS, CONSTANTLY CONFUSED STRETCH

Two different diagrams get drawn with the same five numbers on them, and mixing them up produces some of the most expensive arguments in industrial IT.

ANSI/ISA-95 — a functional model

Internationally IEC/ISO 62264. It says what activities belong at each level and defines the objects and attributes exchanged between them. Part 1 (models and terminology) is now ANSI/ISA-95.00.01-2025; Part 2 (objects and attributes) is 2018; Part 3 (activity models of manufacturing operations management) is 2013. It says nothing whatsoever about networks, firewalls or IP addresses.

Purdue (PERA) — a network zoning model

From the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture. It is the diagram with the DMZ between Level 3 and Level 4, and it is a security and traffic model. Level numbers here mean “which network segment”, not “which activity”. IEC 62443 zones and conduits build on it. Covered properly in A54.

The practical consequence: a single physical server can host Level-2 and Level-3 functions while living in one Purdue zone, and that is not a contradiction. Conversely, an edge gateway may sit in the Level-2 zone while performing a Level-3 activity. When somebody says “that's a Level 3 system”, always ask which model they mean — and if they do not know that there are two, you have learned something useful about the project.

ISA-95 Part 3 also names the four operations models that share the Level-3 space, and they are worth memorising because they are how MES vendors organise their modules: production operations, maintenance operations, quality operations, inventory operations. Each has the same internal shape — definition, resources, scheduling, dispatching, execution, data collection, tracking, analysis — which is why an MES that does production well can usually be extended into maintenance without rewriting its spine. Page 7 unpacks all four.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A plant wants an interlock: if the filler bowl level falls below 18 % while the labeller is running, stop the conveyor within 200 ms. The site has a modern MES with a rules engine and a five-second data refresh, and the integrator proposes implementing the rule there because “it is easier to change and it is already licensed.” What is the strongest objection?

It cannot promise a bound. This is the single most important idea on the page, so hold it carefully: “real time” does not mean fast — it means bounded. A PLC scanning every 10 ms is not impressive because 10 ms is quick; it is impressive because it is 10 ms every time, including the worst time, on a scheduler with no garbage collector, no page faults, no antivirus and no other tenant. An MES rules engine may well respond in 40 ms on a good day — and in 9 seconds when a report query is running, and never at all when the database is failing over. A requirement expressed as “within 200 ms” is a statement about the tail of the distribution, and Level 3 has no tail guarantee at all. Option 3 is the seductive wrong answer, because it treats latency as an average and the network as the only variable. Option 1 is often true in practice but it is a commercial objection, not an engineering one — and commercial objections lose arguments to enthusiastic integrators. This one does not.

Levels 0 and 1 — The Only Layer That Cannot Lie, and the One That Forgets

WHAT A CONTROLLER ACTUALLY HOLDS CORE

A PLC does not have a database. It has a picture of this instant, and it repaints that picture completely, from scratch, every few milliseconds. Everything the rest of the stack knows about the plant is a photograph somebody took of that picture before it was painted over.

Recall the scan from B03: read all inputs into the process image, execute the logic against that frozen copy, write all outputs, repeat. The freeze is what makes the logic deterministic — every rung sees the same instant — and it is also why the controller's memory is a state, never a log. A tag called Line2_Filler_BowlLevel holds one number. When the next scan writes a new number, the old one is gone, unrecoverably, in the same way that yesterday is gone.

THE SAMPLING PROBLEM — WHY THE LAYER ABOVE ALWAYS SEES LESS CORE

The controller repaints at, say, 10 ms. SCADA polls at 1,000 ms. That is a ratio of 100:1 — so on the most optimistic reading, 99 % of the controller's states are never observed by anything above it. This is not a defect to be fixed; it is the price of the abstraction, and it has an exact consequence you can calculate.

Three real events; two survive the climb, both distorted; one is deleted from history by arithmetic alone. No component has failed.

TO SEE AN EVENT OF DURATION d, SAMPLE FASTER THAN d/2 Nyquist, applied honestly to a plant: Tsample < d / 2 To reliably measure the length of it: Tsample < d / 10 Kelaniya, capper reject burst d = 40 ms → need T < 20 ms SCADA poll T = 1,000 ms Ratio 50× too slow Consequence: the burst can only ever be counted in the PLC, and reported upward as a COUNT, never observed from above as a VALUE.

That last line is the design rule the whole industry runs on, and it is worth a paragraph. Anything faster than the poll must be counted, latched or totalised at Level 1 and reported upward as an accumulated number. This is why every real PLC program is full of counters, first-out latches, min/max holds and run-hour totalisers that look like clutter to a newcomer: they are the plant's memory prosthesis. The layer above cannot see events; it can only read summaries that the layer below agreed in advance to keep. Which summaries exist is a decision made once, in the program, and changing it later means changing controller code — which is why “can we add that KPI?” is sometimes a five-minute job and sometimes a shutdown.

TAGS, TYPES AND THE FIVE ARITHMETIC TRAPS STRETCH

Every value that leaves Level 1 leaves as a typed number in a named location. Both halves cause trouble.

  • 1
    Signed 16-bit overflow. An INT stops at 32,767 and wraps to −32,768. A bottle counter incrementing 22,000 per hour overflows in 1 hour 29 minutes. Half the “the counter is faulty” tickets in the world are this. Use DINT (2.1 billion, ~11 years at this rate) and still handle rollover explicitly at the reading end.
  • 2
    Floating-point equality. IF Level = 18.0 will one day be false when the level is 18.0, because 18.0 is not exactly representable and the comparison is bit-for-bit. Always compare with a band, never with equality. A REAL also carries only about 7 significant decimal digits, so a totaliser in a REAL loses its last digit somewhere past ten million — exactly where a monthly bottle count lives.
  • 3
    Word and byte order. A 32-bit value fetched over Modbus arrives as two 16-bit registers, and there are four plausible orders (ABCD, CDAB, BADC, DCBA). Three of them give nonsense that looks like a scaling error. Establish this on day one against a known value — see A01 and B05.
  • 4
    Scaled integers. Level 1 usually carries engineering values as integers with an implied divisor — 1,834 meaning 18.34 %. If the divisor lives only in the SCADA configuration, then the meaning of the tag is stored in a different system from the tag. This is the most common cause of a value being right on one screen and wrong on another.
  • 5
    Quality is a third thing. A value read over any protocol has three parts: value, timestamp and quality. A tag reading 0.0 with quality BAD is not zero — it is unknown. Systems that discard quality (and many spreadsheets, gateways and cheap dashboards do) turn every communications failure into a plausible-looking zero, which then gets averaged into a KPI. Page 13 returns to this; it is the deadliest of the six data lies.
💼

A short, high-value habit: whenever you are handed a tag, ask for its data type, its units, its scaling, its update rate and its quality behaviour — five questions, thirty seconds. In most plants nobody has ever asked all five about the same tag, and the act of asking routinely uncovers a fault. It is also the fastest way to establish, in a first week on site, that you know what you are doing.

Level 2 — HMI, SCADA and DCS: Three Words, Three Different Machines

THE DISTINCTION NOBODY EXPLAINS CORE

These three words are used interchangeably in job adverts, in tender documents and by vendors who would rather you did not think about it. They are not synonyms. They differ in scope, in where the truth lives, and in what happens when they die — and those three differences decide the architecture of a plant.

HMI

A window, not a system. One panel, one machine, usually mounted on the machine. It holds no independent truth: every value on the screen is read live from the controller behind it. Unplug it and the machine keeps running, blind. Its configuration is a screen layout plus a tag address list.

SCADA

Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition. Many controllers, one supervisory view, and — the crucial difference — its own tag database, alarm engine, user model and archive. Born in pipelines and power networks where the “field” was four hundred kilometres away and comms were unreliable, which is why it assumes loss of contact is normal.

DCS

Distributed Control System. Designed top-down as one product: controllers, I/O, operator stations and engineering tools sharing a single configuration database. You build a loop once and it appears everywhere. Process industries, heavy redundancy, control lives in the field. Assumes comms are reliable because it owns them. See A61.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Kelaniya's SCADA holds a tag called L2_GoodCount. The MES reads it once a minute and adds the difference to the work order. One night the SCADA server reboots for a Windows update, taking 4 minutes 20 seconds. Line 2 keeps running throughout. When SCADA comes back, what does the MES record for that period, assuming nobody designed anything specifically for this case?

The correct amount — and this is the single best argument for the design pattern behind it. Because the PLC holds a monotonically increasing totaliser rather than a per-minute rate, the MES's arithmetic is Δ = countnow − countlast, and that difference is indifferent to how long the gap was. Four minutes or four hours, the number comes out right the moment contact is restored. A totaliser is self-healing across an outage; a rate is not. Had SCADA been reading “bottles in the last minute”, those 4 min 20 s would have been gone for ever — option 1 — and that is what happens in a great many plants, which is why their monthly totals never quite reconcile. Two honest caveats, and they are exam material: the totaliser must be retentive (page 3) or a controller power cycle zeroes it, and the reader must handle rollover, since a DINT wraps at 2,147,483,647 — about eleven years at Kelaniya's rate, which sounds safe until you meet a counter someone declared as INT and it wraps every eighty-nine minutes.

The Historian — The Plant's Memory, and How It Is Usually Ruined

WHAT A HISTORIAN IS, AND WHAT IT IS NOT CORE

A process historian is a time-series database: a store optimised for one shape of record — (tag, timestamp, value, quality) — written once, never updated, and read back mostly as ranges. It is not a backup, it is not a relational database, and above all it is not lossless. It throws data away deliberately, permanently, according to rules you chose, and it will never tell you what it discarded.

That is not a criticism. A historian earns its place by being able to hold ten to a hundred times more plant history on the same disk than a naive store, and to return a year of one tag in under a second. It buys that with lossy compression, and the entire skill of owning one is deciding, per tag, how much loss you can afford — because you are deciding it once, for ever, at the moment the data is written.

Data passes three lossy gates before you ever see it. Two are configurable per tag; the third is a retention policy set by IT.

THE SWINGING DOOR, IN PLAIN GEOMETRY STRETCH

Compression is usually a variant of the swinging-door (boxcar–backslope) algorithm, and the geometry is simple enough to draw on a napkin. Hold the last archived point A. For every new sample P, draw two lines from A: one to P + E and one to P − E, where E is the compression deviation. Keep the steepest lower line and the shallowest upper line seen so far — a wedge, narrowing as samples arrive. As long as the wedge is still open, every sample so far lies within ±E of a straight line from A, so none of them need storing. The instant a new sample closes the wedge, the previous sample is archived and becomes the new A.

THE ONLY GUARANTEE A HISTORIAN MAKES For every discarded point, |vtrue − vreconstructed| ≤ E ...and that is the whole guarantee. Not the average error. Not the shape. Not the peak. A bound of ±E, per point. E is stated in one of three ways, and mixing them up is the most common configuration error in the plant: % of SPAN E = 0.01 × (rangehi − rangelo) ← the vendor default % of READING E = 0.01 × v ← sensible for flows ENGINEERING E = 0.1 µS/cm ← the only one that thinks about the DECISION

Kelaniya's fault, precisely: the conductivity transmitter was ranged 0–200 µS/cm because that is the card the supplier had. The release decision lives at 10 µS/cm. A 1 % of-span deviation is therefore ±2.0 µS/cm on a 10 µS/cm question — 20 % uncertainty on the only number anyone cares about. The range card of a transmitter, chosen by a purchasing decision, silently set the resolution of the plant's quality evidence. That sentence is worth reading twice; it is the deepest idea on this page.

💼

“Historian tuning” is a real, billable, under-supplied piece of consulting, and it is almost pure judgement rather than programming. The method is a triage: sort every tag into five classes — safety and quality evidence, control-loop diagnosis, energy and throughput accounting, slow context (ambient, tank levels), and pure noise — then set E in engineering units of the decision for the first two and generously for the rest. At Kelaniya that exercise covered 3,400 tags in six days, cost LKR 144,000, and was the second-largest line in the repair.

FOUR MORE HISTORIAN FACTS THAT BITE STRETCH

Stepped vs interpolated

An analogue tag is read back with straight lines between stored points. A discrete tag must be read back as a step — it held its last value until it changed. Configure a digital tag as interpolated and a valve that opened at 03:00 appears to have crept open over twenty minutes. Every historian has this setting; roughly half of them have it wrong somewhere.

Whose timestamp?

The stamp may come from the device, the collector or the archive. Only the first is the truth. If the collector stamps, then a 40-second network stall relabels forty seconds of plant history as having happened at one instant — and the events arrive in the wrong order.

Store-and-forward is not free

A good collector buffers to local disk when the archive is unreachable and back-fills later, keeping the original timestamps. But back-filled data arrives out of order, and any calculation that ran in the meantime — a shift total, an OEE figure — was computed on a hole. Recalculation is a design decision, not an accident.

Compression is irreversible

There is no “turn it up and re-run last year.” The discarded points do not exist anywhere. This is the only decision in the entire stack that cannot be revisited, which is precisely why it deserves more thought than the software that was expensive.

Where Data Sleeps — Three Shapes, Three Stores, and the Arithmetic

PLANT DATA COMES IN EXACTLY THREE SHAPES CORE

Every argument about “which database” dissolves once you notice that industrial data is not one thing. It is three things with three different access patterns, and forcing all three into one store is the commonest and most expensive architectural mistake in the field.

A work order is not a trend. A trend is not an alarm log. Store each where its access pattern lives, and join them by time and equipment, not by cramming them together.

THE ARITHMETIC THAT SETTLES THE ARGUMENT CORE

Kelaniya collects 3,400 tags at 1 Hz. Somebody always proposes “just put it in SQL Server, we already have a licence.” Here is what that costs, honestly.

NAIVE RELATIONAL ROW tag id 4 + timestamp 8 + value 8 + quality 1 + row overhead ~24 + index entry ~20 = 65 bytes 3,400 tags × 1 Hz × 65 B = 221,000 B/s = 19.09 GB per day = 6.97 TB per year Insert load = 3,400 rows/s = 293.8 million rows/day HISTORIAN / TIME-SERIES STORE ~12 bytes per stored point, ~20:1 compression 3,400 × 12 / 20 = 2,040 B/s = 176 MB per day = 64.3 GB per year RATIO 108× less disk — and the query "one tag, one year" touches 64 MB instead of 2 GB. AFTER ROLL-UP (hourly min/max/avg/count, 4 × 12 B) 3,400 × 24 × 365 × 48 B = 1.43 GB per year a further 45× — which is why five-year-old data answers different questions from last week's.

Two honest qualifications, because the numbers above are the optimistic case for both sides. Modern relational engines have columnstore and native time-series extensions that close much of the gap, and several excellent open time-series databases are “relational” in every sense that matters to an SQL user. The real distinction is not the vendor's noun; it is whether the store is organised by time and tag or by key and relationship. Ask which of those two the index is built on, and you will know what you are dealing with regardless of what it is called.

The second qualification matters more in Sri Lanka than the first: 293.8 million rows a day is not a licensing problem, it is a backup problem. Whatever you build must be restorable, and the restore time of a 7 TB relational database on a plant server is measured in days. A 64 GB archive is restorable over lunch. Sizing decisions are usually justified on disk price; they should be justified on recovery time.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A quality manager asks for “every conductivity reading for batch L2-2611-04, and the operator who released it.” The readings are in the historian; the batch start and end times and the release signature are in the MES relational database. What is the one thing that must be true for this query to be answerable at all?

They must share a clock. Time-series and relational data are joined on time — the MES supplies an interval, the historian supplies the readings inside it — and that join inherits the error of the worse of the two clocks. If the MES server drifts 40 seconds from the historian, then every batch boundary is 40 seconds wrong, and at Kelaniya's changeover rate that regularly attributes the first crate of the new batch to the old one. This is why NTP discipline on every server, and PTP where sub-millisecond ordering matters, is not IT housekeeping but a data-integrity control — and why regulated industries audit it. Level 3 — MES and MOM: The Layer That Knows What Actually Happened

THE ONE-SENTENCE DEFINITION CORE

An MES turns a plan into a sequence of authorised, recorded actions on named equipment with named material — and turns what actually happened back into a record the business can be held to. ERP knows what was ordered. Level 2 knows what is happening now. Only Level 3 knows what happened, to which unit of product, on which machine, from which lot, under whose authority.

The older term is MES — Manufacturing Execution System. ISA-95 prefers MOM — Manufacturing Operations Management — for a good reason: “execution” sounds like it is only about running orders, when in practice the same layer also runs maintenance, quality and inventory. Vendors use both words for the same software. Use MOM when you want to be precise, MES when you want to be understood.

Four operations models, one activity spine. This is why an MES that does production well can usually grow into maintenance — and why one that was written only as a downtime tracker never can.

For food, beverage, pharma and medical devices — four of Sri Lanka's more valuable export sectors — forward genealogy is not an efficiency feature, it is a market-access requirement. An engineer who can specify a genealogy model (what constitutes a lot, where the splits and merges are, how rework is recorded) is doing work that the buyer's audit will look for directly. It is also, usefully, the part of an MES project that the software vendor cannot do for you: only the plant knows where its lots actually break.

WHAT LEVEL 3 CANNOT DO STRETCH
  • 1
    It cannot control. No interlocks, no loops, no timing promises — the argument from page 2. An MES that writes setpoints does so as a request that Level 1 is free to reject, and Level 1 must be programmed to reject it under the wrong conditions. “The MES sent a bad recipe” is never an acceptable root cause; the acceptable root cause is “the controller accepted a bad recipe.”
  • 2
    It cannot be the system of financial record. Its inventory numbers are operational truth, not book value. The reconciliation between the two is a designed process (page 8), not an assumption.
  • 3
    It cannot invent data the floor does not produce. A downtime reason must be entered by someone or inferred from a signal that exists. Most failed MES projects fail here: the model demanded twenty facts a shift that nobody had time to supply, so the operators entered “other” and the analysis died.   • 4
    It cannot survive being the only copy. If the MES is down, production must continue — which means a defined degraded mode, on paper, practised. A plant that stops when its MES stops has built a Level-1 dependency at Level 3, which is the architecture the classifier on page 4 refuses.

Level 4 — ERP: The Business's Model of a Plant It Has Never Seen

ERP IS A LEDGER THAT HAPPENS TO KNOW ABOUT MACHINES CORE

An ERP is, at its heart, a double-entry accounting system with a manufacturing vocabulary bolted to the front. Everything it does is a posting: a discrete, timestamped, auditable, reversible-only-by-another-posting event that moves value from one account to another. It has no concept of “now”, no concept of a trend, and no way to represent a machine that is 63 % of the way through a batch. It only knows what has been declared.

This is not a limitation to be worked around; it is the whole point. The business needs a record that cannot silently change, that balances, and that a regulator or auditor can reconstruct. Every friction between the floor and ERP comes from the same root: the floor lives in states that change continuously, and ERP lives in transactions that happen instantaneously and never un-happen. Level 3 exists, in large part, to translate between those two worldviews.

Master data — slow, controlled, expensive to be wrong

Material master, bill of materials, routing, work centre, cost centre, standard cost, planner codes. Changed under formal change control, often by a different department in a different country. A wrong scrap factor here is the fault of page 1, D4.

Transactional — fast, high volume, auditable

Sales order → planned order → production order → goods issue → confirmation → goods receipt → invoice. Each is a posting with a document number. The production order is the object Level 3 cares about; everything else is business plumbing.

BACKFLUSH — WHY IT EXISTS, AND WHY IT DRIFTS CORE

A bottle of water consumes one preform, one cap, one label and a twenty-fourth of a carton. Kelaniya makes 362,861 bottles a day. Posting a goods issue for each material for each bottle would be roughly 1.5 million postings a day, and no ERP on earth is willing. So instead the system uses backflush: when a production order is confirmed for a quantity, ERP automatically deducts the BOM quantity for that many units, including a standard scrap allowance held on the master record.

It is an elegant solution to a real problem, and it is exact only if the standard scrap equals the actual scrap. It never does, and the error does not cancel — it accumulates in one direction, month after month, invisible because both sides of the posting balance perfectly.

THE BACKFLUSH DIVERGENCE LAW Consumed in reality Ca = P (1 + sa) Consumed on the book Cs = P (1 + ss) Monthly divergence D = P (sa − ss) Cover held SS = Ca × d / 26 (d = days of cover) Months until the shelf is empty while the book says fine: N = SS / D Kelaniya, labels: P = 9,434,380, ss = 2.5 %, sa = 4.1 % D = 9,434,380 × 0.016 = 150,950 labels / month Ca = 9,434,380 × 1.041 = 9,821,190 SS = 9,821,190 × 4 / 26 = 1,510,952 N = 1,510,952 / 150,950 = 10.0 months NOTE THE SHAPE: D is proportional to PRODUCTION, while SS is proportional to production too — so N depends only on d / (26 (sa − ss) (1 + sa)). Growing the plant does NOT buy you time. Only tightening the scrap estimate, or counting more often, does.

That last observation is the examinable one, and it surprises people. Doubling output doubles both the divergence and the cover, so the fuse burns at exactly the same speed. The only two levers are the accuracy of the standard and the frequency of the physical count — which is precisely why serious plants replace the annual wall-to-wall count with cycle counting: a rolling count of a few items every day, weighted so that fast-moving and high-value materials are counted monthly and slow ones annually. Kelaniya's fuse was ten months and its count was annual; the arithmetic guaranteed the outcome.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Kelaniya's fix for D4 was to feed the MES's measured scrap into the ERP backflush instead of the standard. A month later, finance objects: the standard cost of a case has not changed, but a large purchase price variance and a large consumption variance have appeared in the accounts, and the plant now looks worse than it did. What has actually happened?

The loss was always there. Only its hiding place has changed. Under backflush-at-standard, the 1.6 % of labels that were consumed but not booked simply vanished from the accounts until the annual count, at which point they appeared as a single lump “inventory adjustment” that everyone attributed to counting error, pilferage or the weather. Booking actual consumption moves that loss out of the once-a-year lump and into a monthly variance line attached to the process that caused it — which is the entire purpose of a variance account. Making a loss visible is routinely mistaken for creating it, and this reaction is so predictable that you should warn the finance team before you make the change, not after. Option 4 is a real and worthwhile follow-up — standard cost should eventually be revised toward the observed 4.1 % — but doing it first would bury the evidence again. The correct sequence is: measure, expose the variance, run it for a full quarter, then revise the standard and watch the variance collapse toward zero. That collapse is your proof the fix worked.

The Seven Integrations — What Actually Crosses Each Boundary

FOUR MESSAGES, NOT FOUR HUNDRED CORE

ISA-95's most useful single contribution is the observation that the entire Level-4 to Level-3 conversation reduces to four categories of information. Everything a vendor sells as a bespoke interface is one of these four wearing a different name.

↓ Product definition

How to make it: BOM, routing, parameters, spec limits, the master recipe. Slow-moving, master-data-controlled, sent when it changes.

↓ Production schedule

What to make, how much, by when, on what. The work order. Sent daily or on release.

What we could do: which lines are available, their capacity and current commitments. Sent on request or on change — and the one most often skipped, which is why ERP schedules keep landing on machines that are down.

What we actually did: quantities good and scrapped, materials consumed, time used, who and which lot. The confirmation. This is where D1 and D4 both lived.

THREE CONCEPTS THAT DECIDE WHETHER AN INTERFACE WORKS STRETCH
  • 1
    Idempotency. An interface will retry — the network will drop an acknowledgement at least once a year. If the receiver books a confirmation twice, the plant appears to have made twice the product. The cure is a unique transaction identifier carried in the message, with the receiver keeping a short memory of the ones it has already applied. Design every upward message so that sending it twice is harmless. This single discipline prevents more integration incidents than any other.
  • 2
    Poll versus report-by-exception. Polling asks “what is it now?” on a fixed rhythm: simple, predictable load, and blind between polls (page 3). Report-by-exception sends only on change: far less traffic, far more responsive — and silent when broken, which is why every exception-based link needs a heartbeat so that “no news” can be distinguished from “no link”. Fault D2 in B07 was exactly this failure at a different layer.
  • 3
    Who owns the boundary. Every interface has two ends and, in most organisations, two owners — often two companies. The single most useful artefact on any integration project is a one-page interface specification naming, for each message: the trigger, the exact fields with units and types, the direction, the cadence, the transport, the error behaviour, the retry rule, and the person on each side who may change it. If that page does not exist, the interface is not designed, it is merely working.
💼

Vendors will not write it for you (it constrains them), and IT will not (they do not know the plant). Write it once for one interface, insist on it being signed by both ends, and you will find yourself invited to every subsequent integration meeting at the site.

Kelaniya's IT department refuses to open an inbound firewall port from the plant network to the corporate network for the new dashboard. The integrator says this makes the project impossible. Is it?

No — and this is the architectural reason MQTT conquered industrial telemetry. In a request/response design the consumer must reach into the plant, so somebody must open a hole pointing inward, and that hole is exactly what IEC 62443 zoning exists to prevent. In a publish/subscribe design the edge node dials out to a broker in the DMZ and the dashboard dials out to the same broker; both connections are outbound, both are TLS, and no inbound rule is needed in either direction. The broker becomes the only listener, and it can be hardened, monitored and placed exactly where the Purdue model wants it. The firewall argument is not an obstacle to the design; it is the reason for the design. Option 3 is a genuine fallback and is often right — keep the dashboard local — but it fails the moment two sites need one view. Option 4 confuses a network-layer tunnel with an application-layer pattern; a VPN carries the same inward-facing risk with a friendlier name, and IT is right to resist it.

The Unified Namespace — The Argument of the Decade, Both Sides

THE PROBLEM THAT CREATES IT CORE

Point-to-point integration has a combinatorial problem that every plant discovers at about the same size. Kelaniya has eight systems that need each other's data: three line SCADAs, a historian, an MES, an ERP, a CMMS and a laboratory system. Wire them directly and the arithmetic is not kind.

THE INTEGRATION-COUNT LAW Point to point, N systems fully connected: Ip2p = N(N − 1) / 2 ← each pair, once Through a hub, N systems each connect once: Ihub = N Crossover: N(N−1)/2 > N whenever N > 3 N = 4 p2p 6 hub 4 ratio 1.5× N = 8 p2p 28 hub 8 ratio 3.5× ← Kelaniya N = 12 p2p 66 hub 12 ratio 5.5× N = 15 p2p 105 hub 15 ratio 7.0× N = 20 p2p 190 hub 20 ratio 9.5× ...and every interface has TWO ends, usually owned by TWO organisations, so the real maintenance burden is M = 2 I → 56 owned ends at Kelaniya versus 16. That last paragraph is the honest core of it. Nobody ever built 28 interfaces at Kelaniya — they built six, and refused the other twenty-two, which is why the plant manager's dashboard was a spreadsheet. The cost of point-to-point is not paid in interfaces built; it is paid in questions never answered because the interface was not worth building.

WHAT A UNIFIED NAMESPACE ACTUALLY IS CORE

A Unified Namespace is an architectural pattern, not a product and not a standard. Its claim is simple: every system publishes its current state, by exception, into one hierarchically-named hub, and any authorised system may subscribe to any part of it. The hub — in practice an MQTT broker, usually speaking Sparkplug B — holds the present state of the business, addressed by a name that mirrors the physical plant.

The same eight systems. The picture on the left is what most plants actually have, minus the twenty-two links they could not afford.

Four properties follow, and they are the genuine benefits:

  • 1
    Producers do not know their consumers. Adding a dashboard, an analytics job or a new MES module requires no change on the plant floor — which means it requires no change-management, no downtime window and no controls engineer. This is the benefit that actually converts sceptics.
  • 2
    The hub holds current state, by exception. With Sparkplug birth certificates and retained messages, a consumer that connects at 03:00 immediately receives the present value of everything it subscribes to, without polling anything.
  • 3
    The namespace is the contract. CeylonBeverage/Kelaniya/Filling/Line2/Filler/BowlLevel_PV means the same thing to every system, for ever. That is a semantic achievement, not a networking one — page 14.
  • 4
    Outbound-only connections. The firewall argument from page 10, which makes the pattern acceptable to IT in a way that point-to-point never was.
THE HONEST COUNTER-ARGUMENT STRETCH

This is a live disagreement among competent engineers, and you should be able to argue it from either side. Six objections that deserve answers rather than enthusiasm:

1 · A hub is a single point of failure

Twenty-eight brittle links fail one at a time; one broker fails everything at once. The answer is clustering and a defined degraded mode — but that is now your availability problem, at Level 3, with no vendor to blame.

2 · A broker is not a historian

MQTT holds the latest value, not history. You still need the archive of page 5, and you must decide whether it subscribes to the hub or collects independently — two different truths if you get it wrong.

3 · It solves addressing, not semantics

Publishing .../GoodCount from three lines that define “good” differently produces fault D1 faster and more widely. The UNS makes bad definitions travel further, not less far.

4 · Transactions do not belong on it

Integrations 1–4 are ordered, acknowledged, idempotent business transactions. Publish/subscribe gives weak ordering and no natural acknowledgement. Telemetry on the hub; transactions over an API. A UNS that swallows work-order confirmations will eventually book a phantom batch.

5 · Modelling is the real work

Standing up a broker takes a day. Deciding what the hierarchy is, who owns each branch, what a metric is called and who may publish into it takes months — and it is exactly the work most projects skip, then blame the technology.

6 · It is not a product

Anyone selling you “a UNS” is selling you a broker plus consulting. That may be excellent value. It is not the same as buying a thing.

A defensible position, and the one to hold in an interview: use a unified namespace for telemetry and current state — integration #6, and the many analytical consumers that grow off it — and keep ordered business transactions on request/response APIs with transaction identifiers and acknowledgements. The pyramid and the hub are not rivals; they answer different questions, and a mature plant runs both. Anyone who tells you the ISA-95 hierarchy is obsolete has usually confused the functional model (still exactly correct) with the network model (which the hub genuinely does flatten).

💼

This page is interview material. “What do you think of the Unified Namespace?” is now a standard question for automation and digital-manufacturing roles, and the expected bad answers are enthusiasm without objections, or dismissal without understanding. An answer that states the N(N−1)/2 argument, names the four real benefits, then raises the transaction objection unprompted, will be the best answer that panel hears that week.

The Freshness Budget — The One Number That Decides Where a Function Lives

EVERY DECISION HAS A SHELF LIFE CORE

Ask of any proposed feature: how old is the data allowed to be at the instant the decision is made? That number — the required freshness — is the single most useful question in industrial software architecture, because it settles, without argument or vendor involvement, which level of the stack the function belongs at.

Data does not arrive stale by accident. Staleness is accumulated, contribution by contribution, along a chain that is entirely predictable once you have seen it drawn. And the number that matters is the worst case, not the average — because a decision that is correct 95 % of the time and catastrophically late 5 % of the time is a decision that has not been designed.

One further consequence worth naming, because it explains a whole genre of failed project. Do the freshness arithmetic on a napkin at the requirements meeting; it takes four minutes and it is the highest-return four minutes in the whole engagement.

In most rooms, neither number has ever been said aloud. You will have reframed the entire discussion in one sentence, and everything after it will be about engineering rather than about product features.

Six Ways Plant Data Lies — and the Test That Catches Each

NOT ERRORS. LIES. CORE

An error is a number that is wrong and looks wrong. A lie is a number that is wrong and looks perfectly reasonable — it has the right units, it sits in the right range, it moves plausibly, and it will be averaged into a KPI and presented to a board. Every fault in this primer is a lie in that sense, and there are only six species of them. Learn the six and you can audit any plant's data in an afternoon.

Four systems, four numbers, four different lies, one shift. Every number is defensible inside its own system. No component has failed.

THE SIX, WITH THEIR TESTS CORE
  • 1
    DEFINITION — the same name, two meanings. “Good count”, “downtime”, “available”, “yield”, “on hand”. The test: ask three people in three departments to define the term, separately, in writing. If any two differ you have found a live fault. The cure: a data dictionary with a named owner per term, and the awkward second question — measured where, exactly?
  • 2
    TIMESTAMP — the right value at the wrong instant. Three separate questions: who stamped it (device, collector or archive — only the device is truth), on what clock (NTP-disciplined? within what error?), and in what zone. The test: compare the same event's timestamp in two systems, now. The Sri Lankan trap: the country is UTC+05:30, and a distressing amount of software assumes whole-hour offsets — a system that stores +05:00 puts every event 30 minutes early, which is enough to land a changeover in the wrong batch and a night-shift crate on the wrong day.
  • 3
    RESOLUTION — true, but too coarse to answer with. Fault D2. Also integer scaling that rounds a 0.05 % band to zero, and any KPI derived from an hourly roll-up being asked a per-minute question. The test: state the decision threshold, then state the archive's guaranteed error band. If the second is more than a fifth of the first, the data cannot support the decision.
  • 4
    GAP — missing data that arrives as a plausible number. The deadliest of the six, because it is invisible. A tag with BAD quality read by something that ignores quality becomes 0.0, and zero averages beautifully.
  • 5
    DUPLICATION — the same fact counted twice. A retried confirmation, two collectors writing the same tag, a shift report entered by both the outgoing and incoming supervisor. The test: is there a transaction identifier, and does the receiver reject repeats? If the answer is “it has never happened”, the answer is no. The cure: idempotency, from page 9.
  • 6
    RECONCILIATION — several truths and no rule. Not a fault of any one number but of the estate: three systems hold a fact, none is declared master, and no tolerance is published. The test: ask “which system is right when these disagree, and by how much are they allowed to?” The cure: name a master per fact and publish a tolerance, e.g. “MES is master for production count; ERP must agree within 0.5 % at month end; a larger gap raises a ticket.” A tolerance you have written down is a control. A tolerance in somebody's head is a rumour.
THE ZERO CATASTROPHE, WITH NUMBERS STRETCH

Lie 4 deserves its own arithmetic, because it is the one that reaches the boardroom most often and is diagnosed correctly least often.

A COMMS DROP MASQUERADING AS A PERFORMANCE LOSS True line rate 22,000 bph Nameplate 24,000 bph TRUE performance 22,000 / 24,000 = 91.67 % Now suppose 5 % of the one-second samples come back with quality BAD, and the reader treats BAD as 0.0: mean = 0.95 × 22,000 + 0.05 × 0 = 20,900 bph apparent performance 20,900/24,000 = 87.08 % A 4.58 percentage-point "performance loss" that is entirely a NETWORK availability figure wearing a production costume. Six months of engineering effort will be spent on the filler. THE FIX IS ONE LINE OF ARITHMETIC: exclude BAD samples from the mean instead of valuing them at zero, and report the EXCLUSION RATE as its own KPI: mean = Σ(good values) / count(good samples) = 22,000 data availability = 95.0 % Two honest numbers instead of one dishonest one.

Every aggregate should be published with its own data-availability figure beside it. “Line 2 performance 91.7 %, computed from 95.0 % of expected samples” is a professional statement. “Line 2 performance 87.1 %” is a guess dressed as a measurement, and it is what almost every dashboard in the world displays.

Context and Naming — What Turns a Number Into Information

FIVE CONTEXTS, OR IT IS JUST A NUMBER CORE

A value on its own is useless. 18.4 is not information. 18.4 % level, in the Line 2 filler bowl, at 03:14:22 local time, during work order 4471-02, running batch L2-2611-04, under operator Perera is information — and every one of those five contexts must come from somewhere, be stored with the number, and survive the journey up the stack.

Equipment

Which physical thing produced it. Comes from the asset model. Without it, aggregating across three lines is meaningless.

Time

When, on which clock, in which zone. Page 13's second lie lives here.

Material

Which lot, which product, which recipe version. Comes from Level 3; it is what makes genealogy possible.

Order

Which work order or campaign. Turns a measurement into something that can be costed.

Person

Who was responsible, and what they were allowed to do. Required in regulated industries; useful everywhere.

…and quality

Not a context but a companion: was the number itself trustworthy at the moment it was taken? Page 13, lie 4.

Notice where each context comes from. Equipment context is architectural — it is decided once and lives in a model. Material, order and person contexts are transactional — they change every few hours and must be injected by Level 3. A plant that has only equipment context can do OEE and condition monitoring. A plant that has all five can do genealogy, costing, and root-cause analysis that spans systems. The gap between those two plants is almost entirely an MES, which is the honest answer to “why do we need one?”

THE EQUIPMENT HIERARCHY — ISA-95 AND ISA-88 CORE

Both standards share the top three levels and then diverge according to whether your plant is discrete or process. Learn the shape; the vocabulary follows.

Three shared levels, then a fork by plant type, then a shared bottom. Kelaniya has both branches under one site, which is normal and is why the model must allow it.

One subtlety that separates people who have read the standard from people who have heard of it: ISA-95 distinguishes equipment from equipment class, and lets a work order be dispatched to a class (“any 400 ml capable line”) rather than a specific machine. The one-platform fallacy. Every large vendor now offers “a single platform from sensor to boardroom”, and it is a genuinely attractive proposition — one throat to choke, one data model, one training budget. It is also, by page 2's argument, an offer to be excellent across five time scales separated by twelve orders of magnitude, which no engineering organisation has ever managed. In practice one or two layers of any single-vendor stack are excellent and the rest are adequate. That may still be the correct purchase — adequate-and-integrated frequently beats excellent-and-isolated — but buy it knowing which layers you are compromising on, and make the vendor tell you which ones they think those are. A vendor who cannot answer that question honestly is telling you something about the next five years.

EDGE OR CLOUD — ANSWERED BY PAGE 12, NOT BY FASHION STRETCH

The debate is usually conducted as a preference. It is a calculation, and you already have it.

Keep it at the edge when…

The required freshness is under a few seconds; the function must work when the link is down; the data volume is large and the conclusions are small (compute where the data is); the data is commercially or legally sensitive; or connectivity is metered and unreliable.

Send it to the cloud when…

The question spans sites; the compute is bursty (training a model, a quarterly analysis); the consumers are many and distributed; you need elasticity you cannot justify buying; or you want somebody else to run the backups.

A pattern that resolves most of it: edge decides, cloud learns. The loop closes locally in bounded time; the model that improves the loop is trained centrally on months of data from many sites and pushed down as parameters. That split respects both the freshness budget and the economics, and it is what almost every serious industrial-analytics architecture converges on regardless of vendor.

Security is not a later phase. Whatever you choose, the minimum is not negotiable and is cheap if done first: a current asset inventory (you cannot protect what you have not listed), network segmentation with no path from a plant device to the open internet, outbound-only connections wherever data must leave (page 10), controlled remote access with named accounts and multi-factor, tested restores — not backups, restores — and a realistic patching policy that acknowledges you cannot reboot a controller on Tuesdays. IEC 62443 gives the framework; A54 gives the depth.

SIX WAYS A SOFTWARE STACK FAILS CORE
⚠ THE UNSTATED DEFINITION

One word, two meanings, no owner. Costs the most, shows up as an unexplained gap between two reports, and is always diagnosed as “a system problem”. Test: ask three departments to define it in writing.

⚠ THE ARCHIVE ON DEFAULTS

The resolution of your quality evidence set by a purchasing decision about a transmitter range. Irreversible, silent, and only discovered when an investigation needs the data. Test: compare the deviation band with the decision threshold.

⚠ THE SHADOW SYSTEM

The undesigned eighth integration: a person, a spreadsheet and a night's delay. It is always the busiest interface on the site and it is on nobody's architecture diagram. Test: follow one number from screen to board pack and count the keystrokes.

⚠ THE COMPUTABLE FUSE

Backflush at standard, or any other systematic bias against a fixed buffer. It fails on a schedule you can calculate — and therefore on a schedule you can pre-empt. Test: N = cover / (P(sa − ss)).

⚠ BAD QUALITY VALUED AT ZERO

A communications availability figure wearing a production costume, and the reason for a great many wasted mechanical investigations. Test: does the aggregation exclude BAD, and is the exclusion rate published?

⚠ THE PHANTOM BATCH

A lost acknowledgement, a retried confirmation, and production booked twice. Rare, catastrophic for trust, entirely preventable. Test: does every upward message carry a transaction ID that the receiver checks?

WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — SIZING AN ARCHIVE, AND CHOOSING ITS RESOLUTION CORE

Tap for the point of the example ↓ The answer is not the storage number, it is the last block. The archive comparison is arithmetic and any engineer can do it. WORKED EXAMPLE 5 — THE MARGINAL INTERFACE STRETCH

A group site runs 9 systems with 11 point-to-point interfaces built (of 36 possible pairs). A tenth system — an energy-monitoring platform — must read from six of the nine. Compare the point-to-point and hub approaches, and state the decision rule.

POINT TO POINT, this project only 6 new interfaces × 2 ends = 12 newly owned ends each needs: spec, build, test, monitor, and an owner on both sides who will still be here in 2029. HUB, if the six already publish 1 new interface × 2 ends = 2 newly owned ends ...but ONLY if they already publish. If they do not, the first hub project pays for 6 publishers as well, and is therefore NOT cheaper on project one. Hub: 6 + 1, then 1, then 1. cumulative p2p = 6, 12, 18 interfaces cumulative hub = 7, 8, 9 Crossover at project 2; by project 3 the hub is 2× cheaper and the gap keeps widening.
Tap for the discussion ↓ Two honest points that separate a good answer from a slogan. First, the hub is not cheaper on the first project, and pretending otherwise is how architecture arguments lose credibility with a finance committee. Present it as an investment with a stated crossover — project two — and it becomes a normal capital case. Second, the arithmetic assumes the next three projects exist. If this energy platform is genuinely the last integration the site will ever do, six point-to-point links are the correct engineering answer and the hub is over-engineering. Architecture is a bet on the number of future consumers. PROBLEM SET — TWELVE QUESTIONS CORE

Work each on paper before revealing. Several have a deliberate trap; two have no single numeric answer and are marked as such.

P1. A capper produces reject bursts lasting about 250 ms. Your SCADA polls at 500 ms. You want to (a) detect that bursts occur and (b) measure their duration to within 10 %. What poll intervals are required, and what should you actually do?

Tap to reveal ↓(a) T < d/2 = 125 ms. (b) T < d/10 = 25 ms. The 500 ms poll fails both. What to actually do: neither — do not chase the poll rate. Count and time the bursts in the PLC (a counter plus a min/max/accumulated-duration trio) and publish four summary tags. Polling at 25 ms across a plant network is fragile, expensive and still not guaranteed; a controller counter is exact and free. This is page 3's design rule, and it is the expected answer.

P2. A tank level transmitter is ranged 0–10 m. The control decision that matters is a ±40 mm band around 6.2 m. The historian is set to 0.5 % of span compression. Can the archive support post-incident analysis of that band?

Tap to reveal ↓E = 0.005 × 10 m = ±50 mm on a ±40 mm decision band. No. The guaranteed error exceeds the entire band, so a stored value of 6.200 could truly have been anywhere from 6.150 to 6.250 — outside the band in both directions. Required, using the threshold/20 rule of thumb against the 80 mm band: E ≤ 4 mm, i.e. 0.04 % of span — a twelvefold tightening. Note the general lesson: a wide-range transmitter used for a narrow-band decision is a data-quality problem long before it is a metrology problem.

P3. A bottle counter is declared as a signed 16-bit INT and increments at 22,000/h. How long until it misbehaves, and what does the reading system see? How long would a DINT last?

Tap to reveal ↓32,767 / 22,000 = 1.49 h = 89.4 minutes, then it wraps to −32,768. A reader computing Δ = now − last sees a difference of about −65,535 — which, unhandled, either posts a huge negative production figure or is silently clamped to zero, losing an hour and a half of output every hour and a half. DINT: 2,147,483,647 / 22,000 = 97,613 h = 11.1 years. The trap: DINT does not remove the requirement to handle rollover — it merely makes the failure rare enough to be forgotten by the time it happens, which is worse. Always write the modulo-difference explicitly.

P4. A site wants 4,200 tags at 1 Hz kept for five years. Compare a naive relational store (65 B/row) with a historian (12 B/point, 25:1). Then say which number should decide the architecture.

Tap to reveal ↓Relational: 4,200 × 65 = 273,000 B/s → 8.61 TB/year → 43.1 TB over five years, at 4,200 inserts/s (363 million rows/day). Historian: 4,200 × 12 / 25 = 2,016 B/s → 63.6 GB/year → 318 GB over five years. Ratio 135×. Which number decides: not the disk cost — disk is cheap and this is the answer most people give. It is the restore time and the query time. Restoring 43 TB onto a plant server takes days during which the plant has no history; restoring 318 GB takes an hour. And “one tag, five years” scans 76 MB in the historian against roughly 10 GB in the naive table. Architecture decisions should be argued on recovery and query behaviour, because those are the ones you experience at 03:00.

P5. A quality hold decision must be made within 5 minutes of a spec excursion. Chain: sensor 100 ms, scan 50 ms, LIMS sample and test 240 s, result entry 60 s, MES processing 5 s, notification 30 s, human 90 s. Does it meet the requirement, and what is the only useful lever?

Tap to reveal ↓Total = 0.1 + 0.05 + 240 + 60 + 5 + 30 + 90 = 425.15 s = 7.09 min against 300 s required — fails by 42 %. The laboratory test alone is 240 s, i.e. 80 % of the entire budget, and no amount of software touches it. The only useful levers are (a) an at-line or in-line measurement replacing the laboratory step — an instrument purchase, not an IT project — or (b) renegotiating the requirement, since 5 minutes may have been chosen without anyone costing it. Result entry at 60 s is worth automating (an interface from the analyser, integration #7) but on its own takes the total to 6.09 min, still failing. The expected professional answer is to report that the requirement is not achievable with a laboratory step in the loop, with the arithmetic attached — not to build something that misses it quietly.

P6. Caps: production 9.43 million/month, standard scrap 0.8 %, actual 1.9 %, cover 7 days, annual physical count. Compute the fuse. Then explain why doubling production does not change it.

Tap to reveal ↓D = 9,430,000 × 0.011 = 103,730/month. C_a = 9,430,000 × 1.019 = 9,609,170. SS = 9,609,170 × 7/26 = 2,587,161. N = 2,587,161 / 103,730 = 24.9 months — longer than the count interval, so caps are not the exposed item; labels at 4 days cover and a 1.6 % gap are (10.0 months, page 8). Why production cancels: substituting, N = [P(1+s_a) d / 26] / [P(s_a − s_s)] = d(1+s_a) / (26(s_a − s_s)) — P appears in both numerator and denominator and vanishes. Only cover, the scrap gap and the counting calendar matter. Growth buys you nothing; it merely makes the eventual stock-out bigger.

P7. A plant reports OEE 68.0 % on scheduled time against nameplate. Management asks for 78 % and the site delivers it in four months with no change in units shipped. Reconstruct at least three base changes that could have done it, and state the control that prevents this.

Tap to reveal ↓Since OEE = G/(T×R), with G fixed at the shipped quantity, a 14.7 % relative rise requires T×R to fall by 12.8 %. Any of these does it: (1) reclassify changeovers as planned downtime, cutting T; (2) re-base R from nameplate to a “demonstrated” or routing rate 10–15 % lower; (3) move the good-count point upstream so G rises instead (fault D1 — note this changes G, not T×R, and is the easiest of the three to sell); (4) exclude a chronically bad shift or product from the calculation as “non-representative”. The control: publish the three bases with the number, freeze them for the target period, and require a signed change note — treated exactly like a drawing revision. Additionally, always publish the underlying units shipped next to the percentage; an index that moves while its own numerator does not is self-diagnosing.

P8. A group has 14 systems. Compute the fully-connected point-to-point interface count, the owned-end count, and the hub equivalents. Then give the one number that actually persuades a steering committee.

Tap to reveal ↓14 × 13 / 2 = 91 interfaces, 182 owned ends. Hub: 14 interfaces, 28 ends — a ratio of 6.5×. The persuasive number is none of those, because no one has ever proposed building 91 interfaces. It is the marginal one: adding the fifteenth system costs 14 new interfaces point-to-point and 1 through the hub. Steering committees do not fund a redesign of what already works; they fund the next project. Frame the argument entirely on the cost of the next three integrations (worked example 5) and it converts.

P9. A line truly runs at 1,450 units/h. Eight per cent of one-second samples return BAD quality and the reporting layer treats BAD as 0. What average is reported, what is the apparent loss, and what two numbers should be published instead?

Tap to reveal ↓Reported mean = 0.92 × 1,450 = 1,334 units/h — an apparent 8.0 % production loss that does not exist. Against a nameplate of, say, 1,600, the performance factor reads 83.4 % instead of the true 90.6 %: 7.2 percentage points of phantom loss, which is more than most improvement programmes ever deliver. Publish two numbers: the mean over good samples only (1,450) and the data availability, 92.0 %. Note the second is a network and collector KPI and belongs to a different owner — which is exactly why merging it into the production figure is so damaging: it hides one team's problem inside another team's number.

P10. A cloud analytics platform stores Sri Lankan plant events with a +05:00 offset because the developer's library only handled whole hours. Shifts run 06:00–14:00, 14:00–22:00, 22:00–06:00 local. What is the consequence, and how would you detect it in five minutes?

Tap to reveal ↓Sri Lanka is UTC+05:30. Storing +05:00 makes every event appear 30 minutes earlier in UTC than it was, so when converted back for reporting every event is displaced by half an hour. Consequences: the last 30 minutes of every shift is attributed to the next shift; changeovers land in the wrong batch, so genealogy is wrong for the products made in that window; and any 15-minute-bucket analysis is systematically shifted by two buckets. Five-minute detection: take one event with an unambiguous physical time — a line start, a planned CIP, anything with a witness or a paper log — and compare its rendered timestamp in the platform with the wall clock. A half-hour discrepancy is unmistakable. Do this once for every new system on the day it is commissioned; it takes five minutes and it is the highest-yield acceptance test there is. Note also that Sri Lanka observes no daylight saving, which removes one class of bug and hides another: nobody ever tests the offset code, because it never changes.

P11. An MES posts a production confirmation to ERP over HTTP. The response is lost in the network; the MES retries after 30 s and receives a success. What has happened, what did it cost, and give three designs that prevent it — ranked.

Tap to reveal ↓The first request very probably succeeded; only the acknowledgement was lost. The retry posts a second confirmation, so the order is confirmed twice: production over-stated, and — worse — the backflush consumes materials twice, so book stock now under-states reality by a full order's bill of materials. It corrupts three things at once: output, inventory and cost. Ranked designs: (1) Idempotency key — the MES generates a unique transaction ID per logical confirmation and ERP rejects a repeat with the same ID. Best: correct under any failure, needs no coordination beyond a field. (2) Query-before-retry — on timeout, ask ERP whether the document exists before resending. Workable, but there is a race window and it doubles the interfaces. (3) Reconciliation report — detect duplicates after the fact and reverse them. Weakest: it is a cure, not a prevention, and it depends on someone reading a report. Note that all three are cheaper than the first incident, and only the first is a design rather than a hope.

P12. Kelaniya makes 362,861 bottles a day; one concentrate bag doses a 12,000 L batch of 30,000 bottles. Full cost is LKR 24.60/bottle. A supplier reports a contaminated bag. Compute the recall exposure with day-level and with lot-level records, and state the two conditions under which the lot-level number is not achieved.

Tap to reveal ↓Day-level: 362,861 × 24.60 = LKR 8,926,380. Lot-level: 30,000 × 24.60 = LKR 738,000. Difference LKR 8,188,380 per event, ratio 12.1×. The two conditions under which lot-level fails to deliver: (1) The genealogy is backward-only. Answering “what went into this pallet?” does not answer “where did this bag go?” — and the recall needs the forward query, which must chase one input through blends, rework and re-packs. Many systems sold as traceability solutions do only the first. (2) The chain is broken at a merge point. If bulk product from two batches is combined in a surge tank with no record of the mix, the lot identity is destroyed at that vessel and everything downstream reverts to day-level. Genealogy is only as fine as its coarsest link, and the coarsest link is almost always a tank, a hopper or a rework loop that nobody modelled. Identify those three on a P&ID before you specify a traceability system — which is precisely the skill from B07.

DERIVATION 1 — THE ARCHIVE LAWS STRETCH
STORAGE N tags at frequency f, b bytes per stored point, compression ratio k : B/s = N f b / k per year = N f b × 31,536,000 / k For a naive relational store, k = 1 and b includes row overhead and index entries, which is why b jumps from ~12 to ~65 — a 5.4× penalty BEFORE compression is considered. The two effects multiply, which is where the observed 80–135× ratios come from. RECONSTRUCTION ERROR — WHY THE BOUND IS EXACTLY E The swinging door archives point A, then admits every subsequent sample P_i for which there EXISTS a straight line from A passing within ±E of every P_j, j ≤ i. The moment no such line exists, P_(i−1) is archived. Therefore, by construction, for every discarded point: | v_true − v_reconstructed | ≤ E ...and nothing stronger can be said. In particular: • the error is NOT reduced by sampling faster. More samples merely give the wedge more chances to stay open, so MORE points are discarded, each still within ±E. Faster sampling changes the compression RATIO, not the error BOUND. (This is page 1's predict card.) • a PEAK is the worst case for this bound, because a peak is precisely where a straight line through its neighbours is furthest from the truth. A peak of height h above the local trend survives the archive only if h > E. Set E below the smallest feature you must be able to see. • the max-time rule forces an extra point after T_max seconds regardless. It bounds the GAP, never the ERROR. THE THREE INDEPENDENT LOSSES, IN ORDER sample interval → what was never observed exception band → what was never sent compression E → what was never stored (+ roll-up → what was later deleted) Effective resolution = the WORST of the four.
DERIVATION 2 — THE INTEGRATION-COUNT LAW STRETCH
N systems. A point-to-point interface joins an unordered pair, so the number of possible interfaces is the number of 2-subsets of an N-set: Ip2p = C(N,2) = N(N − 1)/2 Θ(N²) Through a hub every system connects once: Ihub = N Θ(N) MARGINAL COST — the number that actually decides Adding the (N+1)th system: ΔIp2p = C(N+1,2) − C(N,2) = N ΔIhub = 1 So the point-to-point marginal cost GROWS with the estate while the hub marginal cost is constant. This, not the total, is the engineering argument: a plant never builds all C(N,2) links, it builds a few and then refuses the rest — so the true cost of point-to-point is paid in questions never answered, which appear in no budget. MAINTENANCE BURDEN Each interface has 2 ends; each end has an owner, and in practice ends are owned by different organisations: M = 2 I, and organisational pairs = I CROSSOVER N(N−1)/2 > N ⇔ N − 1 > 2 ⇔ N > 3 Above three systems the hub wins on count alone. Below three it is pure overhead. Most real arguments occur at N = 5–9, where the ratio is 2–3.5× and both sides can quote a true number — which is why the marginal formulation, not the total, should be used. WHEN THE LAW DOES NOT APPLY If only k of the N systems ever need to exchange data, Ip2p = C(k,2), not C(N,2). Quoting the full N² figure when k = 4 is dishonest, and a competent CIO will catch it.
DERIVATION 3 — PLACEMENT AND OBSERVATION ARE DIFFERENT LAWS STRETCH
PLACEMENT — a sum of worst cases Staleness accumulates along a serial chain, and because the contributions are sequential and independent, the worst case of the chain is the SUM of the worst cases: Sworst = tsensor + tscan + tcollect + tnet + tproc + tpresent + thuman Requirement: Sworst ≤ Frequired Two cautions that separate this from a naive addition: • tcollect is the FULL poll interval, not half of it. The average wait for the next poll is T/2, but the worst case is T — and a requirement is a statement about the worst case. Using T/2 understates every budget by half the largest term. • retries multiply, they do not add. A transport with r retries and timeout τ contributes up to (r+1)τ. OBSERVATION — a comparison, not a sum To DETECT an event of duration d, at least one sample must fall inside it. With uniform sampling at T, a sample is guaranteed inside the event iff T < d ; guaranteed to catch its shape at all iff T < d/2 (Nyquist, in its practical plant form). To MEASURE the duration to within ε: T < εd, so ε = 10 % gives T < d/10. WHY THEY ARE CONFUSED, AND WHY IT MATTERS Both are "about time", but one bounds an AGE and the other bounds a RESOLUTION. A 1 s poll can keep a 60 s decision perfectly fresh (placement passes) while being blind to a 40 ms burst inside it (observation fails). Conversely a 10 ms controller counter observes the burst perfectly and may report it hours late (observation passes, placement fails). Every requirement must be checked against BOTH. The commonest error in industrial specifications is to answer one of them and believe both have been answered.
DERIVATION 4 — THE BACKFLUSH FUSE, AND WHY GROWTH DOES NOT HELP STRETCH
Production P per month. True scrap sa, standard ss. Reality consumes Ca = P(1 + sa) The book consumes Cs = P(1 + ss) Monthly divergence D = Ca − Cs = P(sa − ss) Cover of d days held against actual consumption: SS = Ca d / 26 = P(1 + sa) d / 26 Months until the physical shelf empties while the book still shows stock: N = SS / D = [P(1 + sa) d / 26] / [P(sa − ss)] P CANCELS: N = d (1 + sa) / (26 (sa − ss)) CONSEQUENCES 1 The fuse length is INDEPENDENT of output. Doubling the plant doubles both the error and the buffer. Growth changes the SIZE of the eventual stock-out, never its TIMING. 2 N is linear in cover d. Halving inventory halves the fuse — a working-capital decision is silently a data-control decision (worked example 3). 3 N is hyperbolic in the scrap gap. Closing the gap from 1.6 to 0.4 points does not shorten the exposure by a quarter; it lengthens the fuse FOURFOLD. 4 The failure is CERTAIN and DATED. Compare N with the counting interval; if N < interval, the stock-out is not a risk, it is a schedule. SANITY CHECK — Kelaniya labels d = 4, sa = 0.041, ss = 0.025 N = 4(1.041) / (26 × 0.016) = 4.164 / 0.416 = 10.01 months ✓ matches page 8 exactly.
ELEVEN EXAM TRAPS CORE
#The trapThe correct statement
1Using HMI, SCADA and DCS as synonymsAn HMI is a window onto one controller's truth; a SCADA keeps its own copy of many controllers' truths; a DCS never separated controller from screen. The differences are where the tag database lives and what happens when it dies.
2“Real time means fast”Real time means bounded. A 10 ms scan matters because it is 10 ms in the worst case. An MES that averages 40 ms and occasionally takes 9 s is not faster or slower — it is a different category of promise, and it may not hold an interlock.
3Treating the historian as a backup or as losslessCompression is lossy and irreversible. The discarded points do not exist anywhere. It is the only decision in the stack that cannot be revisited, so it deserves more care than the expensive software did.
4Quoting a deadband as a percentage without saying of what±1 % of span, of reading, and in engineering units are three different settings that coincide only by accident. Convert to engineering units and set it beside the decision threshold.
5“Should we use OPC UA or MQTT?”Wrong question — they are not the same kind of thing. OPC UA is an information model (which also has transports, including a pub/sub one); MQTT is a transport with no model. Many plants correctly run both.
6Believing Sparkplug B replaces MQTTSparkplug B is MQTT, plus a mandated topic namespace, a typed payload, and a birth/death session model. Its real contribution is that a subscriber can always tell whether a value is still true.
7Buying “a Unified Namespace”It is an architectural pattern, not a product, and it solves addressing and transport — not semantics. Publishing three incompatible definitions of “good count” into a UNS spreads the fault further, faster.
8Conflating ISA-95 levels with Purdue levelsISA-95 is a functional model (which activities belong where) and says nothing about networks. Purdue is a network zoning model, and is where the DMZ lives. One server can host Level-2 and Level-3 functions inside one Purdue zone without contradiction.
9“Backflush at standard is close enough”The error is systematic and one-directional, so it accumulates. Its fuse length is computable: N = d(1+sa)/(26(sa−ss)). If N is shorter than the counting interval, the stock-out is a schedule, not a risk.
10Treating a timestamp as a single factThree separate questions: who stamped it (device / collector / archive — only the device is truth), on what clock (disciplined to what accuracy), and in what zone. Sri Lanka is UTC+05:30; software that assumes whole hours displaces every event by 30 minutes.
11“Our network is 1 ms, so our data is real time”Network latency is one of six or seven terms and is usually the smallest. Quote Sworst, the whole chain including presentation and the human, or quote nothing.

A twelfth, for completeness, because it is the one that ends careers rather than exams: assuming that because two systems agree, they are right. Two systems agree most easily when one of them is copying the other. Ask where each number was independently derived before treating agreement as confirmation.
