When Single-Loop PID Runs Out

Advanced control is not "better tuning". It is changing the structure of the problem so that ordinary tuning can succeed.

A single feedback loop has exactly one lever and one measurement, and it can only act after an error has appeared. That is enough for the large majority of loops. But there are five specific situations where no amount of tuning helps, because the limitation is structural — and each one has a named remedy. Recognising which of the five you are looking at is the whole skill of this module.

The Decision Map
The Iron Rule: Base Layer First
  • 1
    Advanced control sits on top of the regulatory layer and inherits all its faults. An MPC whose sub-loops have sticking valves will perform worse than the PID alone, because it assumes its setpoints are being followed. Industry surveys repeatedly find that a large fraction of loops in a typical plant are running in manual or performing poorly — fix that first and much of the "need" for advanced control evaporates.
  • 2
    Every advanced scheme adds a failure mode. Cascade adds a mode-management problem; feedforward adds a model that can go stale; select schemes add windup traps; MPC adds a model to maintain and an engineer to keep. Before adding structure, ask: who will own this in three years?
  • 3
    Quantify the benefit before building. Reduced variability translates into money in one of three ways: (a) operate closer to a quality or safety limit, (b) reduce energy or reagent per tonne, (c) increase throughput. If you cannot name which one and estimate it, the project has no defence at the next budget review.
  • 4
    The variability argument. If a product must stay above a minimum specification, the achievable average is limit + k·σ. Halving σ lets you move the average down toward the limit — that saved margin is the money. This "same limit, less standard deviation" picture is how every advanced-control benefits case is presented.
Benefit arithmetic, the version that convinces a plant manager:
Saving = (k·Δσ) × (value per unit of the give-away) × throughput
e.g. a dryer held at 2.5% moisture against a 2.0% minimum, with σ = 0.25% and a 2σ safety margin. Halving σ to 0.125% lets the target drop to 2.25%, i.e. 0.25% less water evaporated on every tonne — a direct, calculable energy saving. Present this, not the block diagram.
Quick check: A plant proposes an MPC for a distillation column. Audit of the base layer shows 6 of 14 loops in manual, two control valves with visible stiction, and no loop tuning records. What do you recommend?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Postpone the MPC and fix the base layer. An MPC manipulates the setpoints of regulatory loops; if six of them are in manual, the MPC has no actuators — it would be writing setpoints to controllers nobody is following, and the operators will switch it off within a week. The two sticking valves are worse than useless: MPC identifies its model from plant tests, so stiction will be baked into the model as process dynamics, and every prediction thereafter will be wrong. Recommended sequence: (1) repair the two valves and travel-test every final element in the scheme; (2) bump-test and tune all 14 loops, documenting K/τ/θ, the operating point, filters and scan times; (3) get the loops accepted by operations and left in automatic for a sustained period — that alone often captures a good share of the available benefit; (4) then re-evaluate whether MPC is still justified, and if so run the step tests for model identification on a healthy plant. Also worth saying out loud: the honest reason six loops are in manual is usually that they misbehave, and each one is a diagnosis waiting to be made (stiction, wrong tuning, bad measurement, or a strategy that never suited the process). The professional framing: "the MPC business case is real, but it is currently sitting on a foundation that will make it fail — here is a three-month base-layer programme that captures part of the benefit immediately and de-risks the rest."

Cascade Control

The highest-value, lowest-risk structural change in process control: put a fast loop inside a slow one, and the slow loop stops seeing most of the plant's dead time.

The Architecture
What Cascade Actually Buys — Four Distinct Benefits

① Inner disturbances rejected locally

Coolant supply temperature, header pressure, valve nonlinearity and stiction are all handled by the fast inner loop before the outer PV even moves. This is usually the largest single benefit.

② Effective dead time reduced

The outer loop's "process" is now the closed inner loop plus the slow section — the valve and jacket dynamics have been absorbed, so the outer loop can be tuned tighter.

③ Nonlinearity linearised

A valve with a bad installed characteristic, or a heat-transfer gain that varies with flow, is inside the inner loop's own feedback — so the outer loop sees a much more linear actuator.

④ Constraint enforcement

The inner setpoint can be clamped (e.g. jacket never below 5 °C to avoid freezing, never above 90 °C to protect the product) — a limit that is invisible to a single loop.

The Rules of Cascade
  • 1
    The inner loop must be substantially faster than the outer — a common requirement is 3× to 10× (inner τ or dead time at least three times smaller). If the two are of similar speed the loops will interact and fight; if the inner is not faster, cascade gains nothing and adds complexity.
  • 2
    The inner variable must lie on the path between the valve and the outer PV, and must respond to the valve promptly and monotonically. Jacket temperature, flow, pressure, drive speed and current are the classic inner variables. Flow is the most common inner loop in industry — it linearises the valve almost completely.
  • 3
    Tune inner first, then outer — always, with the outer in manual. The outer loop's model depends on the closed inner loop, so re-tuning the inner invalidates the outer tuning. Document both.
  • 4
    Inner loop: tight, often P-only or PI with little reset. The inner loop does not need zero offset — the outer loop's integral action will trim its setpoint. Excess reset in the inner loop just adds lag the outer loop must fight.
  • 5
    Handle the windup problem. If the inner loop saturates (valve fully open) or is switched to manual/local, the outer loop is writing a setpoint nobody is following, and its integral will wind up. The standard solutions are external reset feedback (feed the actual inner PV back into the outer controller's integral, so it "knows" what really happened) or an inner-to-outer status signal that freezes the outer integral. Every DCS provides one of these — configure it.
  • 6
    Get the mode logic right. Inner in cascade (setpoint from outer) versus auto (local setpoint) versus manual; the transitions must be bumpless in both directions, and operators must understand what "the cascade is broken" means. More cascade schemes are defeated by confusing mode behaviour than by bad tuning.

Quick check: A temperature loop manipulating a steam valve is cascaded to a steam flow inner loop. The steam header pressure swings badly as other users start and stop. Explain precisely what the cascade does about that, and what it does not fix.
Tap to reveal ↓A: What it fixes: a header pressure change alters the flow through a given valve opening — for a single loop that is an unmeasured disturbance which only reveals itself when the temperature has already drifted, many minutes later. With a flow inner loop, the flow transmitter sees the change within a second or two and the inner controller repositions the valve to restore the commanded flow before the temperature moves at all. The outer loop is effectively commanding kg/h of steam rather than valve percent, so header pressure and valve characteristic both disappear from its problem. This also linearises the actuator: the outer loop's gain no longer changes with valve position or ΔP, which was a major source of "stable at low load, oscillating at high load". What it does not fix: (1) if the header pressure falls so far that the required flow is unachievable even with the valve fully open, the inner loop saturates — the disturbance is now beyond the scheme's authority, and the outer loop will wind up unless external reset feedback is configured. That is a capacity problem, not a control problem. (2) It does nothing about disturbances entering downstream of the flow measurement — a change in feed rate or ambient loss still has to be caught by the slow outer loop (that is what feedforward on Page 3 is for). (3) It does not remove the outer loop's own dead time and lag; the reactor or exchanger is still slow. The professional summary: cascade removes everything between the valve and the inner measurement from the outer loop's problem — no more and no less. Pick the inner variable to enclose the disturbances and nonlinearities you actually have.

Feedforward Control

Feedback waits for an error. Feedforward acts on the cause. Used together they are the most cost-effective pair in process control.

If a disturbance can be measured before its effect appears, you do not have to wait for it to spoil the product. Feedforward computes the corrective move directly from the disturbance measurement, using a model of the process. Because it is open loop, it cannot destabilise anything — and for the same reason it can never be exact, which is why it is always paired with feedback trim.

The Architecture
The Design Equation
The disturbance reaches the output through Gd(s); our correction reaches it through Gp(s). For perfect cancellation the two contributions must sum to zero: Gd(s)·d + Gp(s)·Gff(s)·d = 0 Gff(s) = − Gd(s) / Gp(s) ← the ideal feedforward STATIC (steady-state) feedforward — the version most plants actually use: Kff = − Kd / Kp (ratio of the two process gains; the minus sign is the sign of the correction) DYNAMIC feedforward — with FOPDT models for both paths: Gff(s) = −(Kd/Kp) · (τps + 1)/(τds + 1) · e−(θd−θp)s └── LEAD/LAG ──┘ └── DEAD-TIME MATCHING ──┘ Read the two dynamic terms carefully — they are the whole art: · Lead/lag shapes the timing. If the disturbance acts FASTER than our correction (τd < τp), we need LEAD (a kick) to catch up. · Dead-time term is only realisable if θd ≥ θp — i.e. the disturbance must take LONGER to arrive than our correction does. If θd < θp the ideal feedforward requires PREDICTION of the future and cannot be built; the best available action is to apply the correction immediately and accept a transient. This is the single most important feasibility test.
Why Feedforward Is Always Paired with Feedback

Feedforward alone

Fast, cannot destabilise (it is open loop), acts before the error. But: relies entirely on model accuracy, corrects only the disturbances it measures, and has no way of knowing it was wrong — errors accumulate silently.

Feedback alone

Self-correcting, needs no model, catches every disturbance including unmeasured ones. But: acts only after the error exists, and its speed is bounded by dead time.

Together: feedforward does the bulk, correctly and instantly, for the big measured disturbance; feedback trims the residual — model error, unmeasured disturbances, drift. Typical result on a load-change test: feedback only peak deviation 100% (reference) + static feedforward peak deviation 30–50% + dynamic feedforward peak deviation 10–20% The remaining error is model mismatch, and it is what the feedback integral removes.
  • 1
    Commission feedforward with the feedback loop in AUTO, and add the FF gain gradually. Start at half the calculated Kff, make a step in the disturbance, and observe: if the PV moves the same way as before, increase Kff; if it now moves the opposite way, you have over-compensated. Tuning feedforward is a bisection on the sign of the residual.
  • 2
    Get the sign right before anything else. A feedforward with the wrong sign doubles the disturbance instead of cancelling it. Verify by reasoning through the physics, then confirm with a small deliberate step.
  • 3
    Prefer multiplicative feedforward for ratio-like relationships. When the required correction is proportional to throughput (fuel with air, reagent with flow), multiply rather than add — that is really ratio control (Page 4), and it stays correct across the whole load range where an additive term would not.
  • 4
    Beware the stale model. A feedforward gain derived from a heat exchanger when it was clean will be wrong once it fouls. Feedforward needs periodic revalidation; the feedback trim's average output drifting away from its normal range is the tell-tale that the FF model has aged.
  • 5
    Do not feedforward from a noisy measurement. The FF path has no filtering benefit from feedback dynamics, so noise passes straight to the valve. Filter the disturbance signal — and remember the filter is part of the dynamic matching.

Quick check: On a dryer, product moisture is disturbed by feed rate. The feed-rate change reaches the moisture measurement in 30 s; a change in burner firing reaches it in 90 s. Can perfect feedforward be built? What is the best achievable scheme?
Tap to reveal ↓A: No — perfect feedforward is impossible here, because θd = 30 s < θp = 90 s. The ideal compensator contains e−(θd−θp)s = e+60s, a positive exponent, which is a 60-second predictor: it would have to know the feed rate a minute before it changes. Physically, the disturbance beats our correction to the measurement by 60 s, so some excursion is unavoidable. Best achievable scheme, in order of value: (1) Apply the static feedforward immediately with no delay term — you cannot prevent the first 60 s of deviation, but you can ensure the correction is already fully applied when it arrives, which typically cuts the peak deviation substantially and shortens recovery. (2) Get the disturbance measurement earlier. This is the real fix: feed rate is usually known before the material reaches the dryer — measure it at the upstream weigh feeder or take the setpoint of the feed controller rather than its PV, buying you the transport time and turning θd into something larger than θp. A feedforward taken from an upstream setpoint rather than a downstream measurement is a standard and underused trick. (3) Reduce θp — is the moisture measurement as close to the dryer exit as it can be, and is the burner's own response (fuel valve, combustion lag) as fast as it could be? (4) Add a feedback trim as always, and consider MPC if there are several such disturbances with different timings, since MPC handles the prediction problem systematically. The examinable principle: feedforward feasibility is decided by a race between two dead times, and the correct engineering response to losing that race is to move the measurement, not to add mathematics.

Ratio Control & Cross-Limiting

When two streams must stay in proportion, the controlled variable is the ratio. And when getting the ratio wrong is dangerous, the scheme must fail safe during transients — that is cross-limiting.

Ratio Control Basics
Two streams: one is the WILD flow (set by production, we do not control it) and one is the CONTROLLED flow (we manipulate it to maintain the ratio R). SPcontrolled = R × Fwild Two implementations, and the difference matters: ① Ratio station (preferred, "flow-flow"): multiply the measured wild flow by R and use the result as the SETPOINT of a normal flow controller. → The controlled flow has its own closed loop, so valve nonlinearity and supply pressure are handled locally. This is really feedforward + cascade. ② Divider / ratio-as-PV: compute the actual ratio (Fc/Fw) and control THAT. → Looks elegant but the division makes the loop gain vary inversely with Fw: at 20% load the gain is five times higher than at 100%. Loops built this way are famous for being stable at high rate and unstable at low rate. Prefer implementation ①. TYPICAL APPLICATIONS · Fuel/air ratio in combustion (the safety-critical case below) · Reagent dosing proportional to flow (pH, chlorination, flocculant) · Blending to a recipe (multi-stream ratio to a common header) · Reflux ratio in distillation · Solvent-to-feed ratio in extraction · Steam-to-carbon ratio in reforming
  • !
    Always linearise flow signals first. If a DP transmitter's raw signal is used, remember F ∝ √ΔP; square-root extraction must happen before the ratio arithmetic, or the "ratio" will be wrong everywhere except the one point where it was calibrated.
Combustion Cross-Limiting — Ratio Control With a Safety Requirement

In a boiler or furnace, running fuel-rich (too little air) creates unburnt fuel — a smoke and explosion hazard. Running air-rich merely wastes energy. So the scheme must guarantee that during every transient, air leads fuel up and fuel leads air down. That asymmetry is achieved with two select blocks:

The logic in two lines: Air SP = HIGH-select( firing demand , measured FUEL × R ) → air can never be less than the fuel needs Fuel SP = LOW-select ( firing demand , measured AIR ÷ R ) → fuel can never exceed what the air can burn Notice that both selects use MEASURED flows, not setpoints — the interlock is on reality, so a stuck damper or a failed fan is caught too. In a real boiler this sits under a full BMS (burner management system) with its own safety instrumented functions for flame, purge and trip logic — cross-limiting is a CONTROL refinement, not a safety system (see A52).

Quick check: A boiler's fuel/air ratio scheme uses the setpoints of the two flow controllers in its cross-limiting selects rather than the measured flows. Why is that dangerous, and what single failure exposes it?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because setpoints describe intent and the interlock needs reality. Cross-limiting exists to guarantee that the air actually flowing is always sufficient for the fuel actually flowing. If the selects compare setpoints, the scheme is satisfied as long as the two commands are in proportion — which they always are, by construction. The exposing failure: anything that prevents the air loop from achieving its setpoint — a stuck or slipping FD fan damper, a failed fan, a blocked air filter, a saturated actuator, or the air flow controller left in manual. The air controller's setpoint dutifully rises with demand, so the fuel ceiling rises with it, and fuel is admitted for air that is not there: the furnace goes fuel-rich, producing unburnt fuel, smoke, possible flame instability and, in the worst case, a furnace explosion when it re-ignites. The same argument applies to a failed air flow transmitter reading high, which is why the measurement itself deserves attention (validity checks, and on larger plant redundant measurement with selection). The rule to carry: interlocks and limiting schemes must compare measured values, never commands — and the same principle appears throughout process control (override schemes, valve-position feedback for external reset, and the "measured, not commanded" requirement in safety instrumented functions per IEC 61511, A52). Note also the boundary of responsibility: cross-limiting improves combustion control, but the protection against a fuel-rich furnace must ultimately rest with the burner management system — a rated safety function with its own sensors, logic and final elements, independent of the control scheme discussed here.

Override, Select & Split-Range

One valve, several objectives — or one objective, several valves. Both need select logic, and both hide a windup trap.

Override (Constraint) Control
Normal objective: maximise throughput / hold a setpoint. Constraint: never exceed a limit (pressure, temperature, motor current, level). Two controllers, ONE valve, resolved by a select block: Valve = LOW-select ( normal controller output , constraint controller output ) The normal controller is in charge until the constraint controller's output falls below it, at which point the constraint takes over automatically and the plant "rides the limit" instead of tripping. WHICH SELECT? Work it out from the valve's action, never from intuition: · Valve fails CLOSED (air-to-open) and the constraint is a MAXIMUM → constraint wants to CLOSE the valve → LOW select · Valve fails OPEN (air-to-close) and the constraint is a MAXIMUM → constraint wants to OPEN the valve → HIGH select Get this wrong and the "protection" becomes the thing that causes the excursion. CLASSIC EXAMPLES · Compressor: capacity control, overridden by discharge pressure and motor amps · Furnace: fuel on outlet temperature, overridden by tube-metal temperature · Pump-out: flow control, overridden by low suction level (cavitation protection) · Dryer: throughput, overridden by maximum exhaust temperature
  • 1
    The windup trap is the whole difficulty of select schemes. The controller that loses the select still sees an error and still integrates — silently, with no effect. When conditions change and it wins, its output is far from reality and the valve slams. Cure: external reset feedback (feed the selected output back as each controller's integral reference) or an explicit "not selected → freeze integral" signal. Every modern DCS has this; check it is actually configured.
  • 2
    Tune both controllers, and tune them differently. The constraint controller usually wants to be fast and slightly aggressive (it is protecting something), while the production controller can be gentle. They never operate simultaneously, so their tunings do not have to be compatible.
  • 3
    Give operators visibility of which controller is in command. "Why won't the plant make rate?" is answered instantly by an indication that the tube-metal override is active. Without it, override schemes get blamed and switched off.
  • 4
    An override is not a safety function. It runs on the control system, shares sensors with control, and is not rated. Real protection needs an independent SIF per IEC 61511 (A52). Override control keeps you off the trip; it does not replace it.
Split-Range Control
ONE controller output, TWO (or more) final elements sequenced across the range — used when a single valve cannot span the required rangeability, or when heating and cooling need separate actuators. Classic 50/50 heat–cool split on a reactor jacket: Output 0–50% → COOLING valve strokes 100% → 0% (reverse) Output 50–100% → HEATING valve strokes 0% → 100% Cooling % = max(0, (50 − OP) × 2) Heating % = max(0, (OP − 50) × 2) Also common: a large/small valve pair for wide rangeability — 0–20% → small (trim) valve 0→100% 20–100% → small stays open, large valve 0→100%
  • 1
    The gain discontinuity is the classic split-range failure. Cooling water may give 3 °C per % of controller output while steam gives 12 °C per % — so a single tuning cannot suit both halves. Symptoms: sluggish on cooling, oscillatory on heating (or vice versa). Cures: re-range the split point so the two halves have similar process gain; resize the valves; apply an output characteriser to each half; or use gain scheduling on which half is active (Page 10).
  • 2
    Choose gap versus overlap on purpose. A gap (e.g. cooling closes at 48%, heating opens at 52%) prevents simultaneous heating and cooling — real energy savings — but leaves a dead zone where the PV drifts. An overlap gives seamless control but can burn steam into cooling water. For temperature-critical reactors overlap is usually right; for utility-cost-sensitive HVAC, a gap.
  • 3
    Calibrate the sequencing at the valves, not just in software. Positioner split-range calibration drifts; verify each valve's actual travel against controller output at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100% during commissioning and record it.
  • 4
    Consider two controllers instead. Modern practice often prefers separate heating and cooling controllers with a shared setpoint and a select/deadband scheme, because each can be tuned for its own dynamics. Split-range is a legacy solution to a one-output limitation that most systems no longer have.
Quick check: A compressor has capacity control on suction pressure, with an override on motor current (fail-closed guide vanes). During a cold night the machine loads up, the current override takes over correctly — but when the load falls again the suction pressure crashes badly before recovering. Diagnose.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Classic override windup. While the current override was in command, the suction-pressure controller lost the select but kept integrating: it saw a persistent error (suction pressure not at setpoint, because the override was holding capacity back) and its integral drove its output steadily toward maximum. When the load fell and the current controller relaxed, the pressure controller won the select again — but its output was now far above anything sensible, so the vanes opened wide, over-shot, and the suction pressure crashed. Recovery then took as long as it took the integral to unwind. The fix is external reset feedback: both controllers' integral terms must track the actually selected output, so the losing controller's output stays "parked" just above (for a low select) the winning value and is ready to resume from reality. If external reset is unavailable, use the "not selected" status to freeze the integral, or configure output tracking. Two further professional points. (1) This failure is intermittent and load-dependent, so it survives commissioning and appears months later on a cold night — which is exactly why select schemes should be tested by deliberately driving the plant into and out of the constraint during commissioning, not just by inspection. (2) Check the select direction while you are there: with fail-closed guide vanes and a maximum-current constraint, the override wants to reduce capacity, so it must be a LOW select — and if it were wired as a high select the machine would have tripped on overcurrent rather than riding the limit.

Dead-Time Compensation

If dead time is the fundamental limit (A57), can we beat it with a model? Partly — and the honest engineering is in knowing when not to try.

The Smith Predictor
IDEA: give the controller a simulated, delay-free version of the process to work against, and correct that simulation with the real (delayed) measurement. Model of the plant, split into its delay-free part and its delay: G(s) = G*(s)·e−θms G* = delay-free model, θm = model dead time The predictor feeds the controller: yfed back = G*(s)·u + [ ymeasured − G*(s)e−θms·u ] └ fast prediction ┘ └──── correction for model error ────┘ If the model were perfect the bracket is zero and the controller sees only the undelayed response — so it can be tuned as if θ = 0, achieving a much faster closed loop. The ideal closed-loop result becomes: y/r = [C·G*/(1 + C·G*)] · e−θs i.e. the delay is moved OUTSIDE the loop: the response is as fast as a delay-free loop, merely shifted in time by θ. That is the theoretical prize.

Smith predictor — where θ is large, well known and CONSTANT, and options 1–3 are genuinely unavailable. It is a legitimate tool; it is just rarely the best available one.

    Internal Model Control (IMC) is the same family of thinking made systematic: design the controller as (an approximation of) the model inverse times a tunable filter. Its practical importance is that SIMC/lambda tuning is derived from it — so every time you use Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)) you are already using model-based control, just packaged as a PID (A57, Page 12).
Quick check: A pulp bleaching loop has 4 minutes of transport dead time that varies from 3 to 6 minutes with production rate. An engineer proposes a Smith predictor. Assess.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Reject it in that form. The dead time varies by a factor of two with production rate, and a fixed-θm Smith predictor is precisely the scheme that cannot tolerate that: tuned for 4 minutes it will be badly mismatched at both 3 and 6, losing most of its benefit and risking instability at the extremes. Dead time that varies with throughput is the single most common reason Smith predictors fail in practice. Better options, in order: (1) Schedule the model dead time against production rate — transport delay is θ = volume/flow, so it is calculable from the flow measurement you already have. A dead time that is computed rather than assumed makes the predictor viable, and the same calculation improves any scheme you choose. (2) Cascade if any intermediate measurement exists between the reagent valve and the final brightness measurement (reagent flow certainly, possibly an intermediate stage) — this removes valve and reagent-supply disturbances at once and is robust to the transport variation. (3) Feedforward from production rate and incoming brightness, since the dominant disturbances here are measurable upstream; combined with the flow-based delay calculation this is often the highest-value change. (4) MPC, which handles both the long delay and the rate dependence within a maintained model and can respect brightness and chemical-cost objectives simultaneously — for a bleach plant this is a common and justified application. (5) Failing all of that, detune honestly: with θ up to 6 minutes, accept a closed-loop time constant of that order and let feedforward do the heavy lifting. The examinable point: before adding model-based compensation, ask whether the model's key parameter is constant. If it is not, either compute it from a measurement or choose a structure that does not depend on it.

Interaction, RGA & Decoupling

When every valve moves every measurement, the first question is not "how do I tune this?" but "which valve should control which variable?" The relative gain array answers it with one number per pairing.

The Problem
A 2×2 process: two manipulated variables, two controlled variables. y₁ = K₁₁·u₁ + K₁₂·u₂ y₂ = K₂₁·u₁ + K₂₂·u₂ → y = K·u, K = steady-state gain matrix If K₁₂ and K₂₁ are non-zero the loops INTERACT: closing loop 1 changes the apparent process seen by loop 2, and vice versa. Consequences: · Tuning loop 1 alone, then loop 2 alone, can give a combination that oscillates. · The loops can pass a disturbance back and forth indefinitely. · In the worst case the PAIRING is wrong and the scheme is unstable at any tuning. Classic examples · Distillation: reflux and reboil each affect BOTH top and bottom composition · Blending: each stream affects total flow AND composition · Sheet processes: cross-direction actuators affect neighbouring measurements · Boiler: fuel affects pressure and temperature; feedwater affects level and temperature
The Relative Gain Array (Bristol)
Λ = K ⊗ (K−1)T (element-by-element, "Hadamard", product) For a 2×2 system this collapses to a single number: λ₁₁ = 1 / (1 − (K₁₂K₂₁)/(K₁₁K₂₂)) and the array is ⎡ λ₁₁ 1−λ₁₁ ⎤ Λ = ⎢ ⎥ (rows and columns each sum to 1) ⎣ 1−λ₁₁ λ₁₁ ⎦ INTERPRETATION of λij — the pairing diagnostic: λ = 1 PERFECT. No interaction; that loop's gain is the same open or closed. 0.7 – 1.3 GOOD pairing. Mild interaction, independent tuning is fine. 0.3 – 0.7 or 1.3 – 4 SIGNIFICANT interaction. Detune, sequence, or decouple. λ > 5 SEVERE. The loops fight hard; closing one massively changes the other. λ = 0 Useless pairing — that MV has no independent effect on that CV. λ < 0 DANGEROUS — NEVER USE THIS PAIRING. The loop gain CHANGES SIGN between open and closed loop, so a controller that works with the other loop in manual will run away with it in auto. PAIRING RULE: choose the pairing whose λ values are closest to 1, and never choose a pairing with a negative λ on the diagonal.
What To Do About Interaction — Four Options

① Re-pair (free)

Compute the RGA for every candidate pairing and pick the one closest to λ = 1. Costs nothing but configuration, and is always the first move. Many "impossible" interacting schemes were simply paired wrongly.

② Detune / sequence speeds

Make one loop clearly faster than the other (a factor of 3–5). The fast loop then looks like an instantaneous actuator to the slow one, so they stop fighting. Cheap and robust; the cost is that one CV is controlled loosely.

③ Decouplers

Add cross-terms that cancel the interaction: D = K−1 (or partial/one-way decoupling). Effective in theory but sensitive to gain errors, and a one-way decoupler is usually all that survives in practice. Static decoupling is far more robust than dynamic.

④ MPC

Handles interaction natively by solving for all MVs together (Page 9). The right answer for genuinely coupled units with constraints — and the wrong answer for a 2×2 problem that a re-pairing would have solved.

Two companions to the RGA worth knowing:
Niederlinski index: NI = det(K) / Π Kii. For a decentralised (independent-loop) scheme with integral action, NI < 0 guarantees instability — a quick necessary test to rule out a pairing before you tune anything.
Condition number γ(K) = σmax/σmin: a large value (say > 50) means the process is nearly singular in some direction — the plant is ill-conditioned, small model errors have big consequences, and no controller will be robust. That is a process design finding, and it is often the honest answer for high-purity distillation.

(3) Consider a change of variables — this is the standard and often decisive fix for blending and distillation: instead of controlling two similar outputs, control a sum and a ratio (total flow and composition, rather than two component flows). (4) If both CVs genuinely must be held and constraints matter, this is a legitimate MPC case — MPC solves for both MVs together and handles near-singular directions by trading them off explicitly rather than letting two PIDs fight. (5) Finally, note the robustness warning: with det(K) = 0.12, a 10% error in any gain changes the determinant by a large fraction, so any decoupler designed from these numbers will be unreliable. Ill-conditioning is a property of the plant, and the professional response is to change what you control, not to add more control.

Inferential & Soft Sensors

When the variable you care about is measured too slowly, too rarely, or not at all — infer it from variables you already have.

The Problem and the Idea
The quality variable is often the WRONG variable to close a loop on: · Lab assay: 1–4 hour turnaround, once a shift · On-line chromatograph: 5–20 minute cycle, high maintenance · Moisture / density / viscosity analysers: slow, drifty, easily fouled Meanwhile temperatures, pressures and flows are available every second, and are physically RELATED to the quality variable. So build an estimate: ŷquality = f( T₁, T₂, P, F, ρ, … ) and close a fast loop on ŷ, while the slow real measurement is used only to bias/correct the estimate when it arrives. This is a cascade in disguise: a fast inferential inner loop inside a slow analytical outer loop.

The Discipline That Keeps Soft Sensors Alive
  • 1
    Always bias-update from the real measurement. The estimate drifts; the lab or analyser value, when it arrives, corrects the offset. Use a slow filter or an outer integral so a single bad lab result cannot step the plant — a common configuration is a bias term with a time constant of several sample intervals.
  • 2
    Validate the input data first. A soft sensor is only as good as its inputs: add range checks, rate-of-change checks, and a "frozen signal" test on every input. A failed thermocouple reading a constant value will silently poison the estimate — and unlike a real analyser, a soft sensor never looks broken.
  • 3
    Beware extrapolation. An empirical model is valid only within the operating envelope of its training data. New grade, new feedstock, new season, cleaned exchanger — and the model is outside its range. Detect this explicitly (a Hotelling T² or squared-prediction-error statistic on the inputs) and fall back to the slow measurement when it triggers.
  • 4
    Prefer first-principles where possible. Pressure-compensated temperature has been solving distillation composition inference for decades and never needs retraining. A physics-based inference extrapolates; a regression does not.
  • 5
    Assign an owner and a revalidation cadence. Compare predicted against measured on a control chart, and review monthly. The characteristic failure of soft sensors is not a crash — it is slow, unnoticed drift that erodes the benefit until someone turns the scheme off.
  • 6
    Never use a soft sensor for protection. An inferred value is not a measurement; safety functions require rated, direct sensing (A52). Also be careful about using one for custody transfer or product release — that is a quality-system and often a regulatory question.
Pressure-compensated temperature — the canonical first-principles inference:
Tcomp = Tmeasured − k·(P − Pref)
k is the slope of the boiling point with pressure at that composition (from VLE data), typically a few °C per bar. Without compensation, a column's tray temperature moves with pressure at constant composition — so an uncompensated temperature loop chases the weather and the condenser, not the product.
Quick check: A neural-net soft sensor for product purity performed beautifully for eight months, then began reading 0.4% high with no alarm. What are the three most likely causes, and what should have been in place?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Three likely causes. (1) An input has drifted or failed — a fouled or de-calibrated temperature transmitter, a plugged DP tap reading low, or a signal that has quietly frozen. The net has no idea; it simply maps whatever it is given. (2) The process has moved outside the training envelope — a new feedstock, a different grade, a cleaned exchanger, a changed reflux policy, or simply seasonal cooling-water temperature. The model is extrapolating, which neural nets do confidently and badly. (3) The plant itself has changed — fouling, catalyst ageing, tray damage — so the true input–output relationship the model learned no longer holds. (A fourth, less common: the bias-update path was broken or left in manual, so the correction that would have caught this was not being applied.) What should have been in place: (a) input validation on every signal — range, rate-of-change and frozen-value checks, with the estimate marked bad and the scheme falling back to the real analyser if any input fails; (b) a model-validity statistic (squared prediction error or Hotelling T² on the inputs) that detects extrapolation and raises an alarm — "the model is being asked a question it was not trained on" is a detectable condition; (c) a predicted-versus-measured control chart reviewed on a defined cadence, which would have shown a 0.4% bias building up long before it mattered; (d) a bias/reconciliation term updated from the lab or analyser, so slow drift is absorbed automatically rather than accumulating; and (e) a named owner with a revalidation schedule. The general lesson: the danger of a soft sensor is not that it fails, but that it fails silently and plausibly. Every inference needs an independent check on it, and a defined fallback when that check fires.

Model Predictive Control

The only widely deployed technology that handles interaction, dead time and hard constraints simultaneously — because it solves an optimisation at every execution instead of applying a fixed formula.

The Receding-Horizon Idea
At every control interval, MPC does four things: 1. PREDICT the future of every CV over a prediction horizon P, using a model and the current state, assuming the MVs stay where they are. 2. OPTIMISE a sequence of future MV moves over a control horizon M so that predicted CVs track their targets, subject to CONSTRAINTS. 3. IMPLEMENT ONLY THE FIRST MOVE of that optimal sequence. 4. Discard the rest, wait one interval, re-measure and repeat. That last step — recomputing from fresh measurements every interval — is what makes it feedback rather than a schedule. It is called the RECEDING HORIZON. The objective function (the quadratic-programme form used by most vendors): min Σi=1..P wy·(ŷ(k+i) − r(k+i))² + Σj=1..M wu·(Δu(k+j))² Δu └─ tracking error over the horizon ─┘ └─ move penalty ─┘ subject to: umin ≤ u ≤ umax (valve limits) |Δu| ≤ Δumax (rate limits) ymin ≤ ŷ ≤ ymax (quality/safety limits) · wy weights each CV's importance (bigger = tracked more tightly) · wu penalises MV movement (bigger = gentler, less valve wear, more robust) · The move penalty is MPC's equivalent of detuning — it is the main robustness knob.

The Practical Realities
  • 1
    Most of the project is model identification, not control. Typically 70–80% of the effort is plant step-testing (or PRBS testing), data conditioning and model validation. Budget for a testing campaign with production's cooperation — and do it on a plant whose base layer is healthy, or you will identify the valve's stiction as process dynamics.
  • 2
    Model form: most industrial MPC uses step-response (finite impulse/DMC-style) models rather than state-space, because they are easy to identify from plant tests and easy for an engineer to inspect. Modern packages support both.
  • 3
    Tuning knobs are few but powerful: CV weights (importance), MV move penalties (aggressiveness/robustness), CV ranges instead of setpoints (zone control — "anywhere in this band is fine", which frees the optimiser and is often the single most useful configuration choice), and constraint priorities for infeasibility handling.
  • 4
    Service factor is the KPI that matters. An MPC that is on 40% of the time delivers roughly 40% of its benefit. Operators switch off what they do not trust or understand, so operator training and transparency (why is it doing that? what constraint is active?) determine the return more than the algorithm does.
  • 5
    Plan for model maintenance. Plants foul, catalysts age, feedstocks change. Without an owner and a periodic revalidation, an MPC's performance decays over 1–3 years. Include this in the business case, not as an afterthought.
  • 6
    Know when it is overkill. A single loop with one measured disturbance needs feedforward, not MPC. MPC earns its keep on multivariable, constrained units: crude and vacuum units, FCC, distillation trains, reformers, cement kilns, paper machines, large blending and mining circuits.
Quick check: An MPC on a distillation column keeps driving the reboiler to its maximum and reporting "infeasible". The operators have started leaving it off. What is happening and how do you approach it?
Tap to reveal ↓A: "Infeasible" means the optimiser cannot satisfy all the constraints simultaneously — it has been asked for something the plant cannot deliver. The reboiler at maximum tells you the binding constraint is energy input, and the MPC is correctly reporting that it cannot meet both product specifications at the current feed rate with the available heat. How to approach it. (1) Find out which constraint set is in conflict — every MPC package reports the active constraints and the infeasibility ranking; read it rather than guessing. Common culprits: a bottom-purity limit tightened by someone, a feed rate above the column's capacity, a pressure or ΔP constraint reducing available boil-up, or a fouled reboiler whose real capacity is now below the configured maximum. (2) Convert setpoints into zones. If the top and bottom specifications are configured as exact targets, the optimiser has no freedom; giving each a range ("between 99.2 and 99.6%") usually restores feasibility immediately and is the single most common fix. (3) Check and rank the soft constraints so that when the plant genuinely cannot do everything, the MPC gives up the least valuable objective first — in a defined, explained order — rather than reporting a bare "infeasible". (4) Validate the constraint values against reality: a maximum reboiler duty entered from the design datasheet is wrong once the exchanger fouls, and an MPC pushing against a false limit looks broken. (5) Check the model — if the gains have drifted, the MPC may be over-estimating how much boil-up a given move buys, causing it to over-drive. (6) Then talk to operations. The real message may be legitimate and valuable: "at this feed rate you cannot make both specifications — the column is heat-limited". That is a genuine finding for a debottlenecking case, not a control fault. The professional framing: an MPC reporting infeasibility is usually telling the truth about the plant; the fault is that nobody configured it to degrade gracefully or to explain itself to the operator — and that, not the mathematics, is why the service factor has collapsed.

The Three Approaches, in Increasing Risk

① Robust fixed tuning

Tune at the highest process gain the loop will meet. The loop is then sluggish elsewhere but never unstable. Costs nothing, needs no maintenance, and is the right answer far more often than engineers admit.

② Gain scheduling

Pre-computed tuning as a function of a measured, reliable scheduling variable (throughput, valve position, batch phase). Deterministic, testable, documentable. The professional default when fixed tuning is genuinely inadequate.

③ Adaptive / self-tuning

The controller identifies the process on-line and retunes itself. Powerful, but it can be fooled by noise, by disturbances mistaken for responses, and by periods of no excitation. Requires supervision.

GAIN SCHEDULING — how it is actually built: 1. Choose a reliable, slow-moving scheduling variable that indexes the change. Throughput is the usual choice; valve position and batch phase also common. 2. Bump-test at 3–5 points across the range, obtaining K, τ, θ at each. 3. Compute the tuning at each point (SIMC: Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ))). 4. Store as a table and INTERPOLATE between points; rate-limit the changes so tuning never steps abruptly. 5. Clamp the schedule at both ends and provide a safe default if the scheduling measurement fails — a lost flow signal must not select a wild gain. For the special case where only the GAIN varies inversely with load, the elegant version is simply to remove the nonlinearity at source: if K ∝ 1/F then a controller gain ∝ F keeps the LOOP GAIN K·Kc CONSTANT — which is exactly what a properly configured ratio or a cascade to flow achieves for free, without any scheduling table at all.
  • !
    Prefer removing the nonlinearity to scheduling around it. Square-root extraction, an equal-percentage valve, a flow cascade, or a titration-curve characteriser all make the process look linear — after which one fixed tuning works everywhere and there is nothing to maintain. Gain scheduling is what you do when the nonlinearity cannot be removed.

Quick check: A flow loop on a DP transmitter without square-root extraction is tuned nicely at 80% flow, then oscillates violently at 25% flow. Explain quantitatively and give the correct fix and two fallbacks.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The transmitter reports ΔP, and F ∝ √ΔP, so the loop's measurement gain (signal per unit flow) is dF/dΔP ∝ 1/√ΔP — the gain rises as flow falls. Quantitatively, ΔP ∝ F², so going from 80% to 25% flow reduces ΔP by (0.25/0.80)² = 0.098, roughly a tenth; the measurement sensitivity therefore rises by 1/√0.098 ≈ 3.2×. A loop tuned for 80% flow is thus running with about three times too much loop gain at 25% — comfortably enough to cross from well-damped into sustained oscillation. The correct fix: enable square-root extraction in the transmitter (or in the DCS input block, but the transmitter is better as it also improves the signal's resolution and any totalising). This makes the reported signal linear in flow, after which one tuning works across the range and the problem disappears permanently. Fallback 1: gain-schedule the controller gain against measured flow — workable but it maintains a table to solve a problem that a configuration bit would remove. Fallback 2: tune for the worst case, i.e. the low-flow, high-gain condition, and accept sluggish response at high flow — always safe, sometimes acceptable, and the right emergency action if you cannot change the configuration today. Two related points worth making in an exam: (a) the same 1/√ΔP argument means low-flow accuracy of a DP element is poor anyway — turndown of an orifice plate is typically only about 3:1 to 4:1, so at 25% you may also be near the bottom of the measurement's usable range; (b) never confuse this measurement nonlinearity with the valve's installed characteristic, which is a separate multiplicative gain variation and is corrected by trim selection or an output characteriser.

Nonlinear Compensation & Rule-Based Control

The best way to control a nonlinear process is to make it look linear. Where that fails, encode the operator's judgement — and be honest that this is heuristics, not theory.

Linearisation by Characterisation
Insert a function block that cancels the process's own curvature, so the controller sees a constant gain. Two places to put it: ① INPUT (measurement) characteriser — linearise the PV Classic: pH. The titration curve gives pH as a function of reagent excess. Apply its INVERSE to the pH measurement and control the linearised variable: x = f−1(pH) → control x, display pH to the operator Effect: the loop gain becomes constant instead of varying 100-fold near the equivalence point. ② OUTPUT (valve) characteriser — linearise the actuator Apply a curve to the controller output so that equal controller increments produce equal FLOW (or equal duty) increments: valve % = g(OP %) with g chosen to invert the installed characteristic Effect: cancels a valve whose gain varies with position or ΔP. ③ SIGNAL LINEARISATION (always do this one) Square-root extraction for DP flow · temperature compensation for gas flow (Fcorrected = F·√(P·Tref/(Pref·T))) · pressure compensation for distillation temperature · density compensation for level in a varying-density service. RULE OF THUMB: a well-characterised loop needs ONE tuning; an uncharacterised one needs a schedule and an argument every time the load changes.

The pH problem, quantified

pH is a logarithmic measure of concentration, so a strong-acid/strong-base titration curve is nearly vertical at the equivalence point. Process gain there can be 100–1000× what it is on the flat portions. No single PID tuning can span that. Cures: linearise via the titration curve, add buffering, stage the addition (coarse + fine reagent valves in split range), and increase mixing volume to slow the dynamics.

The valve problem, quantified

A linear trim valve in a system where most pressure drop is in the pipework has an installed characteristic that flattens badly: gain near the open position can be a fifth of the gain near closed. An equal-percentage trim compensates for falling ΔP and gives a roughly linear installed characteristic — which is why equal-percentage is the default choice for most throttling service (full treatment in the Control Valves module).

Fuzzy Logic & Rule-Based Control — an Honest Appraisal
STRUCTURE 1. Fuzzification: convert numeric inputs into degrees of membership of linguistic sets — error is "slightly positive" 0.7 and "zero" 0.3. 2. Rule base: IF error is positive-large AND rate is positive THEN output change is negative-large. 3. Inference & defuzzification: combine the fired rules and produce a numeric output (usually the centroid of the resulting membership function). A typical fuzzy PID-like controller uses a 3×3, 5×5 or 7×7 rule table indexed by ERROR and RATE OF CHANGE OF ERROR — which is, in effect, a nonlinear PD surface.

Where it genuinely helps

Processes with no usable model but strong operator experience (cement kilns, some furnaces, wastewater, food processes); supervisory decisions rather than regulation ("if the kiln is getting cold and the feed is high, cut feed"); and smoothly switching between operating regimes — a fuzzy scheme is essentially a soft gain schedule with an interpretable rule base.

Where it does not

Anything a model-based method can address — a fuzzy controller on a well-modelled FOPDT loop will not beat a properly tuned PID and cannot be analysed for margins. There is no stability theory comparable to Bode/Nyquist, tuning is by trial, and validation is by testing every combination. Never in a safety function.

  • !
    The professional position. Fuzzy and rule-based control are legitimate tools for capturing heuristics where no model exists — and they are frequently used to dress up a gain schedule. If you can write down K, τ and θ, use A57's methods; if the plant is genuinely un-modellable but experienced operators run it well, encoding their rules is a real engineering contribution. Just do not claim guarantees you cannot demonstrate, and always provide a bumpless fallback to a conventional controller.
Quick check: A neutralisation basin controls pH to 7.0 ± 0.5 with a single reagent valve. The loop is either oscillating hard or drifting, never steady. What is the sequence of fixes, cheapest first?
Tap to reveal ↓A: This is the textbook nonlinear loop: near pH 7 the titration curve is nearly vertical, so process gain is enormous and the loop oscillates; away from 7 the curve is flat, gain collapses and the loop drifts. One fixed tuning cannot serve both, which is exactly the symptom described. Sequence, cheapest first. (1) Verify the measurement and mixing — a fouled or slow electrode, or a probe in a poorly mixed corner, produces the same symptoms and costs nothing to check. Probe location and cleaning schedule matter more than tuning here. (2) Detune for the worst case as an immediate stabilising action: tune for the high-gain condition near setpoint, accepting slow recovery from excursions. Ugly but safe, and it stops the oscillation today. (3) Linearise the measurement using the titration curve — control the inferred reagent-demand variable rather than pH, displaying pH to the operator. This is the single highest-value change and it is a configuration task, not a capital one. (4) Widen the deadband / use a control band — pH 7.0 ± 0.5 is a range, not a setpoint, so control to the band rather than the centre and stop the valve chasing noise. (5) Stage the reagent: coarse and fine valves in split range (Page 5), so fine resolution is available near setpoint and capacity is available for upsets. (6) Add residence time or buffering — a larger or baffled basin slows the dynamics and reduces the effective gain; chemically, buffering flattens the curve. This is a process change but often the definitive answer. (7) Feedforward from influent flow and inlet pH, both usually measurable, so the bulk of the dose is computed rather than discovered. (8) Only then consider MPC or a fuzzy supervisory layer. The examinable principle: for a strongly nonlinear loop, the order of attack is measurement → linearise → restructure (staging, band, feedforward) → process design, and tuning appears only as a stopgap.

Unit-Operation Strategies

The schemes that actually run plants. Each combines two or three of the building blocks from the previous pages — learn them as patterns.

① Three-Element Boiler Drum Level — the Classic

Drum level is the standard example of a loop where naive feedback fails, because of shrink and swell: an increase in steam demand drops drum pressure, steam bubbles expand, and the indicated level rises even though mass is leaving. A single-element level controller sees the rise and closes feedwater — precisely wrong. This is the RHP-zero inverse response of A57 (Page 6) in industrial form.

Why the three elements are exactly the right three: · Steam flow (feedforward) — a mass balance: water in must equal steam out. This provides the bulk of the demand immediately and correctly, and it is immune to the level indication lying. · Drum level (feedback trim) — corrects the accumulated error and the unmeasured losses (blowdown, drift), acting slowly so it cannot be fooled by swell. · Feedwater flow (cascade inner loop) — removes feedwater header pressure variation and valve nonlinearity, so the demand in kg/h is actually delivered. Single-element (level only) is used at low load or start-up; two-element adds steam flow; three-element is standard above roughly 20–30% load.
② Reactor Temperature — Cascade + Split-Range + Constraint
Reactor temperature TIC (outer, slow) → jacket temperature TIC (inner, fast) [cascade — Page 2] → SPLIT-RANGE: cooling valve 0–50%, steam 50–100% [Page 5] with: jacket SP CLAMPED (never below 5 °C, never above 95 °C) OVERRIDE on reactor pressure or cooling-water return temperature FEEDFORWARD from feed rate if the reaction is fed continuously and separately: a SAFETY INSTRUMENTED FUNCTION on high temperature (A52) — independent sensors, independent final element. This one strategy uses four of the building blocks in this module. It is the single most common "advanced" scheme in fine chemicals and pharma.

④ Compressor Antisurge — Constraint Control With Real Consequences
Surge is a flow reversal that can destroy a machine in seconds. The scheme: · Compute the operating point's distance from the SURGE LINE from measured suction/discharge conditions and flow (a "surge control line" set a margin to the right of the surge line, typically 5–10%). · A fast PI controller opens the antisurge / recycle valve as the margin closes. · Add open-loop "recycle trip" logic: if the point crosses a tighter line, or the rate of approach is high, step the valve open immediately — feedback is too slow for a fast transient (a trip of the driver, a downstream valve slam). · Override capacity control (Page 5) so throughput yields to the constraint. · Antisurge valves must be fast (stroke in 1–2 s) and generously sized. Note the structure: a constraint controller plus a rate-triggered open-loop action. Any time the consequence of being late is severe, feedback alone is insufficient and an anticipatory action is added — the same reasoning as cross- limiting in Page 4.
Quick check: A boiler on two-element control (level + steam flow feedforward) suffers level excursions on rapid load swings, and the feedwater valve is seen to be slow to deliver the demanded flow because header pressure varies. What is missing, and why does it matter more at high load?
Tap to reveal ↓A: The third element — the feedwater flow cascade — is missing. In two-element control, the computed demand (steam flow feedforward plus level trim) is sent straight to the feedwater valve as a position. That silently assumes a fixed relationship between valve position and flow. When the feedwater header pressure varies — because other boilers or users are loading, or because the BFW pump is riding its curve — the same valve position delivers a different flow, so the mass balance the feedforward computed is not actually achieved and the level drifts. Adding a feedwater flow controller as the inner loop closes that gap: the demand becomes kg/h rather than valve percent, and header pressure, valve characteristic and any stiction are all rejected locally within seconds (Page 2). Why it matters more at high load: (1) the required flow rate is larger, so a given percentage error in delivered flow is a larger absolute mass imbalance and moves the level faster; (2) the valve is further open, where a linear-trim valve's installed gain is flattest and its authority against pressure changes is weakest; (3) at high load the drum's water inventory is a smaller fraction of its total throughput per minute, so the residence time buffering the error is shorter; and (4) shrink-and-swell effects are strongest during large, fast load swings, so the level indication is least trustworthy exactly when the mass balance most needs to be right. This is precisely why three-element control is standard above roughly 20–30% load, while single-element is acceptable at start-up when flows are small and the level indication is quiescent.

Quick check: A site's monitoring report shows a loop with 99% service factor, no saturation, no oscillation — but a Harris index of 0.25. Is there anything to do?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Yes — this is exactly the loop the Harris index exists to find. Every conventional KPI is clean: it is in automatic, the valve is not saturated, and it is not cycling, so no operator complains and no alarm fires. But η = 0.25 says that roughly three quarters of the observed variance is theoretically removable by better control — the loop is quietly sloppy rather than obviously broken. It is almost certainly detuned far too conservatively, which is the most common cause of a high-service-factor, low-η loop: someone slowed it down years ago to stop a complaint and nobody revisited it. What to do. (1) Bump-test to get a current K, τ, θ and check the model against whatever tuning record exists — often you will find the gain is a fraction of what SIMC would suggest. (2) Retune to a defined λ, verify PM/GM, and confirm with a +50% gain perturbation (A57). (3) Check for an excessive measurement filter or a slow task period, both of which force conservative tuning and are frequently the real cause. (4) Re-measure σe and η afterwards to prove the improvement — this is what makes the programme defensible. (5) Then ask the value question: does reduced variability on this variable convert into money (closer to a quality limit, less energy, more throughput)? If not, log it and move on — a low η is an opportunity, not an obligation. The reverse case is equally important and often more useful: a loop with η near 1 that operations still complain about cannot be improved by any controller, because its variance is dominated by dead time and unmeasured disturbances. That loop needs feedforward, cascade, or a process change — and knowing that saves an engineer from weeks of futile retuning.

Design-for-Failure Checklist
  • 1
    Every input needs a validity check and a defined action on failure: range, rate-of-change, frozen-signal. What happens to the scheme when the feedforward transmitter fails? It must degrade to plain feedback, announce it, and not step the valve.
  • 2
    Every mode transition must be bumpless in both directions — auto↔manual, cascade↔auto, MPC on↔off, scheme↔fallback. Test all of them, including the ones "that will never happen".
  • 3
    Every select or cascade needs windup protection configured and verified by test (external reset feedback or integral freeze). This is the most frequently omitted item in the whole subject.
  • 4
    Every computed value needs limits — a ratio, a feedforward output, an inferred PV or an MPC target must be clamped to a sane range so one bad number cannot move the plant.
  • 5
    Every scheme needs an obvious "what is in control right now" indication on the operator display: which controller won the select, whether feedforward is active, which MPC constraint is binding.
  • 6
    Never put a control refinement in the safety path. Overrides, cross-limiting and antisurge improve control; protection requires an independent, rated SIF with its own sensors and final elements (A52). State this boundary explicitly in the design document.
  • 7
    Document the model and the operating point. "K = 1.8 °C/% at 45–55% MV, 128–146 °C, clean exchanger, tested 2026-07-25" is a model. Without the qualifiers it is folklore, and the next engineer will re-test from scratch.
  • 8
    Put the scheme under management of change. A filter increased, a task period changed, a valve replaced with a different characteristic, a transmitter re-ranged — each invalidates part of the design. MoC is what stops the slow decay.
Quick check: A feedforward scheme commissioned last year is found with its feedforward gain set to zero. The maintenance log shows only "loop tuning adjusted — operator request". What probably happened, and how should the project have prevented it?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Almost certainly the feedforward misbehaved during an event and somebody neutralised it the fastest way available. The usual candidates: (1) the disturbance transmitter failed or drifted, so the feedforward injected a large, wrong correction — with no validity check the scheme had no way to know, and zeroing the gain was the only cure the shift had; (2) the model went stale (fouled exchanger, changed feedstock), so the feedforward over- or under-corrected consistently and the loop looked worse with it than without; (3) the sign or scaling was wrong for a new operating regime, e.g. after a valve or transmitter re-range; or (4) it simply did something the operator could not explain and was not trained to interpret. Note that "operator request" in the log is itself the finding — the scheme lost the operators' confidence and no engineer was involved in the decision. What the project should have built in: (a) input validation with defined degradation — on a bad disturbance signal the feedforward output should ramp smoothly to zero, raise an alarm, and leave the feedback loop in control, rather than requiring a human to do that manually; (b) clamped feedforward output so no single bad reading can make a large move; (c) an operator display element showing feedforward active / degraded / off with the current contribution, so its behaviour is visible rather than mysterious; (d) training plus a one-page explanation of what the scheme does and when to distrust it; (e) KPI monitoring that would have flagged "feedforward gain = 0" the next day rather than a year later — monitoring a scheme's configuration, not just the loop's performance; and (f) management of change, so disabling a designed function requires an engineering entry and a review, not a tuning note. The general lesson: an advanced scheme is not commissioned when it works — it is commissioned when it fails safely, explains itself, and is being watched.

Recap & Failure Modes

Eight ideas, six ways it goes wrong, and five closing checks.

The Eight Ideas That Carry Everything
  • 1
    Advanced control changes the structure, not the tuning. Each of the five structural limits — dead time, measurable disturbance, ratio, constraint, interaction — has a named remedy. Diagnose which one you have before choosing a tool.
  • 2
    Base layer first, always. Valve health, model, tuning, filters, scan time, anti-windup. Advanced control inherits every fault beneath it, and MPC on bad loops performs worse than the loops alone.
  • 3
    Cascade is the highest-value, lowest-risk structural change. It removes the valve, the utility supply and the inner nonlinearity from the outer loop's problem — provided the inner loop is 3–10× faster and you tune inner first.
  • 4
    Feedforward acts on the cause; feedback cleans up. Kff = −Kd/Kp, with lead/lag for timing — and it is only realisable if θd ≥ θp. If you lose that race, move the measurement upstream.
  • 5
    Select schemes always hide a windup trap. The losing controller integrates silently and dumps a step when it wins. External reset feedback is mandatory, not optional — and interlocks must compare measured values, never commands.
  • 6
    Pairing before tuning. λ = 1/(1 − K₁₂K₂₁/K₁₁K₂₂); choose λ closest to 1, and never a negative λ. Where the plant is ill-conditioned, change what you control (sum and ratio) rather than adding decouplers.
  • 7
    Remove nonlinearity rather than schedule around it. Square-root extraction, equal-percentage trim, a flow cascade, a titration characteriser — each makes one fixed tuning correct everywhere and leaves nothing to maintain.
  • 8
    Service factor is the benefit. A scheme that is off half the time delivers half the value, so transparency, graceful degradation, training and monitoring are engineering requirements. Design them in at the specification stage.
⚠️ Six Ways Advanced Control Goes Wrong
① Advanced control on a broken base layer

MPC or feedforward built over loops in manual, sticking valves and untuned controllers. The model identifies the stiction as process dynamics, the MPC writes setpoints nobody follows, and the scheme is off within weeks. Cure: audit and repair the regulatory layer first — it often captures much of the benefit by itself.

② Windup in a select or cascade scheme

The losing controller integrates unseen and slams the valve when it takes over; or a cascade's outer loop winds up while the inner is saturated or in local. Appears months later during an upset. Cure: external reset feedback, verified by deliberately driving the plant into and out of the constraint during commissioning.

③ Feedforward with a stale or unvalidated model

Gain derived from a clean exchanger, never revalidated; or a failed disturbance transmitter injecting a large wrong correction with no validity check. Ends with someone zeroing the gain. Cure: input validation, clamped output, graceful degradation, visible status, revalidation cadence.

④ Wrong pairing, or decoupling an ill-conditioned plant

Loops fight each other because λ was never computed, or a decoupler built from gains whose determinant is near zero makes matters worse. Cure: RGA and Niederlinski before configuration; change the controlled variables (sum/ratio) when the plant is genuinely ill-conditioned.

⑤ Complexity nobody owns

An elegant scheme whose designer has left, with no documentation of the model, the operating point or the mode logic. Operators distrust it, the service factor decays, and it is eventually bypassed. Cure: named owner, one-page operator explanation, KPI monitoring of the scheme's own configuration, MoC.

⑥ Control refinement mistaken for protection

Cross-limiting, overrides or antisurge treated as the safeguard against a hazard, sharing sensors and logic with control. Cure: state the boundary explicitly — protection needs an independent SIF with rated sensors and final elements per IEC 61511 (A52). Control keeps you off the trip; it does not replace it.

Final check 1: In one paragraph, explain why cascade control reduces the effective dead time seen by the outer loop, and what determines the limit of that benefit.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The outer loop's "process" is no longer the valve plus the whole plant; it is the closed inner loop in series with the remaining slow section. Because the inner loop is fast and has its own feedback, it converts the outer controller's output from a valve position into a delivered inner variable (a flow, a jacket temperature) within a time set by its closed-loop dynamics — typically λi + θi, which is small. Everything the inner loop encloses (valve hysteresis and stiction, supply pressure variation, the inner section's lag) is therefore removed from the outer loop's model, so the outer loop's effective dead time falls to roughly θo plus the inner loop's own closed-loop time, and its effective gain becomes linear and constant. The limit of the benefit is set by two things: (1) the speed separation — once the inner loop's closed-loop response is no longer small compared with the outer loop's dynamics (the usual requirement is a factor of 3–10), the inner loop stops looking like an instantaneous actuator and the two loops begin to interact, at which point cascade adds complexity without gain; and (2) the irreducible outer dead time — cascade removes nothing that lies downstream of the inner measurement, so a reactor with 90 s of its own transport and thermal delay still has 90 s after cascading, and the remaining route to improvement is feedforward, moving the outer measurement, or a process change.
Final check 2: A plant wants to reduce variability on a product quality variable. List, in order, the interventions you would evaluate — and say which single measurement you would take first.
Tap to reveal ↓A: First measurement: a period of routine operating data on the quality variable and its loop — from which you get σe, the service factor, output saturation, any oscillation and its period, and the Harris index η. That single dataset tells you whether the variance is even removable by control before you spend anything. Then, in order of cost: (1) Base layer — is the loop in automatic, is the valve healthy, is the measurement filtered to death, is the scan period sensible? A loop in manual or a sticking valve outranks every other consideration. (2) Retune if η is low and the tuning is conservative — free, and often the largest single step. (3) Linearise — square-root extraction, valve trim or characteriser, pressure compensation. Configuration-level work that makes one tuning correct everywhere. (4) Cascade to a fast intermediate variable if one exists — cheap, robust, removes utility and valve disturbances. (5) Feedforward from the dominant measured disturbance, checking θd ≥ θp, and take the measurement from an upstream setpoint if that wins the race. (6) Restructure — ratio, override, split-range, or re-pair after computing the RGA if several loops interact. (7) Inferential measurement if the quality analyser is the bottleneck, so a fast loop can close at all. (8) Reduce dead time physically — sensor location, sample line, mixing, analyser cycle. Frequently the definitive fix and often cheaper than software. (9) MPC if the unit is genuinely multivariable and constrained with an economic objective. (10) Process change — buffering volume, a better valve, a second actuator. And the closing step in every case: re-measure σe and η afterwards and convert the improvement into the money argument of Page 1, because that is what funds the next project.
Final check 3: Why must a cross-limiting combustion scheme use measured flows rather than setpoints, and what is the general principle behind that rule?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Because the scheme's purpose is to guarantee that the air actually flowing is always sufficient for the fuel actually flowing. If the select blocks compare the two controllers' setpoints, the constraint is satisfied whenever the two commands are in proportion — which they always are by construction, so the interlock is vacuous. The exposing failure is anything that stops the air loop from achieving its setpoint: a stuck FD fan damper, a failed fan, a blocked filter, a saturated actuator, or the air controller left in manual. The fuel ceiling then rises with a command that reality is not following, fuel is admitted for air that is not there, and the furnace goes fuel-rich — unburnt fuel, smoke, flame instability and, at worst, an explosion on re-ignition. The general principle: limiting and interlock logic must act on measured values, never on commands. The same rule appears throughout the subject — external reset feedback uses the selected output (or valve position feedback) rather than the computed one; cascade windup protection uses the inner loop's actual PV and status; and safety instrumented functions per IEC 61511 require rated, direct measurement of the hazardous condition rather than an inference or a command. A command expresses intent; only a measurement expresses what the plant is doing, and protection must be based on the latter.
Final check 4: A 2×2 scheme has λ₁₁ = −3. What does this mean physically, and what will happen if it is commissioned?
Tap to reveal ↓A: A negative relative gain means the loop's gain changes sign between the other loop being open and being closed. Physically: with loop 2 in manual, increasing u₁ raises y₁; with loop 2 in automatic, loop 2 reacts to the interaction and its response overwhelms the direct effect, so the net result of increasing u₁ is that y₁ falls. The direct and indirect paths oppose each other, and the indirect one wins. What happens on commissioning: the engineer tunes loop 1 with loop 2 in manual, and it behaves perfectly — correct action, sensible response, good margins. The moment loop 2 is put in automatic, loop 1's controller is acting with the wrong sign for the actual closed-loop process: its corrections make the error grow, and the pair runs away until something saturates or trips. The failure is configuration-dependent and appears only when both loops are in auto, which is exactly why it survives to site. Worse, the fault presents as "loop 1 is unstable", tempting the engineer to detune loop 1 — which does not help, because the sign is wrong at any gain. The correct actions: compute the RGA before configuring and reject any pairing with a negative diagonal element; check the Niederlinski index as a second test (NI < 0 guarantees instability for a decentralised scheme with integral action); re-pair to the alternative, whose λ will be 1 − (−3) = 4 — significant interaction requiring detuning or sequencing, but at least sign-consistent and stabilisable; and if neither pairing is acceptable, change the controlled variables or move to MPC.
Final check 5: Name three situations where the correct advanced-control answer is "do nothing more" — and justify each.
Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) A loop with a Harris index near 1. Its variance is already at the theoretical minimum imposed by its dead time, so no controller — PID, Smith predictor or MPC — can reduce it. Anything further must be a process change (move the measurement, add feedforward, add buffering) or nothing. Retuning such a loop is pure waste, and recognising it protects the engineer's time. (2) An averaging surge-tank level. Here variability in the level is the objective: the tank exists to absorb flow variation so it is not passed downstream. Tight control defeats its purpose by transmitting every upstream upset onward. The right answer is deliberately loose P-only or wide-band control with the setpoint mid-tank — and the temptation to "fix" it because the trend wanders should be resisted and explained. (3) A single loop with no measurable disturbance, no constraint, no interaction and modest dead time that is already meeting its specification. A well-tuned PID is the correct, cheapest and most maintainable answer; adding cascade, feedforward or MPC would add failure modes, maintenance burden and an owner requirement for no measurable benefit. Honourable additions: a loop whose benefit case does not close (reduced variability that converts to no money), and a scheme whose plant is about to be modified or shut down. The professional point: knowing when to stop is part of the discipline. Every structure added must be justified by a named limitation it removes and a benefit it earns, and must have someone to own it in three years — otherwise the honest recommendation is to leave a good PID alone and spend the effort on the twenty loops in the plant that are still in manual.

WE1 · Cascade Design and the Benefit It Buys

Given: A reactor temperature loop. Valve → jacket: K = 0.8 °C/%, τ = 25 s, θ = 5 s. Jacket → reactor: K = 1.4 °C/°C, τ = 500 s, θ = 70 s. Compare single-loop control with a cascade, using SIMC (λ = θ) throughout.

Step 1 — the SINGLE-LOOP process (valve → reactor, series combination) Gain: K = 0.8 × 1.4 = 1.12 °C per % valve Dead time: θ = 5 + 70 = 75 s Time constants: two lags, 25 s and 500 s. Using the half-rule (Skogestad), the smaller lag is split — half added to the dominant τ, half to dead time: τ ≈ 500 + 25/2 = 512 s θ ≈ 75 + 25/2 = 87.5 s Controllability: θ/τ = 87.5/512 = 0.17 SIMC with λ = θ = 87.5 s: Kc = τ/(K(λ+θ)) = 512/(1.12 × 175) = 512/196 = 2.61 %/°C Ti = min(τ, 4(λ+θ)) = min(512, 700) = 512 s Achievable closed-loop time constant ≈ λ = 87.5 s; settling ≈ 4λ+θ ≈ 437 s Step 2 — the INNER loop (valve → jacket) K = 0.8, τ = 25, θ = 5, λi = θi = 5 s Kc,i = 25/(0.8 × 10) = 3.13 %/°C Ti,i = min(25, 40) = 25 s Inner closed-loop time constant ≈ 5 s, effective inner dead time ≈ 5 s → the inner loop responds in about λi + θi = 10 s Step 3 — the OUTER loop sees the closed inner loop + the slow section Outer process ≈ unity gain from jacket SP to jacket T, then 1.4 °C/°C, so Ko = 1.4 °C per °C of jacket setpoint τo = 500 s θo = 70 + (inner closed-loop response ≈ 10 s) = 80 s SIMC with λo = θo = 80 s: Kc,o = 500/(1.4 × 160) = 500/224 = 2.23 °C/°C Ti,o = min(500, 640) = 500 s Step 4 — SPEED SEPARATION CHECK inner response 10 s vs outer response 80 s → ratio 8:1 ✓ (want ≥ 3:1) Step 5 — compare Single loop: closed-loop τ ≈ 87.5 s Cascade: closed-loop τ ≈ 80.0 s → only ~9% faster on SETPOINT But on a coolant-supply DISTURBANCE: single loop must wait for the reactor to move: ~87.5 s before it even begins to correct, and the reactor temperature deviates throughout. Cascade catches it in the jacket in ~10 s, so the reactor barely moves — a reduction in peak deviation of roughly an order of magnitude.

Discussion / insight. The arithmetic makes the crucial point that beginners miss: cascade barely improves setpoint response, but transforms disturbance rejection. The setpoint path still has to go through the 500 s reactor and its 70 s dead time whatever you do. What cascade eliminates is everything upstream of the jacket measurement — coolant supply temperature and pressure, valve stiction, and the valve's nonlinear installed characteristic — which for a real reactor is where most of the variance comes from. Note also the half-rule in Step 1: when reducing a two-lag process to FOPDT, the smaller time constant is split between τ and θ rather than simply dropped, which keeps the model honest near the crossover frequency. And note the speed-separation check in Step 4 is not decoration — had the jacket been slow (say τ = 200 s), the ratio would have fallen below 3:1, the loops would interact, and cascade would add complexity for nothing.

WE2 · Feedforward Design, Sign, Gain and Dynamics

Given: A heat exchanger heats process fluid with steam. Outlet temperature is the CV, steam valve the MV, and feed flow the disturbance. Step tests: +10% steam valve → outlet +6.0 °C (τ = 80 s, θ = 15 s). +20% feed flow → outlet −9.0 °C (τ = 40 s, θ = 30 s). Design the feedforward.

Step 1 — the two path gains Manipulated path: Kp = +6.0/10 = +0.60 °C per % valve Disturbance path: Kd = −9.0/20 = −0.45 °C per % feed flow Step 2 — static feedforward gain Kff = −Kd/Kp = −(−0.45)/0.60 = +0.75 % valve per % feed flow Sign check by physics: more feed → outlet gets COLDER → need MORE steam → valve should OPEN → positive Kff ✓ (matches the arithmetic) Step 3 — verify with a numerical example Feed flow rises 20%. Disturbance effect = 20 × (−0.45) = −9.0 °C Feedforward action = 20 × 0.75 = +15% on the valve Its effect = 15 × 0.60 = +9.0 °C → cancels exactly ✓ Step 4 — dead-time feasibility (the make-or-break test) θd = 30 s (disturbance reaches the outlet) θp = 15 s (our correction reaches the outlet) θd − θp = +15 s ≥ 0 → REALISABLE ✓ Ideal compensator therefore includes a 15 s pure delay: apply the correction 15 s AFTER the flow change, so it arrives at the same moment as the disturbance. (Applying it immediately would over-cool for 15 s, then be correct — a small transient in the OPPOSITE direction.) Step 5 — lead/lag dynamic term Gff(s) = Kff · (τps + 1)/(τds + 1) · e−(θd−θp)s = 0.75 · (80s + 1)/(40s + 1) · e−15s Lead/lag ratio = τp/τd = 80/40 = 2.0 → a LEAD of 2:1 Interpretation: our path is SLOWER than the disturbance path, so the correction must be kicked (over-applied initially, then relaxed) to keep up. Step 6 — practical settings and commissioning Configure: gain 0.75, lead 80 s, lag 40 s, delay 15 s, output clamped ±40%. Commission at HALF gain (0.375) first; step the feed flow; observe residual: · outlet still dips → increase Kff · outlet now rises → Kff too high Leave the feedback PID in AUTO throughout to trim the remainder.

Discussion / insight. Three examinable points. First, the sign must be confirmed twice — by arithmetic and by physics — because a sign error doubles the disturbance instead of cancelling it, and the resulting excursion is usually blamed on the feedback loop. Second, the dead-time test in Step 4 is the go/no-go decision, and here we pass with 15 s to spare, which is why a delay block appears in the compensator: feedforward is not simply "act fast", it is "act at the right time". Third, the lead/lag ratio of 2:1 has a physical reading — the steam path is twice as sluggish as the feed-flow path, so a pure static gain would arrive too late and the outlet would dip transiently before recovering. In practice many plants implement only the static gain plus the delay and accept the transient; the lead/lag earns its place when the τ mismatch is large, as it is here.

WE3 · RGA, Pairing and the Change-of-Variables Fix

Given: A blending header. MVs: u₁ = stream A valve, u₂ = stream B valve. CVs: y₁ = total flow, y₂ = composition (% A). Gains from step tests: K₁₁ = 1.0, K₁₂ = 1.0, K₂₁ = 0.6, K₂₂ = −0.4. Assess the pairing, then propose a better scheme.

Step 1 — relative gain λ₁₁ = 1/(1 − K₁₂K₂₁/(K₁₁K₂₂)) = 1/(1 − (1.0 × 0.6)/(1.0 × (−0.4))) = 1/(1 − (0.6/−0.4)) = 1/(1 + 1.5) = 1/2.5 = 0.40 Array: ⎡ 0.40 0.60 ⎤ ⎣ 0.60 0.40 ⎦ (rows and columns sum to 1 ✓) Step 2 — interpret λ₁₁ = 0.40 lies in the 0.3–0.7 band → SIGNIFICANT interaction, but the pairing is at least sign-consistent (λ > 0) and therefore stabilisable. The alternative pairing has λ = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60 — also in the same band, so swapping does not resolve it. Neither pairing is comfortable. Step 3 — Niederlinski index det(K) = (1.0)(−0.4) − (1.0)(0.6) = −0.4 − 0.6 = −1.0 Π Kii = (1.0)(−0.4) = −0.4 NI = det(K)/Π Kii = (−1.0)/(−0.4) = +2.5 > 0 → not ruled out ✓ (NI < 0 would have GUARANTEED instability for independent loops with reset.) Step 4 — the change of variables (the real answer) Note the physics: total flow F = FA + FB, composition x = FA/(FA+FB). Rather than pairing two valves against two coupled outputs, control: · TOTAL FLOW with the SUM of the two flows (a flow controller whose output is split between the streams), and · COMPOSITION with the RATIO FA/FB (a ratio station, Page 4). In the transformed variables the interaction largely disappears, because changing the ratio at constant sum does not change total flow, and changing the sum at constant ratio does not change composition — BY CONSTRUCTION. Step 5 — the resulting structure Total-flow FIC → demand ───┬──→ ×(1/(1+R)) → stream A flow FIC → valve A └──→ ×(R/(1+R)) → stream B flow FIC → valve B Composition AIC → ratio R ────┘ Two flow cascades underneath (Page 2) linearise both valves.

Discussion / insight. This example demonstrates the most valuable move in multivariable control, and it is not a controller at all — it is choosing better controlled and manipulated variables. The RGA of 0.40 tells you honestly that two independent PID loops on (total flow, composition) using (valve A, valve B) will interact badly however they are tuned or paired, because both valves affect both outputs by construction. Transforming to sum and ratio makes the interaction structurally near-zero: it is the same physical plant, described in coordinates that decouple it. The examinable general principle: if the RGA is poor for every pairing, stop looking for a pairing and look for a transformation. Sum-and-ratio for blending, reflux-ratio instead of reflux flow for distillation (Page 12), and total-firing-rate plus air/fuel ratio for combustion (Page 4) are all the same idea. Only when no transformation helps — genuinely ill-conditioned plants such as high-purity columns — is MPC the right escalation.

WE4 · Override Scheme with Windup Analysis

Given: A furnace. Normal control: outlet temperature TIC on the fuel valve (fail-closed, air-to-open), Kc = 1.5, Ti = 200 s. Constraint: tube-metal temperature must not exceed 540 °C, controlled by a second TIC with Kc = 3.0, Ti = 60 s. Design the select, and quantify the windup if external reset is omitted.

Step 1 — which select? Valve is fail-closed / air-to-open: MORE output = MORE fuel = HOTTER. The tube-metal constraint is a MAXIMUM, so when it binds it must REDUCE fuel, i.e. reduce the output. The scheme must therefore pass the SMALLER signal: valve = LOW-select( outlet TIC output , tube-metal TIC output ) Step 2 — normal operation Tube metal well below 540 °C → its controller's error is large and positive → its output saturates HIGH (say 100%) → the LOW select passes the outlet TIC → outlet temperature is in command. ✓ Step 3 — constraint operation Tube metal approaches 540 °C → its output falls; when it drops below the outlet TIC's output, the select switches and the constraint takes over. The plant now "rides the limit": fuel is held at whatever the tubes allow, and the outlet temperature falls below its setpoint. That is correct and intended behaviour — the alternative is a trip. Step 4 — WINDUP WITHOUT EXTERNAL RESET (the quantification) While the constraint is in command, the outlet TIC has a persistent error. Suppose the outlet runs 12 °C below setpoint for 10 minutes (600 s). Its integral contribution grows at Kc·e/Ti per second: rate = 1.5 × 12 / 200 = 0.09 % per second over 600 s → +54% of output accumulated Its output therefore climbs from, say, 70% to 100% and PINS at the limit (and with a poor implementation keeps integrating internally beyond it). When the tubes cool and the constraint controller's output rises back above the outlet TIC's, the select hands control back to a controller demanding 100% fuel — a step of +30% or more onto the valve. The furnace over-fires, the tube metal shoots back through 540 °C, and the scheme cycles between the two controllers with a period of many minutes. The "protection" has become the disturbance. Step 5 — the fix, quantified External reset feedback: both controllers' integral terms are driven by the ACTUALLY SELECTED output rather than their own computed output. The losing controller then integrates only to the point where its output sits just above the selected value (for a low select), so: handover step ≈ 0%, and control transfers smoothly in both directions. Alternative if unavailable: freeze the integral whenever "not selected". Step 6 — commissioning test (non-negotiable) Deliberately drive the furnace into the tube-metal limit and back out again, twice, and record both controller outputs. If either output parks at 0% or 100%, external reset is not working — whatever the configuration screen says.

Discussion / insight. The value of the numbers in Step 4 is that they turn a vague warning into a predictable magnitude: with Kc = 1.5, Ti = 200 s and a 12 °C error, the losing controller accumulates 54% of output in ten minutes — easily enough to slam the valve on handover. Notice also the asymmetry of the tunings: the constraint controller is deliberately faster (Kc = 3.0, Ti = 60 s) because it is protecting equipment, while the production controller is gentler. They never operate simultaneously, so their tunings need not be compatible — a point that surprises engineers used to single loops. Finally, note what this scheme is not: it keeps the furnace off the tube-metal trip, but it shares sensors and logic with the control system and is not a rated protective function. The actual protection against tube failure must be an independent safety instrumented function (A52).

WE5 · Benefits Case for a Variability Reduction

Given: A dryer must deliver product at ≥ 2.0% moisture (below that, the customer rejects for over-drying cost; the constraint is a minimum). Current operation: mean 2.60%, σ = 0.30%, running a 2σ margin. Throughput 12 t/h, 8000 h/yr. Evaporating 1% of moisture from a tonne of product costs 28 kWh; energy is 42 LKR/kWh. Advanced control (feedforward from feed rate plus an inferential moisture inference) is expected to halve σ. Quantify the benefit and the payback on a 4.2 M LKR project.

Step 1 — where the current setpoint comes from Target = limit + k·σ = 2.0 + 2 × 0.30 = 2.60% ✓ (matches current operation) Step 2 — the new achievable target σnew = 0.15%, same 2σ confidence: Targetnew = 2.0 + 2 × 0.15 = 2.30% Moisture given away recovered = 2.60 − 2.30 = 0.30 percentage points (i.e. we can leave 0.30% more water in the product and still meet the spec) Step 3 — energy saved Energy per tonne saved = 0.30 × 28 = 8.4 kWh/t Annual tonnage = 12 t/h × 8000 h = 96 000 t/yr Annual energy saved = 8.4 × 96 000 = 806 400 kWh/yr Step 4 — annual money Saving = 806 400 × 42 = 33.87 M LKR/yr Step 5 — payback Payback = 4.2 / 33.87 = 0.124 yr = ≈ 1.5 months Step 6 — the honest sensitivity analysis (what a reviewer will ask) · If σ only improves to 0.20% (not 0.15%): target 2.40%, recovery 0.20 pts, saving = 22.6 M LKR/yr → payback ≈ 2.2 months. Still overwhelming. · If service factor is only 60%: benefit ×0.6 = 20.3 M LKR/yr → ≈ 2.5 months. · Additional unquantified benefit: less product rejected, less over-drying damage, higher saleable yield (water sold as product weight). · Risk: the moisture inference must be validated and owned (Page 8), or the σ improvement decays and the benefit goes with it.

Discussion / insight. This is the calculation that gets advanced control funded, and it is worth more to an engineer's career than any block diagram. The logic is entirely general: a specification limit plus a safety margin proportional to σ means the average give-away is k·σ, so halving σ halves the give-away — and that recovered margin is real, calculable money. Note three professional touches. First, reverse-engineer the current setpoint from the limit and σ (Step 1) to confirm the plant really is running a 2σ margin; if the actual target is 2.60% but 2σ would only require 2.40%, the extra 0.20% is operator conservatism and some benefit is available with no project at all — a finding worth reporting immediately. Second, quote the sensitivity, because a reviewer's first question is "what if it only works half as well?" — a case that still pays back in months is far more persuasive than a single optimistic number. Third, state the sustaining requirement in the same document: the benefit exists only while the scheme is on and the inference is valid, which is why the service factor and an owner belong in the business case rather than in a later handover note.

Problem Set

Ten problems in ascending difficulty. Attempt each on paper before revealing.

P1. A level loop manipulates a valve whose flow varies with header pressure. Name the single structural change that removes the pressure disturbance, and state the required speed relationship.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Cascade the level controller to a flow controller — the level loop's output becomes a flow setpoint in m³/h rather than a valve position in percent. The flow loop then rejects header pressure variation, valve hysteresis and stiction locally, within a second or two, before the level moves at all. Required speed relationship: the inner (flow) loop must be at least 3–10× faster than the outer (level) loop; flow loops typically respond in 1–5 s against level loops of minutes, so this is comfortably satisfied. Flow is the most common inner loop in industry precisely because it linearises the valve almost completely. Two configuration notes: tune the flow loop first with the level loop in manual, and configure external reset feedback so the level controller cannot wind up if the flow loop saturates.
P2. Step tests give Kp = 1.6 °C/% and Kd = +2.4 °C per unit disturbance. Compute the static feedforward gain and state its sign convention.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Kff = −Kd/Kp = −2.4/1.6 = −1.50 % MV per unit of disturbance. The negative sign means the correction opposes the disturbance: since a positive disturbance raises the PV by 2.4 °C per unit, and each 1% of MV raises the PV by 1.6 °C, we must reduce the MV by 1.5% per unit of disturbance to cancel it. Verify numerically: a disturbance of +3 units would raise the PV by 3 × 2.4 = +7.2 °C, while the feedforward moves the MV by 3 × (−1.5) = −4.5%, whose effect is −4.5 × 1.6 = −7.2 °C ✓. Always confirm the sign against the physics as well as the arithmetic — a sign error doubles the disturbance rather than cancelling it, and the resulting excursion is usually misattributed to the feedback loop.
P3. For the loop in P2, the disturbance reaches the PV in 20 s and the MV's effect reaches it in 45 s. Is exact feedforward realisable? What is the best available scheme?
Tap to reveal ↓A: No. The ideal compensator contains e−(θd−θp)s = e−(20−45)s = e+25s, a positive exponent — a 25-second predictor, which is not physically realisable. The disturbance beats our correction to the measurement by 25 s, so some excursion is unavoidable. Best available scheme: (1) apply the static feedforward immediately with no delay term, so the correction is already fully in place when the disturbance arrives — this does not prevent the first 25 s of deviation but substantially reduces its peak and shortens recovery; (2) get the disturbance measurement earlier, which is the real fix — take it from an upstream measurement or, better, from the setpoint of an upstream controller, buying the transport time and often turning θd into something larger than θp; (3) reduce θp by moving the PV measurement closer or speeding the actuator; (4) keep the feedback trim in auto to clean up the residual. The examinable principle: feedforward feasibility is a race between two dead times, and the correct response to losing it is to move a measurement, not to add mathematics.
P4. A boiler burns fuel oil with a stoichiometric air requirement of 14.4 kg air per kg fuel, at 20% excess air. Fuel flow is 800 kg/h. Compute the air flow setpoint and the approximate flue O₂.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Stoichiometric air = 14.4 × 800 = 11 520 kg/h. With 20% excess: air setpoint = 11 520 × 1.20 = 13 824 kg/h. The air/fuel ratio in use is therefore 13 824/800 = 17.28 kg/kg. Flue O₂: the excess air carries its oxygen through unreacted. Using the standard approximation O₂% ≈ 21 × EA/(1 + EA) where EA is the excess-air fraction relative to total: with 20% excess, the excess air is 0.20 × 11 520 = 2304 kg/h out of about 13 824 + 800 = 14 624 kg/h of flue gas, and taking air as 21% O₂ by volume the dry flue O₂ works out at roughly 3.5–4% — the familiar target band for oil firing. Engineering commentary: 20% excess air is a reasonable target for oil (gas typically runs 10–15%, coal 20–25%); too little risks unburnt fuel and smoke, too much wastes energy up the stack, with a common rule of thumb that every 1% of excess O₂ above target costs of the order of half a percent of boiler efficiency. In service the ratio would be trimmed by a flue-O₂ analyser closing a slow outer loop on the ratio setpoint — a cascade whose inner loops are the fuel and air flow controllers, with cross-limiting (Page 4) guaranteeing air-rich transients.
P5. Gains are K₁₁ = 2.0, K₁₂ = 0.4, K₂₁ = 0.5, K₂₂ = 1.8. Compute λ₁₁ and advise on pairing and tuning.
Tap to reveal ↓A: λ₁₁ = 1/(1 − K₁₂K₂₁/(K₁₁K₂₂)) = 1/(1 − (0.4 × 0.5)/(2.0 × 1.8)) = 1/(1 − 0.20/3.60) = 1/(1 − 0.0556) = 1/0.9444 = 1.059. That is very close to 1, so the interaction is mild: the array is [1.06, −0.06; −0.06, 1.06]. Advice: keep the diagonal pairing (u₁→y₁, u₂→y₂), tune the two loops independently in the normal way, and expect essentially no interaction problems — a loop's gain changes by only about 6% between the other loop being open and closed. No decoupler, no detuning, no sequencing required. Sanity checks worth quoting: det(K) = 2.0 × 1.8 − 0.4 × 0.5 = 3.60 − 0.20 = 3.40, comfortably away from zero, so the plant is well conditioned; and NI = 3.40/(2.0 × 1.8) = 0.944 > 0, so the decentralised scheme is not ruled out. The contrast worth noting: compare with the blending example (λ = 25, det = 0.12) — the arithmetic is identical but the conclusion is opposite, which is exactly why the RGA is computed rather than guessed.
P6. A split-range scheme gives 3 °C per % of controller output on cooling and 11 °C per % on heating. Explain the consequence and give three fixes.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The process gain differs by a factor of 11/3 = 3.7× between the two halves of the output range, so a single set of tuning constants cannot suit both: tuned for cooling the loop will be roughly 3.7× too aggressive on heating (overshoot, oscillation); tuned for heating it will be 3.7× too sluggish on cooling. The symptom is a loop that behaves acceptably on one side of setpoint and badly on the other — and because the transition happens at the split point, it looks like an intermittent fault. Three fixes. (1) Re-range the split point and/or resize the valves so the two halves present similar process gain — e.g. give heating a smaller valve or a narrower output span, which is the cleanest structural answer. (2) Apply an output characteriser to one or both halves so equal controller increments produce equal duty increments, cancelling the mismatch in software. (3) Gain-schedule on which half is active — switch the controller gain by roughly the 3.7 ratio when the output crosses the split point, with rate limiting so the change is not a step. A fourth, increasingly preferred option in modern systems: use two separate controllers with a shared setpoint and a deadband or select scheme, each tuned for its own dynamics — split-range is a legacy solution to a single-output limitation most platforms no longer have. Whichever is chosen, also decide deliberately between a gap (saves energy, leaves a dead zone) and an overlap (smooth handover, may heat and cool simultaneously).
P7. An override scheme has a production controller (Kc = 2.0, Ti = 150 s) that loses the select for 8 minutes with a persistent error of 6 units. Estimate the accumulated integral windup and its effect on handover.
Tap to reveal ↓A: The integral term accumulates at a rate of Kc·e/Ti per second = 2.0 × 6/150 = 0.08% of output per second. Over 8 minutes (480 s) that is 0.08 × 480 = +38.4% of output. So when the constraint releases and the production controller wins the select again, its output is about 38% higher than the value the plant actually needs, and the valve steps by that amount — a large, fast upset that will typically drive the process straight back into the constraint, producing a slow cycle between the two controllers with a period of many minutes. The fix is external reset feedback: both controllers' integral terms are driven by the selected output, so the losing controller's output parks just above the selected value (for a low select) and the handover step is essentially zero. If the platform does not support it, use the "not selected" status to freeze the integral, or configure output tracking. Commissioning implication: this fault is load-dependent and intermittent, so it survives a normal FAT — you must deliberately drive the plant into and out of the constraint twice during commissioning and record both controller outputs. If either parks at 0% or 100%, the windup protection is not working whatever the configuration screen claims.
P8. A quality loop has a Harris index η = 0.85 and operations complain about variability. What do you tell them, and what do you propose?
Tap to reveal ↓A: Tell them, with the number, that the loop is already operating close to its theoretical minimum-variance limit: about 85% of the variance that can be removed by feedback control has been removed, so retuning will buy at most a marginal improvement. The residual variance is dominated by the loop's dead time — no controller can react to a disturbance during the interval before its effect is measurable — plus unmeasured disturbances. This is a genuinely useful message, because it stops an engineer spending weeks retuning and redirects the effort. What to propose instead, in order: (1) Reduce the dead time physically — move the measurement closer, shorten a sample line, raise the analyser or scan rate, improve mixing. Since the minimum achievable variance scales with the disturbance energy arriving within θ, halving θ genuinely reduces the floor. (2) Feedforward from the dominant measured disturbance, which removes that disturbance from the feedback problem entirely rather than competing with the limit. (3) Cascade to a faster intermediate variable if one exists, which reduces the effective θ the outer loop faces. (4) Inferential measurement if a slow analyser is the reason θ is large — a fast soft sensor with bias update can transform the achievable floor (Page 8). (5) Process change — buffering volume upstream to attenuate the disturbance before it arrives, or removing its source. (6) Finally, check whether the variability actually costs anything (Page 1's benefit arithmetic); if not, document η and close the item. The general point: η near 1 converts "the control is bad" into "the plant's dead time is the limit", which is a different and much more actionable finding.
P9. A product must stay above 92.0% purity. Current operation: mean 93.6%, σ = 0.80%. An MPC project is expected to reduce σ to 0.45%. If each 0.1% of purity given away costs 180 000 LKR/day, quantify the annual benefit at a 2σ margin and 90% service factor.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Check the current margin: limit + 2σ = 92.0 + 2 × 0.80 = 93.6% ✓ — matches the observed mean, confirming the plant really is running a 2σ policy. New target: 92.0 + 2 × 0.45 = 92.9%. Purity give-away recovered = 93.6 − 92.9 = 0.70 percentage points = 7 × 0.1% units. Gross benefit = 7 × 180 000 = 1 260 000 LKR/day; over 365 days = 459.9 M LKR/yr. Adjusted for a 90% service factor = 0.90 × 459.9 = 413.9 M LKR/yr. Professional additions a reviewer will expect. (1) Sensitivity: if σ only reaches 0.60%, the new target is 93.2%, recovery is 0.40 points, and the benefit falls to 4 × 180 000 × 365 × 0.9 = 236.5 M LKR/yr — still very large, which makes the case robust. (2) State the service-factor dependency explicitly, because it is the single biggest risk to realisation and it is an organisational variable, not a technical one — every percentage point of availability is worth about 4.6 M LKR/yr here. (3) Note the assumption that the 2σ policy is retained; if operations will not accept the same statistical risk at a lower margin, the benefit is not realisable however good the control is, so agreeing the margin policy is part of the project. (4) Add the unquantified upside (throughput, energy, less off-spec) and the sustaining cost (model maintenance, an owner) so the number is defensible rather than merely impressive.
P10. Synthesis. A continuous crystalliser: product size distribution is the quality variable (lab measured, 2 h turnaround); it is disturbed by feed concentration (measured on-line) and feed rate (measured); temperature is controlled by a jacket with a single cooling valve; the loop must never exceed a maximum cooling rate or the crystals shatter; and the operators currently run the unit manually. Design a complete control strategy with justification, and state your commissioning sequence.
Tap to reveal ↓A: Diagnose against the Page 1 map first. Four of the five structural limits are present: a quality measurement far too slow to close a loop on (2 h), two measurable disturbances, a hard constraint (maximum cooling rate), and a fast intermediate variable available (jacket temperature). Interaction is modest, so MPC is not the first answer. The strategy, layer by layer. (1) Base layer: fix the reason the unit is in manual — travel-test the cooling valve, bump-test the jacket, tune the jacket temperature loop. Nothing else is meaningful until the valve and the loop work. (2) Cascade: crystalliser temperature (outer, slow) → jacket temperature (inner, fast), with the jacket setpoint clamped. This removes cooling-water supply variation and valve nonlinearity, and gives a place to enforce the constraint. (3) Constraint enforcement: the maximum cooling rate is a limit on the rate of change of temperature, so implement it as a rate limit on the outer controller's setpoint or output plus an override controller on measured cooling rate (dT/dt, or the jacket-to-body ΔT which is its proxy), low-selected with the temperature controller — and configure external reset feedback on both, or the override will wind up (WE4/P7). (4) Feedforward: both disturbances are measured, so compute the required cooling duty from feed rate and feed concentration and inject it ahead of the temperature loop — checking the dead-time race for each, and preferring the upstream setpoints of the feed controllers over their PVs to win it. (5) Inferential quality control: a 2 h lab result cannot close a loop, so build a soft sensor for size distribution from the variables that physically drive it — supersaturation (from temperature and concentration), residence time, cooling profile and agitation — and close a slow loop on the inferred value with the lab result used only as a bias update through a long filter (Page 8). Add input validation and an extrapolation check, with fallback to a fixed temperature profile when the inference is untrustworthy. (6) Nonlinearity: crystallisation kinetics are strongly nonlinear in supersaturation, so expect gain variation — characterise if possible, otherwise gain-schedule on throughput and tune for the highest-gain condition. (7) Not control: if crystal shattering or a runaway has safety or major-loss consequences, the protective function belongs in an independent SIF, not in the override (A52). Commissioning sequence: jacket loop first, alone, and prove it; then the temperature cascade with the rate limit, driving the plant into and out of the cooling-rate limit twice to verify the override and its windup protection; then feedforward at half gain, stepping each disturbance separately and increasing the gain by bisection on the residual; then the soft sensor in monitoring-only mode for several weeks, comparing against lab results on a control chart before it is allowed to act; finally the outer quality loop, slowly, with operators trained on what each layer does and a one-page explanation at the console. Sustaining: named owner, KPI monitoring including the scheme's own configuration (a zeroed feedforward gain must raise a flag), revalidation cadence for the inference, and MoC on filters, task periods and valve changes. And the benefits case: reduced size-distribution variability lets the mean move closer to the specification, which is where the money is (WE5/P9) — quantify it before building, and re-measure σ afterwards to prove it.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Four derivations to reproduce from memory, a symbol glossary, and the seven mistakes that cost marks and plant.

Derivation 1 · The Ideal Feedforward Compensator
Goal: show Gff = −Gd/Gp, and derive the realisability condition. Step 1 — write the output as the sum of both routes. The disturbance d reaches the output through Gd(s); our correction u = Gff(s)·d reaches it through Gp(s): y = Gd(s)·d + Gp(s)·u = Gd(s)·d + Gp(s)·Gff(s)·d = [ Gd(s) + Gp(s)·Gff(s) ] · d Step 2 — demand perfect rejection (y = 0 for any d ≠ 0). The bracket must vanish: Gd(s) + Gp(s)·Gff(s) = 0 Gff(s) = − Gd(s)/Gp(s) ∎ Step 3 — substitute FOPDT models for both paths. Gd = Kde−θds/(τds+1) Gp = Kpe−θps/(τps+1) Gff = −(Kd/Kp) · (τps+1)/(τds+1) · e−(θd−θp)s └ gain ┘ └── lead/lag ──┘ └── delay ──┘ Step 4 — read the three factors. · Gain −Kd/Kp: the static feedforward. Note it needs only the RATIO of the two gains, so absolute calibration errors partially cancel — one reason static feedforward is robust in practice. · Lead/lag (τps+1)/(τds+1): a LEAD when τp > τd (our path is slower, so the correction must be kicked); a LAG when τp < τd. · Delay e−(θd−θp)s: realisable ONLY if θd ≥ θp. Step 5 — the realisability condition, stated exactly. If θd < θp the exponent is POSITIVE, i.e. e+|Δθ|s — a predictor requiring knowledge of d before it happens. Perfect feedforward is then IMPOSSIBLE, and the best realisable action is the static gain applied immediately, accepting an unavoidable excursion of duration (θp − θd). Equally, if the relative degree of Gp exceeds that of Gd, the ideal Gff would require differentiation — so a filter must be added, again making the compensator approximate rather than exact.

What it teaches. The derivation shows that feedforward is model inversion: we are asking for Gp−1, and every practical limitation follows from the fact that a physical process cannot be inverted exactly. You cannot invert a delay (that would be prediction), you cannot invert extra lags without differentiating, and you cannot invert an RHP zero without creating an unstable compensator. Those three impossibilities are precisely the three limits on feedforward, and they are the same three limits that bound MPC and every other model-based scheme — MPC simply handles them by explicit optimisation rather than by pretending they are not there.

Derivation 2 · Why the Relative Gain Array Works
Goal: derive λ₁₁ for a 2×2 plant and show what "relative gain" means physically. Step 1 — the OPEN-LOOP gain from u₁ to y₁ (loop 2 in manual, so u₂ is fixed): (∂y₁/∂u₁)u₂ constant = K₁₁ Step 2 — the CLOSED-LOOP gain from u₁ to y₁ (loop 2 in automatic, so it moves u₂ to hold y₂ constant). Impose y₂ = constant: Δy₂ = K₂₁Δu₁ + K₂₂Δu₂ = 0 → Δu₂ = −(K₂₁/K₂₂)·Δu₁ Substitute into the y₁ equation: Δy₁ = K₁₁Δu₁ + K₁₂Δu₂ = K₁₁Δu₁ − K₁₂(K₂₁/K₂₂)Δu₁ = [ K₁₁ − K₁₂K₂₁/K₂₂ ] · Δu₁ (∂y₁/∂u₁)y₂ constant = K₁₁ − K₁₂K₂₁/K₂₂ Step 3 — the RELATIVE gain is the ratio of the two: λ₁₁ = open-loop gain / closed-loop gain = K₁₁ / [K₁₁ − K₁₂K₂₁/K₂₂] Divide numerator and denominator by K₁₁: λ₁₁ = 1 / (1 − K₁₂K₂₁/(K₁₁K₂₂)) ∎ Step 4 — read the consequences directly off the formula. · λ = 1 ⟺ K₁₂K₂₁ = 0: no interaction; the loop's gain is unchanged whether the other loop is open or closed. Perfect. · λ > 1: the closed-loop gain is SMALLER than the open-loop gain — the other loop opposes us, so we need more effort than the open-loop test suggested. · 0 < λ < 1: the closed-loop gain is LARGER — the other loop helps us, so a controller tuned open-loop will be too aggressive when the other closes. · λ < 0 ⟺ K₁₂K₂₁/(K₁₁K₂₂) > 1: the cross-product DOMINATES the direct product, so the sign of the closed-loop gain is OPPOSITE to the open-loop gain. A controller that works with the other loop in manual runs away with it in auto. Never use such a pairing. · λ → ∞ ⟺ K₁₂K₂₁ → K₁₁K₂₂ ⟺ det(K) → 0: the plant is nearly singular; the two MVs are doing almost the same thing, and no controller is robust.

What it teaches. The RGA is not a heuristic index — it is exactly the ratio of a loop's gain in two situations that an engineer actually creates: commissioning with the other loop in manual, then putting it in automatic. That is why λ predicts the specific, notorious failure of interacting schemes: the loop tunes beautifully in isolation and misbehaves when its neighbour is closed. The derivation also explains why the RGA is dimensionless and scale-independent (it is a ratio of gains, so units cancel), which makes it comparable across wildly different processes — and why the determinant appearing in the denominator ties interaction to conditioning, connecting the pairing question to the robustness question.

Derivation 3 · The Cross-Limiting Guarantee
Goal: prove that cross-limited combustion can never go fuel-rich during a transient. Definitions. Let R be the required air/fuel ratio, D the firing demand, and Fmeas, Ameas the MEASURED fuel and air flows. The scheme sets: ASP = max( D , R·Fmeas ) (high select) FSP = min( D , Ameas/R ) (low select) Step 1 — the fuel flow can never exceed what the measured air supports. From the low select, for all t: FSP(t) ≤ Ameas(t)/R → R·FSP(t) ≤ Ameas(t) Provided the fuel flow controller does not overshoot its setpoint (a tuning requirement — hence fuel loops in this service are tuned without overshoot), Fmeas ≤ FSP during an increase, so: R·Fmeas ≤ R·FSP ≤ Ameas → the mixture is AIR-RICH ✓ Step 2 — the air flow can never fall below what the measured fuel requires. From the high select, for all t: ASP(t) ≥ R·Fmeas(t) so as fuel falls, the air floor falls only as fast as the fuel ACTUALLY falls — air can never lead fuel downward. ✓ Step 3 — behaviour on a load INCREASE (D steps up). Air: ASP = max(D↑, R·Fmeas) = D↑ immediately → air starts rising at once. Fuel: FSP = min(D↑, Ameas/R) = Ameas/R → fuel is CAPPED by the air that has actually arrived, so it follows the air up. Air leads. ✓ Step 4 — behaviour on a load DECREASE (D steps down). Fuel: FSP = min(D↓, Ameas/R) = D↓ immediately → fuel starts falling at once. Air: ASP = max(D↓, R·Fmeas) = R·Fmeas → air is FLOORED by the fuel still flowing, so it follows the fuel down. Fuel leads. ✓ Conclusion: in BOTH directions the mixture stays at or above stoichiometric air. The asymmetry is produced entirely by using max() on air and min() on fuel, each referenced to the OTHER stream's MEASURED flow. ∎

What it teaches. Two things worth carrying. First, the guarantee depends critically on the selects referencing measured flows: replace Fmeas and Ameas with setpoints and both inequalities in Steps 1 and 2 become statements about commands rather than reality, so a stuck damper or a failed fan voids the proof entirely. Second, the proof contains a tuning requirement hidden inside a logic scheme — Step 1 needs Fmeas ≤ FSP during an increase, i.e. the fuel flow loop must not overshoot. A fuel loop tuned for a fast, slightly overshooting response can transiently exceed its own setpoint and briefly break the air-rich guarantee. This is a good example of why logic and tuning cannot be designed independently, and why combustion fuel loops are deliberately tuned conservatively.

Derivation 4 · Variability Reduction Converts to Money
Goal: derive the standard benefits formula and show why halving σ is worth so much. Step 1 — the operating policy. A product must satisfy a one-sided specification (say a MINIMUM purity or a MINIMUM moisture). Operations choose a target that keeps the probability of violation acceptably small: Target = Limit + k·σ where k is set by the accepted risk (k = 2 → about 2.3% of the time outside for a normal distribution; k = 3 → 0.13%). The quantity k·σ is the GIVE-AWAY: the margin of product quality handed over free of charge to protect against variability. Step 2 — improve the control. Reduce σ from σ₀ to σ₁ at the SAME risk k: Target₁ = Limit + k·σ₁ Recovered give-away = Target₀ − Target₁ = k·(σ₀ − σ₁) Step 3 — value it. With v = value per unit of give-away per unit of production, and Q = annual production: Annual benefit = k·(σ₀ − σ₁) · v · Q Step 4 — adjust for reality. The scheme only earns while it is running, so multiply by the service factor S: Realised benefit = S · k · (σ₀ − σ₁) · v · Q ∎ Step 5 — the halving case, worked symbolically. If σ₁ = σ₀/2 then recovered give-away = k·σ₀/2 — i.e. halving the standard deviation recovers HALF of the entire existing margin, whatever its size. This is why variability projects have such striking paybacks: the benefit scales with the margin the plant is already giving away, which on a poorly controlled unit is large. Step 6 — the diagnostic use of the same formula. Rearranged: kactual = (Target₀ − Limit)/σ₀ If kactual comes out well above the plant's stated policy (say 3.5 when policy is 2), the excess is operator conservatism, and part of the benefit is available immediately with NO project — just an agreed setpoint change. Always compute this before proposing capital.

What it teaches. This is the derivation that funds the engineering, and Step 6 is the part that distinguishes a professional from an enthusiast. Reverse-engineering k from the current target and σ tests whether the plant is actually running the policy it claims: if it is running 3.5σ when 2σ is agreed, you have found free benefit and also learned something important — operations do not trust the current control, which is itself a finding. Note too the honest dependency on S, the service factor: it enters as a straight multiplier, which is the quantitative reason that training, transparency and graceful degradation (Page 14) belong in the business case rather than in a handover note. A brilliant scheme at 50% availability is worth exactly half of a mediocre one that never gets switched off.

Seven Exam & Field Traps
  • 1
    Two different λ's. SIMC's λ is a closed-loop time constant in seconds; the RGA's λ is a dimensionless pairing index. They appear in the same subject and mean entirely different things — state which you mean.
  • 2
    Forgetting the feedforward realisability test. Computing Kff = −Kd/Kp and stopping there. Always check θd ≥ θp; if it fails, say so and move the measurement upstream rather than inventing a compensator.
  • 3
    Omitting windup protection in select and cascade schemes. The losing or open-circuited controller integrates silently and slams the valve on handover. External reset feedback is mandatory, and must be verified by driving the plant into and out of the constraint, not by reading a configuration screen.
  • 4
    Using setpoints instead of measured values in limiting logic. Cross-limiting, overrides and interlocks must compare what the plant is doing, not what it was told. A stuck damper or a loop in manual voids the entire guarantee.
  • 5
    Tuning a cascade in the wrong order, or with insufficient speed separation. Inner first, always, with the outer in manual; and if the inner loop is not at least 3× faster, cascade adds complexity without benefit. Re-tuning the inner invalidates the outer.
  • 6
    Reaching for MPC to solve a pairing or nonlinearity problem. Compute the RGA first — a re-pairing or a change of variables (sum and ratio) is free. Remove nonlinearity at source (square-root extraction, valve trim, characteriser) before scheduling or optimising around it.
  • 7
    Confusing control refinement with protection. Overrides, cross-limiting and antisurge keep the plant off the trip; they are not rated safety functions and share sensors and logic with control. Protection requires an independent SIF per IEC 61511 (A52). State the boundary in the design document.

Eight-Step Hands-On Ladder
  • 1
    Audit one unit's base layer. For every loop: mode, service factor, mean output, saturation, and whether a tuning record exists. Produce the "worst 10" list. This is the single most valuable day an engineer can spend on a plant.
  • 2
    Find a cascade opportunity and cost it. Identify a slow loop whose valve is disturbed by a utility supply, confirm a fast intermediate measurement exists (or price one), and compute the speed separation. Write it up as a one-page proposal.
  • 3
    Design a feedforward on paper for a real loop. Bump-test both paths, compute Kff, check θd ≥ θp, and derive the lead/lag. Half the value is discovering whether the test passes.
  • 4
    Compute an RGA from real step tests. Pick any two interacting loops, step each MV, record both CVs, build the 2×2 gain matrix and evaluate λ and NI. Compare the answer with what the plant actually experiences.
  • 5
    Trace a select scheme in a live DCS and find out whether external reset feedback is configured. Then simulate the handover offline and watch what happens without it — this converts an abstract warning into a memory.
  • 6
    Simulate a cascade in software (Python/Octave/Simulink): build inner and outer FOPDT models, tune with SIMC, and compare single-loop against cascade for a disturbance step rather than a setpoint step. Reproduce the order-of-magnitude difference in WE1.
  • 7
    Write a benefits case for one real variability problem using the Page 1 / WE5 arithmetic, including the reverse-engineered k, a sensitivity table and the service-factor dependency. Have a process engineer challenge it.
  • 8
    Shadow an MPC if you can get access — watch which constraints are active, what happens on an infeasibility, and how operators interact with it. Failing that, build a small MPC in Python with constraints and observe how the move penalty trades speed against robustness.
Self-Assessment — Do You Own This Subject?

Diagnosis

Shown a poorly performing loop, can you decide from evidence whether it needs base-layer repair, retuning, cascade, feedforward, restructuring or nothing at all?

Design

Can you compute Kff and its realisability, choose a select direction from valve action, compute λ and NI for a pairing, and specify the windup protection?

Judgement

Can you say when MPC is justified and when it is overkill — and explain to a manager why the base layer must be fixed first, with numbers?

Delivery

Can you commission a scheme so it fails safely, explains itself to operators, is monitored, and still earns its benefit in three years?

When all four are true you can take a unit that operations run by hand and turn it into one that runs itself — and prove in money that it does.
