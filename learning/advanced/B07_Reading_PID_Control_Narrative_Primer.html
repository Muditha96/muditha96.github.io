📜Reading a P&ID and a Control Narrative

A P&ID is not a picture of a plant. It is a legal, tagged, machine-checkable sentence about what every pipe carries, what every instrument measures, what every valve does when the air fails, and which of those facts a computer is allowed to change. The control narrative is the other half — everything the drawing physically cannot say: numbers, timing, modes, resets. This primer teaches you to read both, letter by letter and clause by clause, then to write the narrative so a programmer cannot misunderstand it. Built around four real faults at a coconut-oil refinery in Ja-Ela that cost LKR 1.59 million a month for seven months — every one of them a documentation error, not an equipment failure — and were closed for LKR 268,000, which the plant earned back in 5.1 days.

Cold Open — Four Faults, Not One Broken Instrument Among Them

A COCONUT-OIL REFINERY, JA-ELA CORE

1,248 tonnes of refined coconut oil a month. Crude oil arrives by road tanker, is neutralised with caustic, bleached with activated earth, then deodorised under vacuum at 235 °C before being filtered and drummed. Eight batches a day, six thousand kilograms each, twenty-six days a month. The plant is fourteen years old, the DCS was replaced four years ago, and the P&IDs on the control-room wall are rev C, dated eleven years ago, with three generations of red pen on top.

Over seven months the plant lost LKR 11.14 million to four separate problems. Every single one was diagnosed at the time as an equipment fault. Every single one was actually a reading fault — somebody looked at a document, understood it wrongly or found it silent, and acted. Not one instrument was broken. That is what makes this the right story to open a primer on documents.

⚠ D1 — NEUTRALISER CHARGE · “the meter drifts”

The batch charge was metered by FQIT-4501. When it failed, stores issued a plain FIT — “same thing, it's a flow transmitter.” It is not: the Q is the totaliser. With no totaliser the batch end-point was re-coded as a timed charge, 8.00 min at 750 kg/min. But flow falls as the day tank empties. Charges ran from 6,157 kg (tank high) to 5,658 kg (tank low) against a 6,000 kg target — and the caustic is dosed on the assumed 6,000. Under-charge means 6.05 % excess caustic → extra neutral-oil loss of 41.1 kg per batch. LKR 785,500/month.

⚠ D2 — DEODORISER STEAM PERMISSIVE · “the interlock didn't trip”

The low-steam-pressure permissive was drawn as a line between two bubbles. Nobody registered the three small circles on it — the ISA symbol for a software link. There is no wire. When a network switch was upgraded the link dropped for 47 minutes and the receiving controller held the last good value, so the permissive stayed TRUE while the header collapsed 2.69 bar. Three batches deodorised on failing steam and were downgraded. LKR 1,656,000 per event, twice in seven months = LKR 473,143/month.

⚠ D3 — TV-4210 · “the actuator was rebuilt properly”

The steam valve on the feed heater is specified FC — fail closed, and the P&ID says so in two characters next to the bubble. After a workshop overhaul the spring was refitted on the other side of the diaphragm and the valve became fail open. It was never bench-tested against the drawing. At the next instrument-air compressor trip the steam valve drove wide open on a stalled oil flow: 2 batches scorched, 9 hours to clean the exchanger. LKR 1,716,000, once = LKR 245,143/month.

⚠ D4 — TRANSFER PUMP P-3102 · “bad contactors”

The control narrative said, in full: “On high level, stop the transfer pump.” No deadband. No delay. No restart point. The programmer implemented exactly that against LSH-3102 at 82 %. With a ±0.35 %span ripple from the returning recirculation the pump cycled every 1.35 min — 44.6 starts per hour against a 15 kW motor rated for about 6. Three contactors, one rewind and a four-hour stoppage later, it was still “a contactor problem.” LKR 87,643/month.

Read the split again. The two most expensive faults — 79 % of the money — were caused by misreading two symbols: one letter and three small circles. The cheapest fault was caused by a sentence that was never written properly in the first place. That is the shape of this whole subject: the drawing punishes you for not knowing the grammar, and the narrative punishes you for not writing it.

💼

In a Sri Lankan plant or a Gulf EPC office, the engineer who can pick up a P&ID cold and say “this valve fails open, that link is software, this loop has no reset defined and sheet 4 is on HOLD” is doing the job of a discipline lead. It requires no calculus and no simulation software — only the grammar in the next eight pages. It is the single fastest-acquired high-status skill in process automation.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

Look at D2 again. The receiving controller held the last good value when the link died, so the permissive stayed TRUE for 47 minutes. Suppose instead the designer had specified a watchdog that forced the permissive FALSE after a timeout. The steam header has a time constant of about 18 minutes and was heading for a 2.9 bar deficit. Roughly what watchdog timeout is needed to keep the pressure deviation under 0.5 bar before the trip acts?

About 3.4 minutes. The deviation follows the usual first-order approach to a new equilibrium, Δ(t) = Δ∞(1 − e−t/τ). Setting 0.5 = 2.9(1 − e−T/18) and solving gives T = −18 ln(1 − 0.5/2.9) = 3.41 min = 204 s. That is τ/5.3 — and it generalises into the rule you should carry out of this page: a communications watchdog must be short compared with the time constant of the thing it is protecting, not compared with the network. At the actual 47 minutes the deviation reached 2.687 bar — 92.7 % of the full excursion. The link had, in effect, no watchdog at all. Option 3 is the dangerous one, and it is wrong for a reason worth naming: a dead link is not the same as a dead process, and declaring the data bad is itself a control action.

THE THREE THINGS A P&ID IS NOT CORE

Not to scale

A 200 m pipe run and a 200 mm nipple are drawn the same length. Never take a dimension, a length or an elevation off a P&ID. Distance lives on the plot plan and the isometric.

Not geometric

Left-to-right on the sheet has no relation to north, up or upstream. The only exception is the small class of items where relative elevation is functionally required — a self-draining slope, a pump NPSH leg, a seal loop — and those are annotated explicitly.

Not a wiring diagram

A signal line says two functions are connected and by what kind of medium. It says nothing about cable routes, core colours, terminal numbers, barriers or marshalling. That is the loop diagram — and confusing the two is exactly D2.

There is a fourth, subtler one worth naming because it catches experienced people: a P&ID is not a statement of the current plant unless it is stamped AS-BUILT. A drawing marked FOR CONSTRUCTION, IFC or rev C with red pen on it is a statement of intent plus somebody's memory. The Ja-Ela P&IDs were rev C from eleven years earlier. D3's valve had been replaced twice since.

💼

When you join a plant, the highest-value first week is spent answering one question: which of these six documents actually exist here, and which are current? Write the answer on one page and give it to your manager. You will have produced something nobody else has, in five days, using no tools.

GRID REFERENCES AND HOW A LINE IS TRACKED ACROSS SIX SHEETS STRETCH

Sheet borders carry letters down one edge and numbers along the other, giving cells like A-3 or D-6. An off-sheet connector reads, in full: TO PID-4300 SH 1 GRID D-6. On the receiving sheet there must be a mirror connector reading FROM PID-4200 SH 2 GRID C-1 carrying the same line number. Three checks fall out, and all three are standard drawing-review findings:

  • 1
    Orphan connector — an arrow leaves a sheet and no matching arrow arrives anywhere. Either a sheet is missing from your set or the design has a genuine hole.
  • 2
    Line-number mismatch across the break — 4"-CO-4211-A1A-H leaves and 4"-CO-4212-A1A-H arrives. Sometimes correct (a legitimate spec break, page 6); usually a drafting error.
  • 3
    Size mismatch across the break — 4" leaves, 3" arrives, with no reducer drawn. Somebody has silently throttled the line, and the hydraulic calculation is now wrong.

At Ja-Ela, the steam supply to the deodoriser crossed three sheets. The permissive of D2 crossed two. Faults hide at sheet boundaries because that is where one person's responsibility stops and another's begins. Make the boundary crossings the first thing you audit.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

You are handed sheet 2 of 6. In the drawing area a control valve carries a revision cloud with a △2 triangle. The revision block's last row is rev C. What do you conclude?

It is a discrepancy, and discrepancies are where the money is. Standard practice is that clouds and triangles show what changed at the current revision and are removed at the next issue. A △2 cloud surviving on a rev C sheet means one of three things: the sheet was re-issued without clearing old clouds (sloppy but harmless), the change was never actually made (dangerous), or the sheet was revised for something else and this cloud is genuinely stale (common). Option 3 is a real trap — numeric revisions typically denote pre-issue design revisions and letters denote issued-for-construction revisions, or the reverse depending on the project procedure, but they are never interchangeable and the procedure is stated in the title block or the project drafting standard. The correct response to any drawing discrepancy is the same: do not resolve it in your head, raise it.

The Tag — A Sentence With a Subject and Verbs

THE GRAMMAR, IN ONE PICTURE CORE

A tag such as FQIT-4501A is not a serial number. It is a compressed English sentence with a fixed word order defined by ANSI/ISA-5.1 — currently the 2024 edition, which succeeded the 2022 revision, which in turn corrected the major 2009 rewrite. Read left to right it says: “flow, totalised, indicated and transmitted, loop 4501, first of a pair.” Every letter is a separate claim about the device, and D1 cost LKR 785,500 a month because one of them was dropped.

Six letters — C, D, G, M, N, O — are explicitly left as user's choice. The parenthesised meanings above are the common industrial habits, not the standard. If a drawing uses one of those six and the legend sheet does not define it, the drawing is defective. This is the single most common legitimate reason two experienced engineers read the same tag differently.

First-letter modifiers — five, not six

D differential · F ratio · K time rate of change · M momentary · Q integrate or totalise. Applied to the first letter, they change what is measured: PDT is a differential-pressure transmitter, a completely different instrument from PT.

The classic collision

D is a first letter meaning user's choice and a modifier meaning differential. Position decides: in DT the D is the variable; in PDT it modifies P. Same for M (user's choice / momentary) and Q (quantity / totalise). FQI = flow totalised; QI = a quantity indicator.

⚠ The S trap — worth its own box

People routinely quote S as a sixth first-letter modifier meaning safety, because of PSV and PSE. It is not one. In the overwhelming majority of tags S is a succeeding letter meaning switch: LS, ZS, FS, PS. Read LSLL as level switch, low-low — never as “safety level”. The safety reading is a narrow convention confined to relief devices, where S is followed by V (valve) or E (element).

Position switches take a third letter

After a position switch, a trailing C or O is a position modifier, not Control or Orifice. ZSC = position switch, closed; ZSO = position switch, open. These are the devices that confirm a valve actually reached its seat — the confirmation demanded by rewrite 4 on page 13.

You find two tags on the same vessel: LT-3102 and LSHH-3102. A colleague says “good, the high-high trip is taken from the level transmitter.” Is he right?

The S is a switch — a separate device. LT transmits a continuous signal; LSHH is a discrete switch that changes state at a high-high level. Sharing loop number 3102 says they measure the same variable on the same equipment; it does not say they share a sensing element or a signal path. Option 3 is almost right in spirit and is the answer of a careful engineer — the loop diagram tells you the wiring — but the tag alone already settles the question of device independence. The Bubble — Shape Says What, Line Says Where

TWO INDEPENDENT STATEMENTS IN ONE SYMBOL CORE

The tag inside the bubble tells you what the function is. The bubble itself tells you two more things, and they are independent of each other and of the tag: the shape says what kind of system implements the function, and the horizontal line says where it lives and whether an operator can reach it. Four shapes × four line styles = sixteen legal combinations, and once you have the grid you never have to memorise a symbol again.

Rows — the shape

Circle: a discrete instrument — one physical device doing one job. Square with inscribed circle: shared display / shared control, i.e. a DCS or a supervisory system where many functions share one processor and one screen. Hexagon: a computer function. Diamond in a square: programmable logic control — a PLC or a safety logic solver.

Columns — the line

No line: field mounted, out on the plant. Solid line: primary location (the main control room / main panel), normally accessible to the operator. Dashed line: same location but normally inaccessible — behind the panel, inside a cabinet, on an engineering screen. Double solid line: an auxiliary or local panel, accessible.

The column matters far more than beginners expect, because it is a statement about who can change it during an event. A trip function drawn with a dashed line is deliberately out of the operator's reach; the same function with a solid line can be adjusted from the console at three in the morning. Accessibility is a safety decision recorded as a line style.

SHARED VS DISCRETE IS A STATEMENT ABOUT COMMON-CAUSE FAILURE STRETCH

Two circles mean two boxes: if one dies the other keeps working. Two square-in-circles with the same DCS behind them mean one box: a processor fault, a firmware bug or a power failure takes both. This is exactly why protection functions are drawn as a different shape from the control functions they protect — the shape difference is the independence claim.

WHAT THE HERO DRAWING IS ACTUALLY CLAIMING TIC-4210 square+circle, SOLID line -> DCS function, operator can reach it TSHH-4211 diamond+square, DASHED line -> SIS logic solver, operator CANNOT TT-4210 plain circle, NO line -> discrete field transmitter Three different shapes = three independent systems. If TSHH-4211 had been drawn as a square+circle it would be living in the same DCS as the controller it is supposed to protect - and would not be an independent protection layer at all. That is a SIL argument made entirely in geometry.

Two ISA-5.1 refinements worth carrying, because they appear on real drawings and confuse people: a bubble drawn with a square outer box but no inscribed circle is commonly used for a function without a normal operator display; and a small tag written outside and beside a bubble rather than inside it usually indicates a device that is part of a vendor package with its own numbering. Both are legend-sheet matters. When a symbol does not match this grid, the answer is on the legend sheet, not in your memory.

On a revamp drawing you find the overpressure trip PSHH-4205 drawn as a square with an inscribed circle and a solid horizontal line — the same symbol as the pressure controller PIC-4205 two centimetres away. What have you just learned, and what would you do?

The Lines — Process Piping, and the Three Circles That Cost LKR 946,000

PART ONE: THE PROCESS LINE NUMBER CORE

Every process line on a P&ID carries a compound identifier, and like the tag it is a sentence rather than a serial number. There is no single global standard for the field order — it is set by the project's piping specification — but the content is near universal, and the commonest order is size, service, sequence, pipe class, insulation.

That last rule is the useful one. A change of line number on a P&ID is a physical statement, not bookkeeping. If the number changes and you cannot see a reducer, a spec break or a service change, something is wrong. The point where the piping class changes is called a spec break and is marked explicitly — it is the boundary between, say, carbon steel 150# and stainless 300#, and it is where the pressure rating of your system actually lives. Getting a valve from the wrong side of a spec break is a classic and occasionally lethal procurement error.

Size is nominal

4" is NPS — a label, not a bore. A 4" sch 40 pipe has 102.3 mm ID; 4" sch 80 has 97.2 mm. If you are computing pressure drop you need the schedule from the piping class, which the line number points at. This is where A47 begins.

Service codes are local

CO, SM, CW, IA, N2 — crude oil, steam, cooling water, instrument air, nitrogen. Common but not standard. The legend sheet defines them, and two plants will differ.

Insulation code is a hazard flag

H heat conservation, C cold/anti-condensation, P personnel protection, T heat traced, S steam traced. P tells you the surface burns people. It is the cheapest safety information on the drawing.

PART TWO: SIGNAL LINES — AND THE ONE THAT IS NOT A WIRE CORE

Between bubbles, the line style says what kind of medium carries the signal. Six styles cover almost everything you will meet, and one of them — the sixth — is categorically different from the other five, because it is not a physical connection at all.

The first five styles all describe something you could cut with a knife. The sixth does not. A software link says: these two functions exchange data through a shared system — a bus, a network, a database, a serial link. Valves and Fail Action — Two Characters, Two Scorched Batches

FAIL ACTION IS A PROPERTY OF THE SPRING, NOT OF THE LOGIC CORE

Next to almost every control valve on a P&ID sit two characters: FC, FO, FL or FI. They answer exactly one question — what position does this valve go to when its motive power is removed? — and the answer is decided by a spring and a diaphragm, not by any controller, any PLC or any logic. D3 happened because a workshop reassembled a spring and nobody put the valve next to the drawing.

CodeMeaningMechanismChosen when
FCFail closedAir-to-open; spring closesThe valve admits something dangerous — fuel, steam, reagent. TV-4210 is FC: no air, no steam.
FOFail openAir-to-close; spring opensThe valve removes something — cooling water, vent, quench, pump recirculation.
FLFail last / lockedLock-up relay, or a self-locking actuatorEither extreme is worse than staying put — some blending and split-range duties.
FIFail indeterminateNothing guarantees a positionAlmost never a design choice; usually a piston actuator without an accumulator. Treat as a finding.

Two refinements you will meet: some drawings write FLO / FLC to mean fail-locked-then-open / closed after a lock-up relay bleeds down, and some write the action as a small arrow on the actuator stem instead of letters. And note the honest limit of the abbreviation: “fail” here means loss of the actuator's motive power — usually instrument air. It does not automatically describe what happens on loss of the electrical signal, and it certainly does not describe what happens on loss of the controller. Those are separate questions, and the next section is the reason people get them wrong.

THE INDEPENDENT TRIPLE: SPRING, SOLENOID, SIGNAL STRETCH

A trip-capable control valve has three failure inputs, and they are genuinely independent. Confusing any two of them is the single most common instrumentation misunderstanding in a commissioning meeting.

1 · The spring — air failure

Decides the position on loss of instrument air. This is what FC/FO/FL/FI describes, and nothing else. Verified on a bench by disconnecting the air.

2 · The solenoid — power failure

A trip solenoid vents the actuator on command. De-energise to trip (DTT): losing power trips the valve — fail-safe, and the norm for safety functions. Energise to trip (ETT): losing power leaves the valve alone — used where a spurious trip is itself dangerous, and it then requires line monitoring.

3 · The signal — loop failure

What the positioner does at 0 mA. Usually configurable to drive to 0 %, 100 % or hold. It may disagree with the spring. A positioner set to “hold” on an FC valve will hold it open while the drawing says fail closed — until the air itself is lost.

THE FOUR-QUESTION VALVE INTERROGATION - ask every one, every time Q1 Air lost, everything else fine -> the SPRING decides (FC/FO/FL) Q2 Power lost, air still present -> the SOLENOID decides (DTT/ETT) Q3 Signal lost (0 mA), air and power OK -> the POSITIONER decides Q4 Logic solver lost / frozen -> whatever the OUTPUT CARD does on failure - hold or drop These four can give four DIFFERENT answers on the same valve, and a P&ID only ever tells you Q1 directly. Q2, Q3 and Q4 live in the narrative and the loop diagram. If nobody has written them down, nobody knows them.

This is why D3 was not really a workshop error. The workshop reversed a spring — that is a mechanic's mistake and mechanics make them. The system failure was that there was no procedure requiring the rebuilt valve to be stroked against the P&ID before it went back on line, and no narrative recording the other three answers. A two-minute bench test against two characters on a drawing would have saved LKR 1,716,000.

THE REST OF THE VALVE SYMBOL CORE

Body type

Two opposed triangles = generic/globe. A circle in the line = ball or plug. A rectangle across the line = gate. A thin disc on a shaft = butterfly. A curved arrow = check valve, and it also carries the flow direction.

Actuator on top

A dome = diaphragm (spring-return, the FC/FO case). A rectangle = piston/cylinder. A circle with M = motor. A small solenoid box = on/off. A hand wheel = manual. Diaphragm implies a spring implies a defined fail action.

Manual valves matter

Isolation valves, bypasses and the three-valve manifold around every control valve are drawn for a reason: they define how the valve is maintained on line. A control valve with no bypass cannot be serviced without a shutdown, and that is a decision with an operating-cost consequence.

One symbol always worth hunting for: the hand switch HS and the solenoid XV/SV attached to a control valve. Their presence tells you the valve has a trip path in addition to its modulating duty — that the same physical valve is doing control and protection. That is a design pattern with a specific and heavily-argued place in A52, and spotting it on a drawing is a genuinely senior observation. A59 takes the valve itself — sizing, characteristic, stiction, noise — much further.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A cooling-water valve is marked FO. Its trip solenoid is energise-to-trip. The plant suffers a total electrical failure — power gone, instrument air compressor stopped but the air receiver still holds pressure for several minutes. Where is the valve in the first minute?

Not FO — not yet. This is the whole point of the independent triple. FO describes the air-failure case only, and the air has not failed yet; the receiver is still holding. The energise-to-trip solenoid needs power to act and there is none, so the trip path is dead too — which is exactly the weakness of ETT and why it demands line monitoring and a documented justification. That leaves the positioner, whose 0 mA behaviour is a configuration setting recorded in the narrative or the instrument datasheet, not on the P&ID. When the receiver finally bleeds down, then the spring wins and the valve opens as marked. So the honest answer to “what does this valve do in a blackout?” is a timeline, not a position — and writing that timeline down is a control-narrative job, which is where this primer goes from page 11.

Relief, Trip and the Set-Point Staircase

PROTECTION IS A LADDER, AND THE P&ID DRAWS EVERY RUNG CORE

A vessel is not protected by one device. It is protected by a graded sequence of independent layers, each with its own set point, each drawn with its own symbol, each allowed to act only after the one below it has failed. The P&ID shows you the layers; the narrative gives you the numbers. Read the two together and you can see the entire safety argument on one sheet.

THE STAIRCASE, CHECKED normal operating 8.00 barg high alarm PAH 9.20 barg +1.20 bar +15.0 % high-high trip PAHH 10.00 barg +0.80 bar +8.7 % PSV set 10.50 barg +0.50 bar +5.0 % accumulation @10 % 11.55 barg +1.05 bar +10.0 % vessel MAWP 12.00 barg +0.45 bar +3.9 % CHECK 1 operating -> PSV margin = (10.5-8.0)/8.0 = 31.3 % comfortably above the usual >=10 % rule, so the PSV will not weep during normal upsets. CHECK 2 trip (10.00) is BELOW PSV set (10.50) by 0.50 bar -> the instrumented layer acts FIRST. The relief valve is the last resort, not the working control. CHECK 3 accumulation 10.5 x 1.10 = 11.55 <= MAWP 12.00 PASS margin 0.45 bar. If this failed, the PSV is too big, too small or set too high, and the vessel is not protected.

Three rules fall straight out and they are worth memorising because they let you audit any protection scheme in ninety seconds:

  • 1
    Every set point must be strictly below the next one up, with a margin bigger than the measurement uncertainty. If PAHH is 10.00 and the transmitter is ±0.4 bar, a trip at 10.0 and a PSV at 10.2 would be indistinguishable in practice. The gaps are where the instrument error budget of B04 gets spent.
  • 2
    The alarm must leave enough time for a human to act. A PAH 1.2 bar below the trip is only useful if the process takes minutes, not seconds, to cross it. On a fast process the alarm is decoration — and pretending otherwise is how alarm systems become the flood that A53 is about.
  • 3
    Accumulation is not optional arithmetic. A relief valve does not hold the set pressure; it opens at it and the pressure keeps climbing while it discharges. The permitted accumulation (typically 10 % for a single non-fire case, more for fire) must still land below MAWP.
RELIEF DEVICE SYMBOLS AND WHAT THEY OBLIGE YOU TO CHECK CORE

PSV / PRV

Drawn as a valve body with an angled outlet and a spring bonnet. PSV conventionally denotes a full-lift device for compressible fluids (a “safety” valve); PRV a modulating relief for liquids. Usage varies by industry and by legend sheet — check, do not assume.

Rupture disc

A short curved line across the pipe. Non-reclosing: once it goes, the system is open until somebody replaces it. Often installed under a PSV to protect it from corrosion, in which case the space between them needs a pressure indicator — and if the disc leaks and the space pressurises, the disc will never burst at its rating.

Where it discharges

Follow the outlet line. To atmosphere, to a flare header, to a closed drain, to a knock-out drum? The discharge routing is a P&ID fact with enormous consequences and it is always drawn. A relief line that stops at the sheet edge with no connector is a serious finding.

On the canonical drawing, PSV-4205 sits on the steam side and is set at 10.5 barg on an 8 barg header. Notice what that tells you without reading a single other document: the steam header can credibly exceed 10.5 barg (or the PSV would not be there), the vessel it protects is rated at least that, and there is a scenario — a stuck upstream reducing valve, most likely — that somebody has already analysed. A relief device is a fossilised record of a hazard study. When you find one you do not understand, you have found a hazard you do not understand.

THE SMALL ANNOTATIONS PEOPLE SKIP CORE

Slope

SLOPE 1:100 with an arrow. It means the line must be built with a fall in that direction — for drainage, for condensate, to avoid a liquid trap in a vapour line. It is one of the very few geometric statements a P&ID is allowed to make, which is why it is written rather than drawn.

Heat tracing

ET electric trace, ST steam trace, plus the insulation code on the line number. On a coconut-oil plant this is not comfort: oil solidifies near 24 °C, so a traced line that loses its tracing becomes a solid plug, and the pump downstream deadheads.

Spec break & battery limit

A short bar across the line with the two class codes either side, or a dashed vertical labelled B/L. Marks where responsibility, rating or ownership changes. Always check what changes there — rating, material, or which contractor is liable.

Vents, drains, sample points

Small stubs with valves and often a cap or plug. They look trivial and they are how the plant is actually commissioned, flushed, purged and taken apart. A vessel with no low-point drain cannot be cleaned; a line with no high-point vent cannot be hydrotested properly.

Utility stations

Clusters of air, water, steam and nitrogen points. They tell you where maintenance can physically work, and they are the first thing missing on a drawing produced by somebody who has never operated a plant.

Vendor package boundary

A dash-dot box around a skid, labelled with the package number. Inside it, the drawing may be a black box and the internal logic belongs to the vendor. D2's chiller was inside one of these — which is exactly why the link across the boundary was a software link rather than a wire.

That last one deserves emphasis, because it is where brownfield plants leak money. A package boundary is a documentation boundary, and documentation boundaries are where narratives stop being written. The plant's control engineer writes up to the dash-dot line; the vendor writes inside it; and the interface — the handful of signals that actually cross — is written by nobody. Make the interface list its own document and you have eliminated a whole class of D2-shaped faults.

THE PROTOCOL CORE

Reading symbols is not reading a drawing. Reading a drawing means starting at a physical quantity in a pipe and following it, without skipping, all the way to a thing that moves — and being able to state, at every hop, what would happen if that hop failed. Seven steps, always the same order, and by the seventh you know more about the loop than the person who drew it remembers.

Those two questions are the whole method. The first is detectability, the second is failure propagation, and every one of the four Ja-Ela faults is a place where somebody answered neither. D2 is question 1 unanswered at hop 4. D3 is question 2 unanswered at hop 7. What a P&ID Cannot Tell You — The Gap the Narrative Fills

THE DRAWING IS A NOUN. THE NARRATIVE IS A VERB. CORE

A P&ID is a statement about what exists. It is deliberately, structurally incapable of stating what happens. This is not a shortcoming to be fixed by drawing harder — it is the reason a second document exists. Once you can name the six categories of missing information, you can audit any project in an afternoon by asking six questions.

#CategoryThe audit questionWhat goes wrong when it is missing
①Values“What number, in what units, on what range?”The programmer picks one. It is never wrong enough to notice and never right.
②Timing“How long must the condition persist? How fast may it ramp?”Nuisance trips on noise, or a trip so delayed it is useless. D4 is a missing deadband, which is timing's sibling.

Run those six questions against D4's entire narrative — “On high level, stop the transfer pump” — and five of the six come back empty. What level, on what range? No answer. Persist for how long, restart at what level? No answer. Does it apply during tank filling? No answer. Latched or automatic? No answer. May the operator override it? No answer. The sentence answers exactly one of six questions, and it was accepted as a specification. LKR 87,643 a month, and every rupee of it was written into existence by a clause that was too short.

WHY THE NARRATIVE IS THE DOCUMENT THAT GOES MISSING STRETCH

There are structural reasons, and knowing them tells you how to fight it:

Nobody's deliverable

The P&ID belongs to process engineering and is a contractual deliverable with a revision procedure. The narrative frequently belongs to whoever is writing the PLC code — often a subcontractor whose contract ends at commissioning. When they leave, the only surviving specification is the code itself.

It is written last, or after

Written properly it precedes the code and is the acceptance criterion. Written in practice it is reverse-engineered from working code six months later, if at all — at which point it documents the bugs as faithfully as the intent.

It has no drawing office

P&IDs get numbered, revised, clouded and stamped because a drafting standard exists. Narratives are Word files on somebody's laptop called narrative_final_v3_REV2.docx. Give them a document number and a revision block and half the problem disappears.

The fix is cheap

Of the LKR 268,000 that closed all four Ja-Ela faults, roughly LKR 96,000 was three weeks of an engineer writing the narrative for eleven loops and four sequences. That single document prevented D4 outright and would have caught D2's missing watchdog at review.

There is a version of this argument you can take to a plant manager who does not care about standards: the control narrative is the only document that lets you change the PLC programmer. Without it, the code is a hostage. With it, the logic is an asset the plant owns. That framing gets budget approved when “ISA-5.06.01 compliance” does not.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A narrative for a heating loop states: “If the temperature transmitter fails, the controller shall hold its last output.” The valve is FC on a steam service. What is wrong with this clause?

Hold is the wrong fail-safe here, and the FC marking is a false comfort. This is the independent-triple trap from page 7 appearing in a narrative instead of on a drawing. The spring only acts on air failure; a dead transmitter takes away the measurement while leaving air, power and controller perfectly healthy. Cause & Effect — The Narrative a Machine Can Audit

WHY THE MATRIX EXISTS CORE

Prose is a poor container for interlocks. Twenty clauses spread over four pages cannot be checked for completeness by reading, because the defects are absences — and absences are invisible in prose. A cause & effect matrix converts the same information into a grid, and a grid can be audited row by row and column by column by anyone, including a computer.

Rows are causes — initiating conditions, each with a tag, a set point, units and a delay. Columns are effects — final actions, each on a named device. A mark at an intersection means “this cause produces this effect”. That is the entire notation, and its power is that an empty row and an empty column are both immediately visible, whereas the same defects in prose are invisible.

The column boxed above — open XV-4290, the emergency dump — has no mark in any row. Somebody specified a dump valve and never said what opens it. In four pages of prose nobody would have noticed. In the grid it is a hole you can see from across the room.

PERMISSIVE, INTERLOCK, TRIP — THREE DIFFERENT THINGS STRETCH

Permissive

Blocks a start. Checked once, at the transition into running. “The agitator must be running before heating may be started.” Silent thereafter — which is exactly why clause 6 on page 13 needed a second sentence.

Interlock

Constrains a running plant. Continuously evaluated; removes a permission while the condition holds and restores it when it clears. “Charging is blocked while level exceeds 88 %.”

Trip

Forces the plant to a safe state. Continuously evaluated, normally latched, and requires a deliberate reset. Lives in the SIS, not the DCS, whenever the risk assessment demands independence.

The distinction is not academic; it determines the logic. A permissive implemented as an interlock will shut a plant down every time the condition flickers. An interlock implemented as a permissive will let a plant keep running into a condition it should have stopped for. And a trip implemented as either will not latch — so it will self-clear, the plant will restart into the same hazard, and the trend will show a sawtooth that everybody blames on the instrument. Ask which of the three you are being asked for, every time. It is a question that visibly marks you as somebody who has done this before.

From Logic Diagram to Code — ISA-5.2 and What Comes After

THE MISSING RUNG OF THE LADDER CORE

Between the cause & effect matrix and the PLC program there is one more representation: the binary logic diagram, standardised as ISA-5.2, Binary Logic Diagrams for Process Operations — a 1976 standard, reaffirmed in 1992, and still the clearest notation anybody has produced for this job. Its stated intent is deliberately hardware-neutral: to symbolise binary control functions in a way that applies to any implementation — electronic, electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic, mechanical or manual. That neutrality is the point. The diagram survives a change of PLC vendor; the ladder does not.

The basic functions

AND — all inputs true. OR — any input true. NOT — inversion. Qualified OR (“≥2 of 3”) — the voting logic every redundant trip uses, and the reason a 2oo3 architecture can tolerate one failed transmitter without either tripping or going blind.

The time functions

DT delay on initiation (the condition must persist), PD pulse duration (output lasts a fixed time regardless of input). These are rule 4 of the matrix, made into a symbol. Getting the wrong one is why a trip either chatters or never clears.

Memory

A latch with set and reset, and — critically — a stated dominance. If set and reset arrive together, which wins? For a safety trip the answer must be set dominant: the trip wins over the reset, always. This one word is the whole content of many incident reports.

THE FOUR-STEP TRANSLATION, AND WHY EACH STEP IS SEPARATE CORE
NARRATIVE -> MATRIX -> LOGIC DIAGRAM -> CODE prose grid gates+time ladder / ST / FBD step 1 narrative -> matrix You lose the reasoning and keep the relationships. What it buys: the six completeness rules of page 14. step 2 matrix -> logic diagram You add TIME and MEMORY, which a matrix cannot hold. What it buys: delays, latching, dominance and voting become explicit and reviewable BEFORE anyone codes. step 3 logic diagram -> code You add the target's dialect and the scan cycle. What it buys: nothing conceptual - and that is the point. If step 3 requires a decision, step 2 was incomplete and you should go back, not invent. THE TEST OF A GOOD SPECIFICATION Two competent programmers, working independently from the same logic diagram, produce functionally identical code. That last test is the most useful thing on this page. Every decision a programmer has to make is a decision an engineer failed to make. When you review delivered code and find behaviour nobody specified, the correct response is not to blame the coder — it is to work out which document should have contained the answer, and to put it there so the next person inherits a specification rather than an archaeology problem.

Where this goes next depends on what you are building. Continuous, modulating control from a narrative is A58. Sequential and batch logic — SFC, GRAFCET, state machines, ISA-88 phases — is A60, and it is the natural successor to this page because a batch plant like Ja-Ela is mostly sequence. The mechanics of ladder, scan order and the timer behaviour that makes a DT block work are in B03.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A high-high level trip latches and requires a manual reset. During commissioning an engineer holds the reset button pressed while the level is still above the trip point, so that he can keep the pump running. The memory block is reset dominant. What happens?

The trip is defeated, silently, for as long as somebody leans on the button. That is precisely what reset dominance means, and it is why the choice is a safety decision rather than a coding convention. For a protection function the memory must be set dominant: an active trip condition overrides any reset attempt, so the button does nothing until the level has actually fallen. Option 0 states what everyone assumes happens and is the reason this defect survives reviews — the assumption is so natural that nobody checks the block's configuration. Option 3 is a good instinct pointing at the real defence in depth: a hardwired trip path that does not pass through the latch at all cannot be held out by a button. Add “what is the dominance?” to your review checklist beside “what is the deadband?” — both are single words that decide whether logic is safe.

SEQUENCES — WRITE THEM AS STEPS, NEVER AS PROSE CORE

A sequence written as a paragraph cannot be tested, cannot be reviewed for missing transitions, and cannot be translated without invention. Written as numbered steps with four fixed fields each, it becomes a state machine that a programmer can implement and a commissioning engineer can sign off line by line.

SEQUENCE 7.2 NEUTRALISER BATCH CHARGE (the D1 sequence, rewritten) STEP 1 CHARGE OIL entry recipe loaded AND V-4201 level < 15 %span AND agitator A-4201 running > 20 rpm action open XV-4501; FQIC-4501 controls charge rate to 750 kg/min; totaliser FQI-4501 reset to zero at step entry exit FQI-4501 totalised mass >= recipe charge (6000 kg) timeout 12 min -> HOLD, alarm "charge incomplete", state the totalised mass achieved STEP 2 DOSE CAUSTIC entry step 1 complete AND FQI-4501 total within 6000 +/- 60 kg (+/-1.0 %) <-- THE D1 GUARD action dose caustic at ratio to the ACTUAL totalised mass, not the recipe target exit caustic totaliser >= computed demand timeout 6 min -> HOLD Step 2's entry condition is the whole lesson of D1 written as one line of specification. Entry conditions

What must be true before the step may begin. These are the permissives of page 14. Missing entry conditions are how a sequence charges an unstirred vessel.

Exit conditions

What ends the step. A step whose exit is only a timer is a step that does not know whether it worked — which is precisely what the timed charge became.

Timeouts and HOLD

Every step needs a timeout and a defined destination. A step with no timeout waits for ever, and a plant waiting for ever at three in the morning is a plant somebody will start bypassing.

The pattern — entry, action, exit, timeout — is the informal ancestor of the sequential function chart and of the ISA-88 phase model, both of which formalise exactly these fields. If you write sequences this way in plain text you will find the transition to SFC in A60 almost mechanical, because you will already have been writing state machines without calling them that.

OPERATOR INTERFACE AND HISTORISATION — THE CHEAP SECTIONS THAT PAY LATE STRETCH

Section 11 — what may be changed, and by whom

Three access levels are usually enough: operator (setpoints within stated limits, start/stop, acknowledge), supervisor (recipes, alarm limits within stated bounds), engineer (tuning, trip settings, bypasses — logged). Specify which parameters sit at which level. Left unspecified, integrators default to making everything operator-adjustable, and trip set points quietly become production variables.

Section 12 — what is recorded

Which tags, at what scan rate, retained how long, with what compression. Get this wrong and you cannot investigate anything. The rule: historise every PV, every OP, every SP and every trip state involved in a protection function, at a rate faster than the process time constant. For the 18-minute steam header of D2, one sample per minute is ample; for a flow loop, one per second.

There is a specific, cheap addition worth insisting on in every narrative you write: a sequence-of-events record for trips, timestamped finely enough to resolve which cause acted first. When six things trip within two seconds, a 1-second historian tells you they all happened at once and you will spend a week guessing. A 1-millisecond SOE record tells you which one was first, and the investigation is over in ten minutes. First-out detection is the difference between a root cause and a plausible story.

WE2 — SIZE A DEADBAND FROM A STARTS-PER-HOUR LIMIT CORE
A sump pump fills at a net 2.40 %span/min when running and the sump refills at 0.80 %span/min when it is off. The level measurement carries +/-0.50 %span of noise. The 7.5 kW motor permits 10 starts/hour and you want a 2x margin. Find the minimum deadband, and the resulting starts per hour.

Careful with the rates. When the pump runs, the level falls — it is a sump pump. So the emptying rate is the 2.40 figure and the filling rate is 0.80. The formula does not care which is which; it needs the two traverse rates.

target N = 10/2 = 5 starts/hour r d/(r + d) = (2.40 x 0.80)/(2.40 + 0.80) = 1.920/3.20 = 0.60000 %span/min DB = (60/N) x 0.60000 - 2a = 12 x 0.60000 - 2(0.50) = 7.200 - 1.000 = 6.200 %span Round UP to 7.0 %span -> start 82.0 %, stop 75.0 % CHECK T = (7.0 + 1.0) x (3.20/1.920) = 8.0 x 1.66667 = 13.333 min N = 60/13.333 = 4.50 starts/hour margin to the 10/h limit = 2.22x PASS

Answer: 7.0 %span, giving 4.50 starts/hour and a 2.22× margin. Sanity check the noise floor: with DB = 0 the pump would cycle at T = 1.0 × 1.66667 = 1.667 min = 36.0 starts/hour, so the noise alone accounts for a 3.6× overrun of the motor rating. If the required deadband ever comes out larger than the usable level range, the answer is not a bigger deadband — it is a filter, a stilling well, or a different tapping.

WE3 — SIZE A COMMUNICATIONS WATCHDOG CORE
A cooling-water supply temperature is sent from a packaged chiller PLC to the plant DCS over Modbus TCP and is used as a permissive. The cooling circuit has a thermal time constant of 6.5 min. On total loss of chilled water the supply temperature would ultimately rise 14.0 degC. The permissive must act before the temperature has risen 1.5 degC. What timeout?
T = -tau ln(1 - dT/dT_inf) = -6.5 ln(1 - 1.5/14.0) = -6.5 ln(1 - 0.107143) = -6.5 x (-0.113329) = 0.7366 min = 44.2 s Specify 40 s (round DOWN for a protective timeout - the opposite of the deadband rule, and for the opposite reason: here a longer value spends the safety margin). CHECK at 40 s = 0.6667 min: dT = 14.0 (1 - e^(-0.6667/6.5)) = 14.0 x 0.097565 = 1.366 degC < 1.5 degC PASS

Answer: 40 s, giving a 1.37 °C excursion against a 1.5 °C allowance. Note the rounding direction reversed between WE2 and WE3, and make sure you can say why: round a deadband UP because a bigger deadband is gentler on the plant; round a protective timeout DOWN because a shorter timeout is safer. Getting the direction wrong is a favourite examiner's trap, and it is also a real design error — the “nice round 60 s” would have given 1.99 °C and failed.

PROBLEM SET — TWELVE QUESTIONS CORE

Q1   What is the difference between LT-3102, LG-3102 and LSHH-3102?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Three separate devices measuring the same variable on the same vessel. LT transmits a continuous signal. LG is a level gauge — a sight glass, purely local, needing no power, and therefore the one an operator trusts during a blackout. LSHH is a discrete switch at a high-high level. The shared loop number says same variable, same equipment; it does not imply a shared element or signal path.

Q2   A bubble is drawn as a plain circle with a dashed horizontal line. Is that legal, and what does it mean?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Legal, and it means a discrete instrument mounted in the primary location but normally inaccessible — behind the panel, inside the cabinet. A single-loop controller or a trip amplifier mounted in the rear of a panel is exactly this. The shape and the line are independent statements, so all sixteen combinations of the page-5 grid are legal.

Q3   Why does a change of pipe schedule alone not usually generate a new line number, while a change of piping class does?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Because the schedule is a property within the piping class — the class specifies material, rating, flange type and the schedule appropriate to each size. Changing class changes the pressure/temperature envelope and the material, which is a genuine design boundary (the spec break). Schedule variation within one class is normal and carries no new information. Caveat from page 6: confirm against the project's own line-numbering rule; specifications differ.

Q4   A trip is specified with a 5 s on-delay. The PLC task in which the logic runs has a 500 ms scan. What is the worst-case actual trip time, and what else must you check?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: The condition can arise just after the input was sampled, so add up to one scan for detection and up to one more for the output: worst case ≈ 5 s + 2 × 0.5 s = 6.0 s, plus the final element's own stroke time. Also check the input filter (often 10–50 ms of hardware filtering) and, critically, that the timer is driven by a real-time clock rather than by counting scans — a scan-counting timer changes duration whenever the program grows. That last one is the varying-scan bug from B03.

Q5   Your C&E matrix has a cause row “instrument air pressure low” with effects “close XV-101” and “close XV-102”. What is wrong?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Completeness rule 6. The effect cannot be executed during the failure that demands it — you are commanding pneumatically-actuated valves to close using the air that has just been lost. Either the valves must fail closed by spring (in which case the matrix row is documenting physics, not logic, and should say so), or they need accumulators sized for the stroke. Writing the row without resolving that is how a matrix creates false confidence.

Q6   Why is “set dominant” the correct memory configuration for a safety trip, and when might reset dominance be right?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Set dominant means an active trip condition beats a simultaneous reset, so a held or stuck reset button cannot defeat a live hazard. Reset dominance is defensible only for non-safety latches where an operator must be able to force a clear — a sequence fault annunciator, say — and even then it should be logged. If you cannot state the dominance of a latch, you do not yet know what the logic does.

Q7   A pump has a net rise of 0.90 %span/min and a net fall of 2.70, with ±0.60 %span of noise. What deadband gives 6 starts/hour?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: rd/(r+d) = (0.90 × 2.70)/3.60 = 2.43/3.60 = 0.67500. Then DB = (60/6) × 0.675 − 2(0.60) = 6.750 − 1.200 = 5.550 %span → round up to 6.0 %span. Check: T = 7.2 × 3.60/2.43 = 10.667 min → 5.63 starts/h. ✓ Note the slow rate dominates: tripling the fall rate from 0.90 to 2.70 changed the harmonic term by far less than you would guess.

Q8   A vessel operates at 4.0 barg, MAWP 8.0 barg, PSV set 7.5 barg, accumulation 10 %. Does it pass, and if not what is the maximum legal set pressure?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: 7.5 × 1.10 = 8.25 barg > 8.0 — FAIL by 0.25 bar. Maximum legal set pressure is 8.0/1.10 = 7.272 barg, so specify 7.2 barg (8.00 − 7.92 = 0.08 bar in hand). The trip must then sit below 7.2 with a margin exceeding the transmitter uncertainty — around 6.7 barg for a ±0.25 bar instrument.

Q9   What is the practical difference between a permissive and an interlock, expressed as logic?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: A permissive is evaluated only at the transition into a running state — edge-triggered on the start command — and is not re-checked afterwards. An interlock is evaluated continuously and removes the run permission whenever it is false. Implementing a permissive as an interlock shuts the plant down whenever the condition flickers; implementing an interlock as a permissive lets the plant keep running into a condition it should stop for. Many real requirements need both — page 13's clause 6 is exactly that case.

Q10   A signal crosses a network with a 6.5 min process time constant and a 14.0 °C ultimate excursion. You are told “use 60 s, it is a nice round number.” What excursion does that allow?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Δ = 14.0(1 − e−1.0/6.5) = 14.0 × 0.142372 = 1.993 °C — which fails a 1.5 °C allowance by a third. This is WE3 with the rounding done the lazy way. A protective timeout is a calculated quantity, and “a nice round number” is not a design method.

Q11   Why is a hexagon bubble a different engineering claim from a square-with-inscribed-circle, beyond the drafting convention?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: Because it names a different availability and integrity class. A shared display/shared control function lives in the DCS — engineered for continuous control, with redundancy and a deterministic scan. A computer function lives on a server: an advanced-control package, an optimiser, a historian-driven calculation. It may be single, non-redundant, patched on Tuesdays, and may stop without the plant noticing. Control that must not stop should never be drawn as a hexagon, and finding a hexagon in a control path is a genuine review finding.

Q12   You inherit a plant with current P&IDs and no control narrative at all. You have three weeks. What do you write first?

Tap to reveal answer ↓A: The cause & effect matrix, then section 10, failure and recovery. Reasons: the C&E is extractable from the existing logic and the P&IDs in days, it is the highest-consequence content, and its six completeness rules generate a defect list you can hand to management as evidence. Section 10 next because it is the section that is never recoverable from code — the code shows what happens on a good day, not what was intended on a bad one. Regulatory-control detail (section 6) is valuable but largely reconstructable later from the controllers themselves. Write what is unrecoverable first.

THE FOUR DERIVATIONS, IN FULL STRETCH
D-1 DEADBAND FROM A PERMITTED START RATE A two-state device cycles about a switching band. Let the controlled variable rise at r and fall at d (both positive, in %span per minute), let the deadband be DB and let the measurement noise have half-band a. The variable must clear the noise at BOTH ends before the comparator changes state, so the effective travel each way is (DB + 2a), not DB. One full cycle is therefore T = (DB + 2a)/r + (DB + 2a)/d = (DB + 2a) (1/r + 1/d) = (DB + 2a) (r + d)/(r d) ... (1) Starts per hour N = 60/T. Inverting (1): DB = (60/N) x r d/(r + d) - 2a ... (2) Two properties worth naming: * the group r d/(r + d) is half the harmonic mean of r and d, so the SLOWER rate dominates. Doubling the faster rate barely moves the answer. * 2a is subtracted independently of DB, so there is a noise floor: if 2a alone already gives N above the limit, no deadband helps and you must filter or move the tapping. Ja-Ela check: r 1.15, d 0.95, a 0.35 r d/(r+d) = 1.0925/2.10 = 0.52024 DB = 0 -> T = 0.70 x 2.10/1.0925 = 1.3455 min -> N = 44.6 N <= 4/h -> DB >= 15 x 0.52024 - 0.70 = 7.104 %span specify 8.0 -> T = 8.70 x 1.92220 = 16.723 min -> N = 3.59 D-2 PROTECTIVE TIMEOUT FROM A PROCESS TIME CONSTANT When a signal path dies and the receiver holds the last value, the true variable relaxes toward a new equilibrium. For a first-order process D(t) = D_inf (1 - e^(-t/tau)) ... (3) Require |D(T)| <= D_max. Solving (3) for T: T = -tau ln(1 - D_max/D_inf) ... (4) Note (4) needs D_inf, the ULTIMATE excursion, not the current rate. Sizing from an observed rate of change under-estimates T early (the process has not yet slowed) and over-estimates it late. D_inf comes from a process calculation - what the variable would reach with the control action absent. D2 check: tau 18 min, D_inf 2.9 bar, D_max 0.5 bar T = -18 ln(1 - 0.172414) = -18 x (-0.189242) = 3.406 min = 204 s. Actual hold was 47 min, giving D(47) = 2.9(1 - e^(-2.6111)) = 2.687 bar = 92.7 % of D_inf. Rounding: round a DEADBAND up (gentler on the plant), round a TIMEOUT down (shorter is safer). D-3 THE SET-POINT STAIRCASE AS A SET OF INEQUALITIES Let u = the measurement uncertainty (one-sided, in process units). For a pressure system the staircase must satisfy, in order: P_op < P_AH - u alarm clears normal operation P_AH < P_AHH - 2u alarm distinguishable from trip P_AHH < P_set - u the INSTRUMENTED layer acts first P_set x (1 + A) <= MAWP accumulation fits in the design where A is the permitted accumulation (0.10 for a single non-fire relieving case). The last inequality is the binding one and should be solved FIRST, because it caps P_set: P_set <= MAWP/(1 + A) ... (5) WE4 check: MAWP 10.0, A 0.10 -> P_set <= 9.091 -> specify 9.0 then P_AHH <= 9.0 - 0.25 = 8.75 -> specify 8.5 (2u below) then P_AH <= 8.5 - 0.50 = 8.00 -> 7.5 leaves 1.0 bar of operator time. Original proposal had P_AHH 9.6 > P_set 9.4, i.e. the relief valve was the working controller. D-4 MATRIX COMPLETENESS AS A COUNTING ARGUMENT A cause & effect matrix of C causes and E effects has C x E cells. Six audits apply, and only two of them look at the marks themselves: rule 1 every effect column non-empty E checks rule 2 every cause row non-empty C checks rule 3 reset stated per cause C checks rule 4 delay stated per cause C checks rule 5 bypass rule stated per cause C checks rule 6 fail-safe state stated per effect E checks Total checks = 4C + 2E, which is LINEAR in the matrix size while the cell count C x E is QUADRATIC. That gap is the whole reason the matrix form is auditable: the deodoriser matrix has 7 causes and 8 effects, so cells = 7 x 8 = 56 checks = 4(7) + 2(8) = 44 and every one of those 44 is a yes/no question answerable by one person in seconds. Double the plant to 14 x 16 and the cells go to 224 (x4) while the checks go to 88 (x2). The audit stays tractable exactly when the drawing stops being readable - which is when you need it. Ja-Ela first pass: 27 marks, 15 defects, 55.6 % of marks carrying at least one hole. Rule 4 (delay) found five; rule 5 (bypass) found four; rule 3 (reset) found three. Rule 2 (dead causes) was the only rule that passed - because a dead cause is the one defect prose DOES display.

1Treating FC as “what the valve does in any failure”FC/FO describe loss of instrument air only. Power, signal and logic-solver failures are three separate questions with three separate answers (page 7, WE5).

A note on editions. ISA-5.1 moved from 2009 to 2022 to 2024, and the 2022 revision was explicitly intended to begin a regular maintenance cycle for the standard. Nothing in this primer's grammar changed between those editions — the letter tables and the bubble grid are stable — but if you are writing a specification, cite the edition your project has adopted rather than the newest. And in every case the project legend sheet overrides the standard for user's-choice letters and service codes.
