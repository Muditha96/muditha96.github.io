Page 1 · Foundations

Why Compressed Air? The Trade-Offs

Precise Definition

Pneumatics is the use of compressed air (a stored pressure–volume potential) to transmit and control power — converting the energy of a central compressor into linear or rotary mechanical work at the actuator.

Air is chosen not because it is the most efficient medium — it is one of the least efficient — but because it is clean, fast, safe and forgiving. To use it well, you must respect the one property that defines it: compressibility. Unlike hydraulic oil, air is a spring. That spring buys you cushioning and overload tolerance, but costs you precise position control.

Strengths

Cheap actuators, very fast cycling, no fire/shock risk, tolerant of overload (just stalls), exhaust simply vents to atmosphere, clean for food/pharma.

Weaknesses

Springy → poor mid-stroke positioning, low energy efficiency (~10–20% wire-to-work), noisy exhaust, limited force vs hydraulics, leaks waste money silently.

Energy density: hydraulics ≈ 200–350 bar · pneumatics ≈ 6–10 bar → a hydraulic cylinder of the same bore makes ~30–50× the force. Pneumatics wins on cost, speed and simplicity, not raw force.
Where this sits in the plant

Pneumatics is the default for discrete, repetitive, point-to-point motion: clamp, push, eject, grip, index. The moment you need smooth, programmable, high-force motion, you move to servos or hydraulics.

Page 2 · System Architecture

The Complete Circuit — Generation to Work

Micro 12 introduced five blocks. The real system has more — and the order matters because each stage protects the next. Energy flows left to right; air quality is won or lost at each step.

A real layout also has isolation valves, a soft-start/dump valve for safe pressurisation, and a silencer on every exhaust port.

Soft-start / dump

Pressurises slowly on power-up (no cylinder slam) and vents the whole circuit on E-stop — a safety must.

Silencers

Fitted to exhaust ports. Exhaust noise is a major source of plant dB; sintered bronze silencers tame it.

Receiver sizing

Smooths compressor cycling and supplies peak demand. Rule: ~6–10 L of tank per CFM of compressor output.

Page 3 · The Physics

Gas Laws — Why Air Is a Spring

Everything odd about pneumatics traces to one fact: gas volume changes with pressure and temperature. The card said air is "compressible" — here is the maths that governs it.

Ideal gas: P·V = n·R·T (the master relation) Boyle (T fixed): P₁·V₁ = P₂·V₂ → compress to ½ volume ⇒ double pressure Gay-Lussac (V): P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ → heat a sealed tank ⇒ pressure rises Combined: (P₁·V₁)/T₁ = (P₂·V₂)/T₂

Two consequences shape daily engineering:

GAUGE vs ABSOLUTE

Always know which you mean

Gauge pressure (barg) is relative to atmosphere; absolute (bara) adds ~1.013 bar. Gas-law maths needs absolute. "6 bar" line pressure is 6 barg = ~7 bara.

FREE AIR — FAD

The hidden 7×

Air at 6 barg occupies ~1/7 of its atmospheric volume. So 1 L of cylinder at 6 bar consumes ~7 L of Free Air Delivery. This factor drives every compressor and energy bill.

Air consumed per stroke (Free Air, normal litres): V_FAD = A·s × (P_abs / P_atm) e.g. 0.00503 m²(80mm) × 0.1 m × 7 ≈ 3.5 NL per extend stroke Heat of compression: compressing air heats it (PV work → temperature); that is why a dryer follows the compressor — cooling air drops moisture out.
The "spring" in numbers

Because P·V is constant at fixed temperature, a trapped column of air behaves like a non-linear spring whose stiffness rises with pressure. This is exactly why a mid-stroke pneumatic cylinder bounces under varying load and cannot hold an arbitrary position the way a servo can.

Page 4 · The Valve Language

Reading ISO 1219 Valve Symbols

A directional control valve (DCV) is described by ports × positions — e.g. a 5/2 valve has 5 ports and 2 positions. The symbol is read like a comic strip: each box is one position; arrows show the flow path that connects the ports in that state.

Read it as: "in the left box, supply 1 feeds output 2 while output 4 exhausts; energise the solenoid and the boxes swap."

NotationPorts / PositionsTypical use
3/23 ports, 2 positionsSingle-acting cylinder, vacuum on/off, pilot signal
5/25 ports, 2 positionsDouble-acting cylinder — the workhorse
5/35 ports, 3 positionsMid-position stop: closed-centre, exhaust-centre, or pressure-centre
Detentdouble-solenoid, no springMemory valve — holds last state on power loss
Actuation matters as much as ports

Single-solenoid + spring return = fail-safe to a known state. Double-solenoid (detent) = fail-in-place. A 5/3 exhaust-centre valve drops a cylinder limp on E-stop; closed-centre traps it (springy hold); pressure-centre pushes both ends. Choosing the centre condition is a safety decision, not a convenience.

Page 5 · The Actuator

Cylinders — Force, Area & the Rod Penalty

Micro 12 gave you F = P × A. The deep-dive truth: a double-acting cylinder has two different areas, because the rod steals piston area on the retract side. Extend is strong; retract is weaker.

Same pressure, two forces: extend uses the full piston; retract loses the rod's cross-section.

A_bore = π/4 · D² (extend force area) A_ann = π/4 · (D² − d²) (retract force area, D=bore, d=rod) F_extend = P · A_bore F_retract = P · A_ann Worked: D=80mm, d=25mm, P=6 bar = 6×10⁵ Pa A_bore = 0.7854·0.080² = 5.03e-3 m² → F_ext ≈ 3017 N A_ann = 0.7854·(0.080²−0.025²) = 4.54e-3 m² → F_ret ≈ 2722 N

Single-acting

Air one way, spring returns. Simple, fewer ports — but spring eats ~10–20% of force.

Double-acting

Air both ways, full force each direction. The standard for clamping & pushing.

Rodless / guided

Long strokes in tight space (rodless) or anti-rotation precise motion (guided / dual-rod).

The load-ratio rule

Never size a cylinder to its theoretical force. Apply a load ratio: aim for the static load to be ≤ 70% of theoretical force for slow moves, and ≤ 50% for fast dynamic moves — the margin covers friction, back-pressure and acceleration. Oversize bore, then throttle speed.

Page 6 · Motion Control

Speed Control — Meter-Out, Not Meter-In

You set cylinder force with pressure, but you set speed by throttling airflow with a flow-control valve (a needle valve + bypass check). There is a right way and a jerky way — this is the single most common pneumatic skill gap.

METER-OUT ✓

Throttle the exhaust

Restrict air leaving the cylinder. The trapped exhaust air forms a back-pressure cushion → smooth, controlled, load-stable motion. Use this for almost everything.

METER-IN ✗

Throttle the supply

Restrict air entering. With nothing resisting on the exhaust side, the piston lurches and "stick-slips," especially on vertical or varying loads. Reserve for single-acting / low-friction cases.

The back-pressure built behind the moving piston is the cushion that makes meter-out smooth.

End cushioning

Adjustable cushions decelerate the piston in the last few mm to stop slam, hammering and seal damage.

Quick-exhaust valve

Dumps exhaust right at the port for maximum speed when you need a fast stroke (e.g. ejection).

Stroke time

Set by orifice flow + volume + load — not just pressure. Sizing valve & tubing bore caps your speed.

Page 7 · Sizing the Flow Path

Flow Coefficients — Cv, Kv and ISO 6358

A valve and tubing that are too small starve the cylinder — it moves slowly, weakly, or never reaches end. Flow capacity is rated by a flow coefficient; matching it to demand is the core sizing calculation.

CoefficientWhat it isNotes
CvUS flow factor (gallons/min of water at 1 psi drop)Dominant on data sheets; quick comparisons
KvMetric equivalent (m³/h of water)Kv ≈ 0.86 × Cv
C & bISO 6358 sonic conductance C + critical ratio bThe rigorous compressible-flow model
QnNominal flow (NL/min) at a stated ΔpMost intuitive for air; read at e.g. 6→5 bar
ISO 6358 air flow: Choked (Δp large, p₂/p₁ ≤ b): Q = C · p₁ · √(293/T) Subsonic (p₂/p₁ > b): Q = C·p₁·√(293/T) · √(1 − ((p₂/p₁ − b)/(1 − b))²) where C = sonic conductance, b = critical pressure ratio (~0.3–0.5)

Practical sizing recipe (no calculator gymnastics):

  • 1
    Find air volume per stroke in free air (bore area × stroke × pressure ratio).
  • 2
    Divide by required stroke time → demand in NL/min.
  • 3
    Pick a valve whose Qn meets demand at your working Δp, then size tubing one step up so the hose isn't the bottleneck.
  • 4
    Re-check: long tubing runs add pressure drop; account for fittings and the silencer.
The hidden bottleneck

Engineers oversize the valve then choke the system with 4 mm tubing and tiny push-in fittings. Flow scales with the fourth power of bore in laminar terms — a small tubing upgrade can transform a sluggish cylinder. Always size the whole path, port to port.

Page 8 · Air Quality

The FRL — Filter, Regulator, Lubricator

The "FRL" box in Micro 12 is three jobs in one. Dirty, wet, unregulated air is the number-one cause of pneumatic failure, so this stage earns its place right before every machine.

Order is fixed: filter first (clean), regulate second (control), lubricate last (add only if the actuator needs it).

Filter

Removes water, particulate and oil aerosol. Grades 40 µm (general) down to 0.01 µm coalescing (instrument/food).

Regulator

Drops variable line pressure to a stable working set-point. Lower pressure = less air wasted; never run higher than the job needs.

Lubricator

Adds controlled oil mist for older actuators. Modern sensors and "non-lube" cylinders prefer no oil — check first.

Dew point: the spec nobody checks

A refrigerant dryer typically gives a +3 °C pressure dew point — fine indoors, but air run outdoors in winter will freeze and block valves. For cold lines or instrument air, a desiccant dryer reaching −40 °C dew point is required. Match dew point to the coldest point in the pipe run.

Page 9 · Beyond Cylinders

Vacuum, Grippers & the Energy Bill

Pneumatics isn't only push and pull. Two areas decide whether a system is modern and economical: vacuum handling, and the relentless cost of leaks.

VACUUM

Pick-and-place by suction

A venturi ejector blows compressed air through a nozzle to generate vacuum at a suction cup — no separate vacuum pump. Used everywhere in packaging, electronics and robot end-effectors. Add a vacuum switch to confirm grip.

ROTARY & GRIPPERS

More than linear motion

Rotary actuators (rack-and-pinion / vane) index and turn. Angular & parallel grippers clamp parts. These feed directly into end-effector design for automation cells.

The cost of compressed air — the number that surprises managers
~10%

Typical wire-to-work efficiency. ~90% of input electricity becomes heat, not motion.

€1k+

Annual cost of a single 3 mm leak at 6 bar running continuously — and plants have dozens.

−7%

Energy saved per 1 bar you drop the system pressure. Lowering set-point is free money.

Leak cost ≈ leak flow (NL/min) × hours × €/kWh × specific energy A 3 mm orifice at 6 bar leaks ~~300 NL/min ≈ several kW of wasted compressor. Fix order: find & fix leaks → lower pressure → recover heat → right-size compressor.
Where this connects forward — Industry 4.0

Smart flow meters and pressure sensors now feed OPC-UA/IIoT dashboards that map air consumption per machine and flag leaks automatically. Compressed-air monitoring is one of the highest-ROI digital twin use-cases on the factory floor — the link that ties pneumatics to Industry 4.0 (A31, coming soon).

Page 10 · Engineering Practice

Interactive Sizer, Failures & Quick Check

Try it — Cylinder force & air-consumption sizer

Drag bore, pressure and stroke. Watch extend force and the free-air consumed per stroke — the two numbers you actually quote.

Extend force 3017 N (≈ 308 kg) · consumes ~3.5 NL free air / stroke
What actually goes wrong on the floor
⚠️ Cylinder slow / weak

Undersized valve or tubing starving flow, or regulator set too low. Check Qn vs demand and the smallest bore in the path.

⚠️ Jerky, stick-slip motion

Metered-in instead of metered-out, or no end cushioning. Move the flow control to the exhaust side.

⚠️ Water in the lines / frozen valves

Dryer dew point too high for ambient, or filter bowl full. Drain, check dew point vs coldest pipe temp.

⚠️ Air bill climbing

Leaks — the silent tax. Ultrasonic leak survey, fix joints, then drop system pressure 1 bar.

Real Industrial Example — full circle to Micro 12

On a case-packer running 60 cycles/min (the very machine from Micro 12), twelve 5/2 single-solenoid valves drive double-acting cylinders to push, lift, clamp and seal. Each is metered-out for smooth motion with end cushioning to survive the cycle count; a venturi gripper lifts the flat cardboard blank; an FRL with a +3 °C dryer keeps valves dry; and a flow meter on OPC-UA flags the day a worn seal starts leaking — before it stops the line. Quick Check

A double-acting cylinder has an 80 mm bore, 25 mm rod, runs at 6 bar, stroke 200 mm. (a) Why is its retract force less than its extend force, and by roughly how much? (b) The machine cycles 30 times a minute — estimate the free-air consumption, and name the cheapest way to cut that air bill.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
(a) Retract uses the annulus area (bore minus rod), so it loses the rod's cross-section. A_bore = 5.03e-3 m², A_ann = π/4·(0.08²−0.025²) = 4.54e-3 m² → about 10% less force on retract (≈ 2722 N vs 3017 N). (b) Per full cycle ≈ (extend + retract volume) × pressure ratio. Extend ≈ 5.03e-3×0.2 = 1.0 L, retract ≈ 0.9 L; total ≈ 1.9 L × ~7 ≈ 13.3 NL/cycle → ×30 ≈ ~400 NL/min. Cheapest cut: fix leaks, then lower the regulator set-point to the minimum the job needs — every 1 bar dropped saves ~7% energy.
