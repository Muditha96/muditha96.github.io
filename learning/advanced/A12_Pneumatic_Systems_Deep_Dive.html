💨Pneumatic Systems

Compressed air is the only utility a factory manufactures for itself — and it manufactures it badly. Roughly 88 % of the electricity that enters a compressor leaves as low-grade heat before a single cylinder moves. What remains is governed by three things almost nobody measures: a conductance, a dead volume, and an absolute pressure. This module derives all three in closed form — including the law that says every unregulated leak in your plant gets bigger the moment you raise the setpoint to fix a slow cylinder — and then walks one Sri Lankan packing plant from LKR 1.2 million a month of invisible loss to a twenty-five-day payback.

The Plant That Raised Its Pressure to Fix One Slow Cylinder — and Paid for It Every Second After

COLD OPEN — EKALA INDUSTRIAL ZONE, SACHET PACKING PLANT CORE

Three vertical form-fill-seal lines and one cartoner, sixteen hours a day, twenty-six days a month — 416 running hours. The compressor room holds two 55 kW oil-flooded screw machines on load/unload control, set to 7.5 bar(g). A clamp-on power logger on the incomer says the room averages 64.0 kW. A thermal mass flow meter clamped on the main after the receiver says the plant consumes 10.00 m³/min of free air.

Those two numbers are the entire diagnosis, and almost no plant in Sri Lanka has either of them. Divide one by the other and you get the plant's specific power: 6.40 kW per m³/min. At LKR 45 per kWh, the room bills

64.0 kW × 416 h/month × LKR 45/kWh = LKR 1,198,080 per month That is the plant's air bill. Nobody in the building has ever seen it, because it arrives inside the electricity bill with no line item.

A four-day survey — an ultrasonic leak detector, a pressure logger at three points, and a high-speed camera on one cylinder — found that 55.7 % of that air never does any work at all, and that the single decision most responsible was taken by a well-meaning maintenance engineer in 2021 to solve a problem that had nothing to do with pressure.

FAULT 1 — THE PLANT IS RUNNING A PERMANENTLY OPEN 6 MM HOLE CORE

The classic measurement needs no instrument at all. On the Sunday shutdown, with every machine isolated but the ring main still pressurised, one compressor was left running and its load and unload periods were timed with a phone. Over ten complete cycles the machine loaded for 42 s and unloaded for 78 s.

Leak flow = FAD × t_load / (t_load + t_unload) FAD (55 kW screw at 7.5 bar) = 8.60 m³/min duty = 42 / (42 + 78) = 0.350 Q_leak = 8.60 × 0.350 = 3.01 m³/min = 30.1 % of plant demand

Thirty per cent is not unusual. It is the world average for a plant that has never run a leak programme. What makes it concrete is turning it back into a hole. The ultrasonic survey found 26 individual leaks — eleven push-in fittings, six cracked polyurethane tubes at cable-chain flex points, four leaking cylinder rod seals, three failed valve exhaust silencers, one filter bowl O-ring and one condensate drain stuck part-open. Not one of them is exotic. Together they are a 6 mm hole that nobody would tolerate for a second if they could see it.

FAULT 2 — SIX OPEN TUBES BLOWING AT THE CEILING CORE

Six stations use compressed air to clear cut-offs, dry a seal line, and eject a reject sachet. Every one of them is a length of 2.5 mm bore copper tube pushed into a bulkhead and pointed at the work. None has a solenoid valve; none has its own regulator; all six run continuously, sixteen hours a day, because “switching them makes the operator's job harder”.

Open tube end, effective area S = 0.85 × (π/4) × 2.5² = 4.17 mm² each nozzle Q = 12 × 4.17 × 8.513 = 426 Nl/min six nozzles Q = 2.56 m³/min = 25.6 % of plant demand Cost 2.56 × 6.40 = 16.38 kW → LKR 306,708 per month

Two things make this worse than it looks. First, an open tube is a terrible way to move air: the jet is all momentum and no entrainment, so most of the thrust the operator wants is being paid for at the compressor rather than being borrowed from the surrounding room air. An engineered amplifier nozzle entrains roughly five parts of free room air per part of compressed air and delivers the same force for about a third of the flow. Second, six nozzles running continuously is not a duty cycle at all — the sachets they blow at are present for about 22 % of the time. Page 13 puts numbers on both.

FAULT 3 — ONE SLOW CYLINDER, AND THE DECISION IT CAUSED CORE

The cartoner's carton-forming clamp is an ISO 15552 cylinder, 63 mm bore, 100 mm stroke. The machine's cycle allows it 0.25 s to extend. A high-speed camera measured 0.62 s. The station therefore gates the entire cartoner, and the line has been run 4 % below its rated speed since 2021 to keep it inside the envelope.

In 2021 a maintenance engineer did the obvious thing: he raised the compressor setpoint from 6.5 to 7.5 bar(g). It worked, in the sense that the extend time fell from about 0.62 s to about 0.52 s — a real 17 % improvement. It also did not solve the problem, because 0.52 s is still twice the budget. So the 4 % speed workaround stayed, and the extra bar stayed too, and has been paid for every second since.

What Pneumatics Actually Is — a Utility You Manufacture, Badly, on Purpose

THE DEFINITION THAT MATTERS CORE

Pneumatics is the transmission of power by a compressible fluid — almost always atmospheric air, taken free, compressed at electrical cost, cleaned, distributed, throttled, expanded through an actuator, and then thrown away. That last clause is the whole subject. Hydraulics returns its fluid to a tank; electricity returns its current through a neutral. Pneumatics is the only common power transmission in a factory that is fundamentally open-loop in its working medium, and every economic and engineering consequence flows from that.

Two properties separate air from oil and from electrons, and you will meet both on every page that follows:

  • 1Air is compressible. It stores energy as it is pressurised, which makes every pneumatic actuator a spring in series with a load. That spring is why pneumatics is forgiving of shock and safe against jamming, and it is also why a pneumatic axis cannot hold an accurate mid-stroke position, why it overshoots, and why it “bounces” when you try to control it like a servo (A09).
  • 2Air is thrown away. The energy in the exhaust — typically a third or more of what the actuator received — is vented to atmosphere through a silencer. Nothing recovers it in ordinary industrial practice. This makes compressed air roughly seven to nine times more expensive per unit of delivered work than the electricity that made it.

SO WHY DOES ANYBODY USE IT? CORE

Because efficiency is not the only currency. Pneumatics survives — and in packaging, textiles, food and electronics assembly it dominates — for six reasons that no amount of energy analysis erases:

Power density

A 32 mm cylinder gives 480 N from a 250 g device. No electric actuator of that mass and cost comes close.

Overload immunity

Stall a cylinder and it simply sits there at full pressure. Stall a servo and you trip a drive, or burn a motor.

Intrinsic safety

No ignition source. Flour mills, solvent lines, paint booths and ATEX zones are pneumatic for this reason alone.

Compliance

The air spring makes grippers and press pads forgiving of tolerance variation — a genuine engineering property, not a defect.

Speed & simplicity

Two-position work at 1–3 m/s with a fitter, a spanner and no commissioning software.

Washdown & hygiene

Stainless cylinders tolerate caustic CIP cycles that would destroy a servo's seals and encoder.

The honest engineering rule that comes out of this: use pneumatics for two-position work at high force and modest duty cycle; use electric for anything that needs a mid-stroke position, a controlled velocity profile, or runs continuously. The economics turn on duty cycle, because the pneumatic penalty is per-cycle and the electric penalty is per-purchase.

SIX HONEST LIMITS OF PNEUMATICS CORE
  • 1You cannot hold an accurate mid-stroke position without adding a servo-pneumatic valve, a position sensor and a controller — at which point an electric axis is usually cheaper and always better.
  • 2You cannot control velocity, only limit it. A throttle sets a maximum flow; the actual speed still depends on load, temperature and supply pressure. Two identical stations will not run at identical speeds.
  • 3Stiffness collapses at low pressure. Air-spring stiffness is proportional to absolute pressure and inversely proportional to trapped volume — a nearly-retracted cylinder is far stiffer than a mid-stroke one (page 3).
  • 4Noise. An unsilenced 5/2 exhaust is 95–105 dB(A) at one metre. Silencers are not an accessory; they are part of the design, and a blocked one is a hidden speed limit.
  • 5Water is inevitable. Compressing air to 7 bar concentrates its moisture eightfold; something must remove it or your plant will (page 4).
  • 6The energy cost is invisible. It arrives inside the electricity bill, attached to no machine and no cost centre. Everything on page 13 exists to make it visible.

A plant runs one 55 kW compressor. Which of these delivers the largest recoverable energy, in kilowatts, at the lowest capital cost?
C — the heat, by an enormous margin. This is the most counter-intuitive result in industrial compressed air and it is worth internalising. About 72 % of a 55 kW compressor's input — roughly 40 kW — leaves as warm air off the oil cooler and aftercooler, at 30–45 °C above ambient, continuously, whenever the machine is loaded. In a dairy, laundry, garment-washing plant or anywhere with a hot-water demand, C is transformative and pays back in months; in an air-conditioned electronics plant it is worth less than zero, because you are now paying to remove it. The right answer to an energy question is always conditional on what the site can absorb.

Air as a Thermodynamic Substance — Where the Money and the Springiness Come From

THE ONE EQUATION EVERYTHING RESTS ON CORE

Dry air at industrial pressures and temperatures is, to better than half a per cent, an ideal gas. Everything in this module — leak flow, cylinder force, stroke time, vacuum evacuation, condensate, energy cost — is a consequence of one equation and its derivatives.

p V = m R T R (air) = 287.1 J/(kg·K) p absolute pressure [Pa] m mass [kg] V volume [m³] T absolute temperature [K]

Two disciplines will save you more grief than any formula on this page. First, always work in absolute pressure. Gauge pressure is a convenience for gauges; every physical law here uses absolute, and confusing the two is the single most common error in pneumatic calculation. In Sri Lanka at sea level, add 1.013 bar; at Nuwara Eliya (1,868 m) add only 0.81 bar — which is why a compressor rated for the coast delivers about 20 % less mass flow in the hills. Second, always work in absolute temperature.

Boyle, 1662

pV = const at fixed T. Halve the volume, double the absolute pressure.

Charles / Gay-Lussac

V/T and p/T constant. A receiver in the afternoon sun is not at the pressure you set at 06:00.

Dalton

Partial pressures add. This is the whole of page 4 — water vapour does not care about the air around it.

THE WORK OF COMPRESSION — THREE ANSWERS, ALL CORRECT CORE

How much energy does it take to compress one cubic metre of free air to 7.5 bar(g)? The answer depends entirely on how much of the heat of compression you allow to escape during the compression.

Isothermal (all heat removed instantly — the thermodynamic minimum) W = p₁ V₁ ln(p₂/p₁) Adiabatic / isentropic (no heat removed — the practical single-stage limit) W = (γ/(γ−1)) · p₁ V₁ · [ (p₂/p₁)^((γ−1)/γ) − 1 ] γ = 1.4 Polytropic (the real machine, with intercooling and oil injection) W = (n/(n−1)) · p₁ V₁ · [ (p₂/p₁)^((n−1)/n) − 1 ] 1.0 < n < 1.4

Put the plant's numbers in. One cubic metre of free air at 1.013 bar, compressed to 8.513 bar absolute:

Isothermal W = 101300 × ln(8.4038) = 101300 × 2.1287 = 215.6 kJ Adiabatic W = 3.5 × 101300 × 0.83700 = 296.8 kJ Actual (measured at the plant) = 384.0 kJ per m³/min: isothermal 3.59 kW adiabatic 4.95 kW actual 6.40 kW isentropic efficiency = 4.95 / 6.40 = 77.3 % (a normal, healthy screw) second-law efficiency = 3.59 / 6.40 = 56.1 % (the honest number)

The gap between isothermal and adiabatic is why every serious compressor is cooled, and why machines above about 8 bar go two-stage with an intercooler: two 2.9:1 stages with cooling between them approach the isothermal ideal far better than one 8.4:1 stage. It is also why the discharge is hot. For a dry single stage:

T₂ = T₁ · (p₂/p₁)^((γ−1)/γ) = 305 K × 1.837 = 560 K = 287 °C (an oil-free stage would glow) Oil-flooded screws inject oil into the compression, absorbing that heat as it forms and pulling n down toward ~1.1; real discharge is 80–100 °C. That injected oil is also why you have an oil separator, and why some of it always gets past (page 4).
THE PER-BAR LAW — AND WHY IT IS WORSE THAN THE RULE OF THUMB CORE

Everyone in industry quotes “about 7 % per bar”. It is a good rule, but it is a local derivative, and its value depends on where you are standing. Differentiate the adiabatic work with respect to discharge pressure:

Let f(p) = (p/p₁)^κ − 1 κ = (γ−1)/γ = 0.2857 relative sensitivity (1/W)(dW/dp) = κ (p/p₁)^(κ−1) / [ p₁ f(p) ] at 7.5 bar(g) → 7.4 % per bar at 6.5 bar(g) → 8.4 % per bar at 5.5 bar(g) → 10.6 % per bar The lower your setpoint already is, the more each further bar is worth.

But that is only half the saving, and the smaller half in most plants. The other half is artificial demand, and it is the reason page 1's plant is bleeding:

THE ARTIFICIAL-DEMAND LAW Every unregulated consumer — a leak, an open blow nozzle, an unregulated cylinder — is a fixed orifice. Its flow is proportional to upstream absolute pressure (page 6): Q ∝ p₁,abs So dropping 7.5 → 6.5 bar(g) cuts the flow through every one of them by 7.513 / 8.513 = 0.8825 → 11.75 % less air and that saving multiplies with the 7.4 % specific-power saving. For a plant that is 55 % unregulated, one bar is worth ~13 %, not 7 %.

AIR IS A SPRING — AND THE SPRING SETS YOUR CONTROL BANDWIDTH STRETCH

Trap air behind a piston and push. The gas compresses adiabatically (the stroke is far too fast for heat transfer), so pVγ is constant. Differentiate to get the stiffness of the resulting spring:

p V^γ = const → dp/dV = −γ p / V F = pA, dV = −A dx → k = γ · pabs · A² / V [N/m] For the page-1 clamp at mid-stroke: A = 3117 mm² = 3.117e-3 m² V = 3117 × 50 = 1.559e-4 m³ p = 6.013e5 Pa k = 1.4 × 6.013e5 × (3.117e-3)² / 1.559e-4 = 52.5 N/mm With a 100 kg moving load: fₙ = (1/2π)√(k/m) = 3.6 Hz

Three consequences follow immediately, and they explain most of what people find frustrating about pneumatics:

  • 1Stiffness rises with absolute pressure and falls with trapped volume. A cylinder near the end of its stroke is stiff; the same cylinder at mid-stroke, with long supply tubing adding dead volume, can be five times softer. Long tubing does not merely slow a cylinder — it makes it spongy.
  • 2Servo-pneumatic position control is bandwidth-limited by this resonance. At 3.6 Hz you cannot close a loop above roughly 1 Hz without exciting it. That is why servo-pneumatics exists in laboratories and press-force control, and almost nowhere else (A10, A09).
  • 3Stick-slip is this spring plus dry friction. Seal breakaway friction exceeds sliding friction; the spring winds up until breakaway, then releases and the piston jumps. It is the reason page 8 insists on meter-out control — a compressed cushion on the exhaust side is the only thing standing between you and a lurching axis.

Water, Oil and Dirt — Why Your Compressor Inhales 390 Litres of Water a Day

WHERE THE WATER COMES FROM CORE

A compressor does not make water. It concentrates it. Ambient air arrives carrying water vapour; compression squeezes the air into an eighth of its volume but the water has nowhere to go, so its partial pressure rises eightfold, blows past saturation, and the excess falls out as liquid. By Dalton's law the water vapour behaves as if the air were not there at all — it condenses purely according to its own partial pressure and the temperature.

B — it is liquid water that was already in the pipe, not vapour that condensed. THE PHYSICS — DARCY-WEISBACH FOR A COMPRESSIBLE FLUID CORE

At the Mach numbers found in a distribution main (typically 0.01–0.03) air behaves as an incompressible fluid locally, so Darcy-Weisbach applies — provided you evaluate density at line conditions, not free-air conditions. That is the step people miss, and it is worth a factor of eight.

THE FIFTH-POWER LAW Substituting v = Q/A ∝ Q/d² and holding f roughly constant: Δp ∝ Q^1.85 · L / ( d⁵ · p_abs ) One pipe size up divides the loss by about three. (63/50)⁵ = 3.18. The fifth power is why pipe sizing is never a close call. If the calculation says a size is marginal, go up one size and stop thinking about it — the material cost difference is a few per cent of the installation, and the energy difference is permanent. This is one of the very few places in engineering where the cheap answer and the right answer are the same answer.

THE RECEIVER — AND A SECOND, INDEPENDENT LEAK TEST STRETCH

A receiver does four jobs: damps pulsation, supplies short peaks the compressor cannot follow, gives condensate somewhere to fall out, and — most importantly for machine life — limits motor starts. The governing equation is just the ideal gas law rearranged for a fixed volume:

t = V · (p₁ − p₂) / ( Q · p_atm ) V system volume (receiver + pipework) [m³] p gauge pressures [bar] Q flow in or out, free air [m³/min] → t in minutes

Turn it around and it becomes the blow-down leak test — the cross-check for the Sunday duty-cycle measurement on page 1. Shut every machine, isolate the compressor, and time the pressure decay:

Ekala: V = 2.0 m³, pressure fell 7.5 → 6.5 bar(g) in 40 s Q_leak = 2.0 × 1.0 / ( (40/60) × 1.013 ) = 2.96 m³/min Duty-cycle method (page 1) 3.01 m³/min Blow-down method 2.96 m³/min Agreement within 1.7 % — two independent methods, one conclusion.

Always run both. They fail in different ways: the duty-cycle method is corrupted if the compressor's FAD has degraded (a worn machine no longer delivers its nameplate), and the blow-down method is corrupted if you guess the system volume badly. When they agree, you can defend the number in front of a finance director. Conductance — ISO 6358, and the End of the Cv-Versus-Kv Argument

THE PROBLEM ISO 6358 SOLVES CORE

Open four valve catalogues and you will find the same valve described four incompatible ways: a Cv (imperial, defined with water), a Kv (metric, also water), a Qn in Nl/min “at 6 bar with 1 bar drop”, and sometimes just a port thread. None of them can be compared, and none of them lets you compute what you actually need — the flow through a chain of components at the pressures your machine really runs at.

ISO 6358 fixes this by modelling every pneumatic component, whatever it is, as an equivalent converging nozzle described by exactly two numbers. That abstraction is the single most useful idea in applied pneumatics, and it is why this page is the hinge of the whole module.

C — sonic conductance

dm³/(s·bar). The flow the component passes per bar of upstream absolute pressure once the flow is choked. This is the number that matters.

b — critical pressure ratio

Dimensionless, 0.2–0.5. The downstream/upstream ratio at which the throat reaches Mach 1 and the flow stops caring about downstream pressure.

WHY FLOW CHOKES — THE PHYSICAL PICTURE CORE

Squeeze a gas through a restriction and it accelerates. Lower the downstream pressure and it accelerates more — until the throat reaches the local speed of sound. At that instant the throat can no longer be told about further reductions downstream, because pressure information travels at the speed of sound and the gas is now leaving faster than the news can travel upstream. The mass flow locks. Dropping the downstream pressure to a hard vacuum does not add one gram per second.

For air (γ = 1.4) the choking condition is p₂/p₁ ≤ ( 2/(γ+1) )^(γ/(γ−1)) = 0.528 for an ideal nozzle Real components choke earlier or later depending on how badly they diffuse the flow; that measured value is b. Sharp orifices b ≈ 0.2–0.35, smooth nozzles and long tubes b ≈ 0.4–0.5.

This is why a leak's flow is proportional to absolute supply pressure and nothing else — the atmosphere it discharges into is far below the critical ratio, so every leak in your plant is permanently choked. It is also the entire basis of the artificial-demand law on page 3.

COMPONENTS IN SERIES — THE LAW THAT RUNS THE REST OF THIS MODULE CORE

A real path is a valve, then fittings, then tube, then more fittings, then a cylinder port. Conductances in series do not add, and they do not combine like resistors either. Because flow depends on the square root of an effective pressure-drop term, they combine in quadrature reciprocals:

1 / C_total² = Σ 1 / Cᵢ² Consequences, and they are severe: • The chain is dominated by its smallest conductance, hard. • Two identical components in series give C/√2 = 0.707 C, not C/2. • Doubling the best component in a chain changes almost nothing. • A component 3× larger than the bottleneck contributes only 5 % of the total 1/C² — it is invisible. Find the bottleneck. (Strictly exact only when all bᵢ are equal; ISO 6358-1:2013 treats the general case. A machine's air path has four components: C = 3.0, 2.8, 0.9 and 3.2. You have budget to double exactly one of them. Which gives the biggest gain, and roughly how big is it?
C — double the 0.9, and it is worth about 55 %. Do the sum. 1/C² terms: 1/9 = 0.111, 1/7.84 = 0.128, 1/0.81 = 1.235, 1/10.24 = 0.098. Total 1.572, so C = 0.798. Doubling the 3.2 instead changes the total from 1.572 to 1.497, giving C = 0.817: a gain of 2.4 %, which no instrument in your factory can even measure. This is the most commercially useful consequence of the series law: in almost every real machine, one component owns most of the restriction, and every rupee spent anywhere else is wasted. Valves — Reading ISO 1219 Properly, and Choosing a Centre That Cannot Kill Anyone

A SYMBOL IS A MOVIE, NOT A PICTURE CORE

Every engineer thinks they can read a pneumatic circuit and most of them read it wrongly, because they treat the symbol as a drawing of the valve. It is not. Each square is a different position of the same valve, and the pipes are drawn connected only to the square that is currently active. To read the other position, mentally slide the squares until the next one lines up with the pipes. Once you have internalised that, ISO 1219 becomes trivial; until you have, it stays baffling.

The port numbers are not arbitrary and they are not letters any more. Air can hold a load; it cannot be trusted to hold a load. That distinction is page 12 in one sentence.

METER-OUT, NEVER METER-IN CORE

Both arrangements slow the cylinder. Only one of them controls it, and the reason is the air spring from page 3.

  • 1Meter-in starves the supply side. Pressure builds slowly behind the piston. Seal breakaway friction is 1.5–2× running friction, so nothing moves until the pressure reaches breakaway — and at that instant the piston is pushed by a charged spring against only sliding friction. It lurches forward, the trapped volume expands, the pressure collapses, it stalls, and the cycle repeats. That is stick-slip, and it destroys repeatability, position accuracy and seals.
  • 2Meter-out puts a compressed air cushion on the far side of the piston. The piston is now squeezed between a driving pressure and a resisting pressure. Any tendency to lurch immediately compresses the cushion, which pushes back harder. It is a genuine damper, in the mechanical sense, and it converts an unstable actuator into a stable one.
  • 3Fit the throttles as close to the cylinder ports as possible — ideally banjo-style, screwed straight into the port. Every centimetre of tube between throttle and cylinder is dead volume that softens the cushion.
  • 4The exceptions, and they are real. Meter-in is correct for a single-acting cylinder on the powered stroke (there is no exhaust side to meter), and for very small bores or vacuum-driven motion where the exhaust cushion cannot form. And a running-away load — a vertical cylinder lowering a mass — needs meter-out on the supporting side or it free-falls.
  • 5Speed controls are one-way restrictors with an integral check valve. Flow is throttled in one direction and free in the other. Fit them the right way round: for meter-out extend, the free direction must be into the port. Fitting them backwards silently converts a meter-out circuit into a meter-in circuit, and the symptom is the stick-slip trace above.
  • 6A quick-exhaust valve is the opposite tool and often the cheapest fix in pneumatics. Screwed into the cylinder port, it dumps the annulus straight to atmosphere through a large opening instead of back through the valve, effectively removing the exhaust path from the chain. On a stroke that needs to be fast and does not need to be controlled — an ejector, a gate, a stamp — it can halve the time for a few hundred rupees.

Cushioning — the End of the Stroke Is Where Cylinders Die

WHAT ACTUALLY ARRIVES AT THE END CAP CORE

Everyone sizes the kinetic energy. Almost nobody sizes the second term, which is usually thirty times larger. A pneumatic cylinder does not coast into its end stop — it is still being pushed, at full supply pressure, throughout the entire deceleration.

E_total = ½ m v² + F_thrust · s_decel m total moving mass: piston, rod, coupling, tooling and workpiece v impact velocity at the start of deceleration F_thrust p · A — the cylinder is still driving s_decel the distance over which you stop it
Repaired Ekala clamp: m = 6 kg, v = 0.60 m/s, 63 mm bore at 6.0 bar(g), stopped over a 20 mm shock-absorber stroke. E_kinetic = ½ × 6 × 0.60² = 1.08 J E_thrust = (6.0e5 × 3.117e-3) × 0.020 = 37.4 J ---------------------------------------------------- E_total = 38.5 J (97 % of it thrust) Equivalent mass, the figure absorber catalogues want: m_e = 2 E_total / v² = 2 × 38.5 / 0.36 = 214 kg The 6 kg you weighed behaves like 214 kg. This is why cylinders that "only move a small part" still tear their end caps off.

And then the number that catches people out even when they have done the energy sum correctly — the thermal rating:

At 30 cycles/min: 38.5 J × 1800 cycles/h = 69,300 J/h A typical small industrial shock absorber: 30 J per cycle ✓ passes 20,000 J/h ✗ fails by 3.5× Shock absorbers overwhelmingly fail on the hourly thermal rating, not the per-cycle rating. The absorber gets hot, the oil thins, the damping collapses, and the unit bottoms out — usually within weeks, and always blamed on "a bad absorber".
HOW AN INTERNAL CUSHION WORKS — AND WHY THE NEEDLE MATTERS CORE

Near the end of travel a cushioning boss on the piston enters a matching recess in the end cap and closes off the main exhaust port with a cushion seal. From that instant, the only way out for the trapped air is an adjustable needle valve. The trapped volume is then compressed adiabatically by the arriving piston.

Cushion length 20 mm on a 63 mm bore: V_trapped = 3117 × 20 = 62.3 cm³ at roughly 6.0 bar a If the needle were fully closed and the piston compressed it to 10 %: p_f = 6.0 × (10)^1.4 = 151 bar absolute Real cushions bleed through the needle and peak at 15–25 bar. That is still two to four times supply, on a casting designed for 10 bar working — which is exactly why the peak must be controlled and never simply removed.
Deceleration available: a = v² / (2 s) 0.6 m/s over 20 mm → 9 m/s² (0.9 g) — comfortable 1.0 m/s over 5 mm → 100 m/s² (10 g) — damaging

Setting a cushion, correctly, in four steps. This is a five-minute job that most plants never do, and it is worth more cylinder life than any lubricant:

  • 1Screw the needle fully in (closed), then back it off one full turn. Never run a cylinder with the needle fully closed — the pressure spike above is real.
  • 2Run the machine at full working speed with the real load. A cushion set on a dry-run at half speed is set wrong; the energy scales with v².
  • 3Open the needle gradually until the piston just reaches the end without an audible knock. Too open gives a metallic knock; too closed makes the piston slow abruptly and bounce back off the compressed cushion, which shows up as a sensor flickering or a cycle-time variation of a few tens of milliseconds.
  • 4Lock the needle and record the setting. Write the number of turns on a label. This survives the next fitter.

A knock at the end of a stroke is a cushion problem; a rattle mid-stroke is stick-slip from a meter-in circuit; a hiss that persists after motion stops is a leak. Then confirm with a reed switch on the cylinder and a scope: a cushion set too tight shows a characteristic double-edge as the piston bounces off the end and returns. It takes ten minutes and it is the difference between guessing and knowing.

End-cap cracking

Repeated 20 bar cushion spikes on a 10 bar casting. Shows as a weep at the cap joint months before it lets go.

Tie-rod fatigue

Impact loads cycle the rods. They stretch, the caps loosen, the seals go, and the cylinder is scrap. Check tie-rod torque annually.

Rod bearing wear

Impact plus side load ovalises the front bearing. The first symptom is a fine oil mist at the rod wiper, not a leak.

Vacuum — the Venturi, the Cup, and the 90 % Saving Nobody Takes

VACUUM IS NOT NEGATIVE PRESSURE, IT IS MISSING ATMOSPHERE CORE

There is no such thing as suction. A vacuum cup does not pull; the atmosphere pushes, and all you have done is remove some of the air that was pushing back on the other side. HOW A VENTURI EJECTOR MAKES VACUUM CORE

Motive air is accelerated through a small converging-diverging nozzle to supersonic speed. Its static pressure collapses as its velocity rises, so the suction chamber around the jet sits below atmosphere, and gas from the cup is entrained into the jet by momentum exchange. The mixture is then recovered in a diffuser and exhausted. There are no moving parts, nothing to wear, and the response is a few milliseconds — which is why ejectors dominate pick-and-place despite being thermodynamically wasteful.

THE 90 % SAVING — AIR-SAVING VACUUM CORE

A vacuum ejector holding a non-porous part is doing nothing. The part is sealed; once the volume is evacuated, no further gas is arriving, and every litre of motive air is being spent to maintain a vacuum that would maintain itself. The fix is two components:

  • 1A non-return valve between the ejector and the cups, so the vacuum cannot bleed back through the ejector when the motive air stops.
  • 2A vacuum switch that turns the motive air off when the setpoint is reached and back on if it decays by a set hysteresis — typically 10–15 % of the level.
Ekala: 8 ejectors, 1.0 mm nozzles, motive 44.1 Nl/min each Continuous: 8 × 44.1 = 353 Nl/min = 0.353 m³/min × 5.91 kW/(m³/min) = 2.09 kW LKR 39,033 per month With air-saving, non-porous carton board, measured duty 8 %: 0.028 m³/min = 0.17 kW LKR 3,122 per month Saving LKR 35,911/month for about LKR 96,000 of parts — 81 days.

The honest caveat, which vendors omit. Air-saving works spectacularly on smooth, non-porous parts — glass, sheet metal, film, laminated board. On porous parts — corrugated board, unlaminated cartonboard, fabric, filter media, wood — the leak rate through the material itself keeps triggering the switch, the ejector chatters, the duty cycle stays high, and you have bought complexity for very little. Measure the duty cycle before you buy: hold a sample part, switch the motive air off, and time how long the vacuum takes to decay by 10 %. If the answer is under a second, air-saving will not pay.

At the Machine — the FRL, the Regulator That Droops, and Two Pressures Instead of One

THE FRL, AND WHY THE L IS USUALLY WRONG NOW CORE

The filter-regulator-lubricator unit is the boundary between your plant's air and your machine's air. It is also the last place anybody looks, and it contains the single most misunderstood component in pneumatics.

F — Filter

5 µm general purpose, with a water separator. Protects the regulator diaphragm and the valve spools. Change annually, on a calendar.

R — Regulator

Reduces plant pressure to what the machine needs. The energy-saving device nobody uses as one. Set it under load, not statically.

L — Lubricator

Almost always wrong today. Modern valves and cylinders are pre-lubricated for life. Adding oil washes the factory grease out and starts a dependency you can never stop.

The lubricator rule, in full: Fit a lubricator only if • the component manufacturer explicitly requires it, or • the equipment is old and has always been lubricated, or • duty is extreme: high cycle rate, high temperature, air motors. Never fit one on a line feeding vacuum ejectors, sensitive valves, food or pharma equipment, or anything downstream of a shared manifold. And once started, never stop: the original grease is gone. This is a one-way door. Choose it deliberately, in writing.
REGULATOR DROOP — THE GAUGE THAT LIES WHILE THE MACHINE RUNS CORE

A regulator is a spring balanced against a diaphragm. To pass more flow the poppet must open further, which compresses the spring further, which means the balanced pressure must be lower. Output pressure therefore falls with flow, and the falling characteristic is called droop. It is not a fault; it is how the device works.

A typical G1/4 regulator, set to 6.0 bar with no flow: 0 Nl/min 6.00 bar ← what the gauge shows when you set it 500 Nl/min 5.85 bar 1000 Nl/min 5.60 bar 1500 Nl/min 5.20 bar 2500 Nl/min 4.40 bar (its "rated" flow) The cylinder on page 8 needs 6.0 bar to make its force and draws about 1,500 Nl/min while it strokes. It sees 5.2 bar. The gauge says 6.0. The operator says "the pressure is fine". Both are telling the truth.
  • 1Set every regulator with the machine cycling at full speed, watching the gauge during the stroke. If the gauge dips visibly, the regulator is undersized — go one port size up. This costs a few thousand rupees and cures a class of fault that otherwise gets blamed on the compressor.
  • 2Relieving or non-relieving? A relieving regulator vents downstream air to atmosphere when you turn the setting down, or when downstream pressure rises above setpoint (from a load pushing back on a cylinder). A non-relieving one does not, so the trapped pressure stays until something else releases it. Use relieving as the default; use non-relieving where venting to atmosphere is unacceptable — and know which one you have before you troubleshoot.
  • 3Pilot-operated regulators use the outlet pressure to control a pilot rather than a spring, which collapses droop to a few tenths of a bar over the full flow range. Specify them on any machine with a large, fast actuator — a press, a big clamp, a blow-mould.
  • 4Hysteresis is real. Approach the setpoint from below and lock. If you overshoot and come back down on a non-relieving unit, the actual pressure may sit 0.2–0.4 bar above the reading.
  • 5Fit a lockable setting or a tamper cap. The regulator is the one adjustment on a machine that an operator can turn to “fix” almost any symptom, and turning it up always works, briefly. This is precisely how the plant on page 1 arrived where it did.
TWO PRESSURES INSTEAD OF ONE CORE

Here is the cheapest structural saving in machine pneumatics, and it is almost never done. Ask what each stroke of an actuator actually needs. Usually one stroke does the work and the other merely returns the tooling — and yet both are charged to full pressure, because there is one regulator.

Repaired Ekala clamp, 63 mm, 100 mm stroke, 2.5 m of 6 mm bore each side: One pressure, 6.0 bar both ways extend (0.3117 + 0.0857) × 6.013 = 2.390 Nl retract (0.2803 + 0.0857) × 6.013 = 2.200 Nl total = 4.590 Nl / cycle Dual pressure: extend 6.0 bar, retract 2.5 bar extend = 2.390 Nl retract (0.2803 + 0.0857) × 3.513 = 1.286 Nl total = 3.676 Nl / cycle Saving 19.9 % on that cylinder, at 30 cycles/min: 0.0275 m³/min → 0.163 kW → LKR 3,050 / month Across a plant with 40 similar actuators: LKR 122,000 / month Hardware: one extra regulator and a check valve per axis.

The circuit is trivial: feed port 4 through the full-pressure regulator and port 2 through a second, lower-pressure regulator, each with its own check valve so the exhaust paths stay independent. The engineering judgement is in choosing the retract pressure — it must still overcome friction, any residual load and the tooling weight, with enough margin to make time. Measure it: turn the second regulator down until the retract time degrades, then add 1 bar.

PREDICT FIRST
A machine's FRL gauge reads a rock-steady 6.0 bar. The cylinder is slow and weak. Which measurement do you take next?
B — at the port, fast, while it moves. A steady gauge reading at the FRL is a statement about static conditions and tells you almost nothing, for two compounding reasons. First, an analogue gauge has a needle with mass and a damping orifice; it physically cannot follow a 250 ms transient, so it shows you the average, not the dip. Second, the FRL is upstream of the regulator's droop, the valve, the fittings and the tube — the entire conductance chain from page 6 lies between that gauge and the piston. Fit a fast pressure transducer (1 kHz is plenty) into the cylinder's own port, cycle the machine, and look at the trace. You will see one of three signatures, and each names its own fault: a slow rise to full pressure means the supply chain is restricted; a fast rise then a collapse means the regulator or the plant cannot sustain the flow; and a rise that stalls part-way and stays there means the exhaust side is choked and back pressure is holding the piston. That single measurement, which takes twenty minutes, replaces weeks of guessing.

Safety — Stored Energy, and the Load That Air Cannot Be Trusted to Hold

HOW MUCH ENERGY IS IN A RECEIVER? CORE

Compressed air feels benign because it is invisible, odourless and everywhere. Put a number on it and the feeling changes permanently.

Available work on sudden release (isothermal expansion to atmosphere): W = p₁V₁ ln(p₁/p₀) − (p₁ − p₀) V₁
A 2 m³ receiver at 7.5 bar(g): W = 851,300 × 2 × 2.1287 − (851,300 − 101,300) × 2 = 3,624,300 − 1,500,000 = 2.12 MJ 1 kg of TNT releases 4.18 MJ. That receiver stores the explosive energy of about half a kilogram of TNT. And a single 12 mm hose at 7 bar, if it lets go at a fitting: jet thrust F = p · A = 7e5 × 1.13e-4 = 79 N, applied at the free end of a whipping steel-braided hose. That is why hose whip restraints exist.
ISO 4414 — WHAT IT ACTUALLY REQUIRES OF YOU CORE

ISO 4414:2010, Pneumatic fluid power — General rules and safety requirements for systems and their components, is the parent standard. It sits under ISO 12100 (risk assessment) and above the machine-specific C-standards, and it interacts with ISO 13849-1 for the performance level of safety functions (A52, A35). The obligations it puts on a designer are concrete:

  • 1Provide a means of isolating and exhausting stored energy, lockable in the isolated position. Not a ball valve you can padlock — a valve that vents downstream when locked off.
  • 2Consider the behaviour of every actuator on loss and restoration of pressure, and on loss and restoration of electrical power — separately, and in both orders. Document the answer for each.
  • 3Prevent uncontrolled movement on restoration. This is what the soft-start valve is for, and it is a requirement, not a nicety.
  • 4Secure against unintended gravity fall where the actuator supports a load. Air is not a means of support.
  • 5Restrain hoses that could whip, and protect against hot surfaces, noise and the discharge of exhaust into a person's face.
  • 6Mark and document. Circuit diagrams to ISO 1219-2, port identification, setting values, and the residual risks that remain for the user to manage.
LOCKOUT ON A PNEUMATIC MACHINE — THE STEP EVERYONE MISSES CORE
The correct sequence: ISOLATE → EXHAUST → VERIFY ZERO → TRY-OUT The step that kills people is VERIFY, and pneumatics has a specific trap that hydraulics does not: A 5/3 closed-centre valve, or any pilot-operated check valve, or any non-relieving regulator, will hold full pressure at the cylinder after the main isolation valve is locked and the manifold gauge reads zero. The manifold is at 0 bar. The cylinder is at 6 bar. The gauge is telling the truth about the wrong place. Verify at the actuator, or fit a manual bleed at every trapped volume, and include "operate all valve solenoids manually to release trapped air" in the written procedure.

And the second trap: a spring-return actuator still has stored energy after the air is gone. A spring-applied brake, a spring-return clamp, or a counterbalance will move when the air is removed — which is exactly what it is designed to do, and exactly what nobody expects while their hands are inside the guard.

SAFE EXHAUST AND DUMP VALVES STRETCH

Where the safety function is “remove pneumatic power on demand” — guard opened, light curtain broken, emergency stop — a plain solenoid valve is not sufficient, because a single spool sticking defeats it and nothing detects that it has. The certified answer is a safe exhaust (dump) valve:

  • 1Two independent channels in one body, each capable of isolating and exhausting on its own.
  • 2Cross-monitoring between the channels, so a discrepancy is detected and the valve latches into the safe state rather than silently degrading to single-channel.
  • 3A feedback signal to the safety controller, so the fault is annunciated and the machine cannot be restarted.
  • 4Large exhaust capacity, because the required performance is expressed in time: the machine must reach a safe state inside the stopping time assumed by your light-curtain safety distance calculation (A35 derives that distance).
  • 5Rated to a Performance Level — typically PL d or PL e per ISO 13849-1, with a stated PFHd and B10d. Use the manufacturer's figures in your own calculation; do not assume the valve carries the whole function.
  • 6The exhaust must be able to breathe. A dump valve behind a fouled silencer takes longer to vent than the calculation assumed, and the safety distance is now wrong. Silencers on safety exhausts are a maintenance item with a written interval.
SIX RULES THAT ARE NOT NEGOTIABLE CORE
Never blow dust off skin or clothing

Air injected under the skin at as little as 2 bar can enter the bloodstream and be fatal. Blow guns must be pressure-limited (2 bar dead-ended) with a relief port and chip guarding.

Never use ordinary PVC pipe

Brittle fracture releases the stored energy of page 12 as fragments. Use metal or a compressed-air-rated system.

Never rely on a detented valve to hold

It holds its position, not its pressure. The load falls when the air bleeds away — minutes to hours later, with nobody watching.

Never plug an exhaust port to slow a cylinder

It converts speed control into an uncontrolled pressure rise and destroys the valve. Use a proper meter-out restrictor.

Never work on a “dead” machine without verifying at the actuator

The manifold gauge reads the manifold. Trapped volumes read whatever they trapped.

Never exceed the receiver's inspection regime

It is a pressure vessel. Safety valve tested, thickness checked, certificate current — and the drain must work, because internal corrosion is what actually thins the shell.

If you take one thing from this page: ISOLATE → EXHAUST → VERIFY AT THE ACTUATOR. The trapped volume behind a closed-centre valve is the pneumatic equivalent of a charged capacitor, and it has the same reputation for surprising people who thought they had switched it off.

THE OPEN EDGE RESEARCH

Three things in this field are genuinely unsettled, and all three are worth watching if you intend to specialise:

  • 1Exhaust-air energy recovery. Roughly a third of the energy delivered to a cylinder leaves through the silencer. Recovering it — with small expansion turbines, with exhaust-to-inlet recycling on a two-pressure network, or by re-using an actuator's exhaust to drive a lower-pressure consumer — is technically demonstrated and economically marginal. The published payback figures vary by an order of magnitude, which usually means the boundary conditions are not being stated. Read any claim here with the demand profile in your hand.
  • 2Model-based and learning control for servo-pneumatics. The air spring plus stiction makes a badly-behaved nonlinear plant, and classical loop shaping tops out where page 3 says it must. Model-predictive and learning-based schemes have pushed positioning accuracy into the tens of microns in laboratories. Whether any of it survives contact with a factory's supply-pressure variation is still open.
  • 3Continuous, per-actuator condition monitoring. Valve terminals now carry flow and pressure sensing per zone (A05), so a cylinder's own air signature — consumption per cycle, fill-time trend, exhaust-time trend — is becoming a measurable quantity. That turns leak detection, seal wear and silencer fouling into trend problems rather than survey problems. The physics on page 8 is exactly the model those diagnostics need, and the interesting research question is how few sensors are enough.
PREDICT FIRST
A plant runs two 55 kW compressors. Average demand is 55 % of one machine's capacity. Both are on independent pressure switches, load/unload, with a small receiver. What is the highest-value control action?
B — sequencer plus receiver, and it is cheap. Read the symptom first: two machines on independent pressure switches, with a small receiver, at 55 % demand. That combination guarantees the worst case — both machines running part-loaded and cycling too fast to ever fully unload, each drawing 20–30 % of rated power while producing nothing. A sequencer makes one machine the base load, running fully loaded near its best efficiency, and lets the other trim; a larger receiver lengthens the cycle so the trim machine actually reaches its unloaded state. Together that is typically 15–25 % of the room's energy for well under a million rupees, with no change to the air system at all. A is the popular answer and is premature — a VSD is excellent, but fitting one before you have sequenced the room means it will spend its life fighting the other machine's pressure switch. C may well be right eventually, but page 13's own hierarchy forbids sizing a new compressor before the demand side is fixed — and this plant has not yet done a leak survey. D is actively harmful: raising cut-out raises the average discharge pressure, costing 7–10 % per bar and increasing every leak, to solve a cycling problem that a receiver solves for a fraction of the cost.

Recap, Failure Modes and Quick Check

THE EIGHT IDEAS THAT CARRY THE SUBJECT CORE
  • 1Compressed air is a manufactured utility with 10–15 % wire-to-work efficiency. It costs roughly eight times what electricity costs for the same delivered work, and the bill arrives invisibly inside the electricity account. Everything economic follows from this.
  • 2Always absolute. Pressure and temperature enter every law here as absolute quantities. Gauge pressure is a convenience for gauges and a trap for engineers.
  • 3Q = 12 · S · p₁ in Nl/min, mm² and bar absolute; equivalently Q = 60 · C · p₁ with S = 5C. A leak is a permanently choked orifice, so its flow is proportional to absolute supply pressure and nothing else — which is the artificial-demand law.
  • 4Conductances in series combine as 1/C² = Σ1/Cᵢ², so a chain is dominated by its smallest member and money spent anywhere else is wasted. Tube conductance is 0.059 di2.5/√L — length hurts twice, because it also adds dead volume.
  • 5t = (A·s + V_dead)·p_w / (C·p₁·Ψ) on the supply side, and v ≤ C_out/A_a on the exhaust side — and the exhaust limit contains no pressure term. That single cancellation explains why raising a plant's setpoint to speed up a cylinder is always a partial fix and always a permanent cost.
  • 6Meter out, not in. The air on the exhaust side is the only damper a pneumatic cylinder has. And at the end of the stroke, E = ½mv² + F·s, where the second term usually dominates by a factor of thirty.
  • 7A +3 °C pressure dew point becomes −24 °C at the tool. Refrigerant drying is sufficient for almost all industrial pneumatics; a heatless desiccant dryer burns 15–20 % of your air forever to solve a problem you did not have.
  • 8Efficiency measures multiply, they do not add, and some of them are blocked by others until those are done. Sequence the work, re-baseline between steps, and present the sequence rather than the shopping list.
SIX FAILURE MODES THAT WILL FIND YOU CORE
1 · The silent silencer

A sintered bronze silencer clogged with oil mist and dust drops from C = 2.0 to 0.4, pushing an exhaust-limited stroke from 0.47 s to 0.82 s. No gauge shows it, no alarm exists, and the machine is blamed on the compressor. Detect: unscrew it and cycle the machine. Prevent: calendar replacement, and specify a silencer one size up.

2 · The leak that grows back

A survey is an event; leakage is a process. Untended, it regrows at 10–20 % a year as fittings age and hoses flex, so a plant that surveys once is back to 30 % within five years having spent the money twice. Prevent: quarterly ultrasonic survey, tagged, ranked by rupees, with the total published.

3 · The pressure ratchet

Every slow machine gets “fixed” with another 0.5 bar, and nobody ever puts it back. Ten years of this is a plant at 8 bar that needs 5.5. Prevent: the setpoint is a controlled document with a stated derivation, and changing it requires a change request.

4 · Exhaust-blind sizing

An engineer sizes the supply chain beautifully, meets the calculated stroke time, and the machine still misses takt — because the exhaust path was never in the calculation. Prevent: compute both limits, always, and state them separately in the design file.

5 · The reversed speed controller

A one-way restrictor fitted backwards silently converts a meter-out circuit into a meter-in circuit. The cylinder develops stick-slip, repeatability collapses, and the fault is chased in the PLC for weeks. Detect: the motion trace on page 8. Prevent: mark flow direction on the drawing and check at commissioning.

6 · Trapped pressure at lockout

The main isolator is locked, the manifold gauge reads zero, and the cylinder behind a closed-centre valve or a pilot check valve is still at 6 bar. Prevent: verify at the actuator, fit manual bleeds on trapped volumes, and put “operate all solenoids manually” into the written procedure.

3. Your plant runs at 7.0 bar(g). A consultant proposes dropping to 6.0 bar and quotes “7 % saving”. Your leak survey says the plant is 35 % unregulated. What do you tell him?
Tap to reveal “You are under-selling your own proposal by about half — it is worth roughly 11 %, not 7 %.” Two effects multiply. 4. A vendor insists you need a −40 °C desiccant dryer for a general assembly plant in Katunayake. Your existing refrigerant dryer holds +3 °C pressure dew point at 7 bar(g). What is your response, with numbers?
Tap to reveal “At +3 °C and 8 bar absolute, the dew point at the tool is about −23 °C. Water cannot condense anywhere in this building, at any hour of any month. What are we buying?” The arithmetic: p_v at +3 °C saturation is 757 Pa; expanding from 8.013 to 1.013 bar divides it by 7.91, giving 96 Pa, which inverts to a dew point of about −23 °C. Sri Lanka's lowest lowland temperature is around 18 °C. And the honest exceptions, because they are real: you would need low PDP for outdoor pipework in a hill-country site, for air stored or used below 0 °C, for direct food or pharmaceutical contact, for instrument air per ISA-7.0.01, for laser optics, and for paint lines where the oil digit matters more than the water digit anyway.
5. A vertical cylinder holds a 40 kg tool head. The circuit uses a 5/3 exhaust-centre valve so operators can jog the head by hand during setup. A safety auditor red-lines it. Is the auditor right, and what do you propose?
Tap to reveal The auditor is right, and the fix is not a different valve. An exhaust-centre valve vents both cylinder ports in the mid position, which is precisely the state the machine enters on de-energisation — so a power failure drops 40 kg. Switching to a closed-centre valve is better but not sufficient: the trapped air leaks past the spool lands and the cylinder seals, and the head descends over minutes to hours, which is arguably worse because it happens when nobody is watching. The correct proposal has three parts: (1) a pilot-operated check valve mounted directly on the lower cylinder port, so even a burst hose cannot drain the supporting side; (2) a mechanical rod lock, spring-applied and air-released, so loss of air engages the lock; and (3) a written requirement for a positive mechanical prop before anybody works underneath, because a rod lock is a machine element and maintenance access needs a physical one. Keep the jogging capability by adding a key-switch setup mode that releases the lock at reduced pressure — do not remove a safety function to preserve a convenience.
6. A pick-and-place head with eight vacuum ejectors handles glossy laminated cartons. Someone proposes air-saving vacuum. Six months later the measured saving is 15 %, not the 90 % promised. What went wrong — and how could it have been predicted in five minutes?
Tap to reveal The parts leak, so the vacuum switch keeps re-triggering and the duty cycle never falls. Air-saving works by evacuating once and then closing a check valve; it only pays if the vacuum decays slowly enough that the ejector stays off for most of the hold. “Glossy laminated” describes the surface, not the edges — a die-cut carton leaks at every cut edge and through the flute if the lamination is single-sided, and a cup landing slightly off a fold seals badly. Add a hysteresis set too tight (5 % instead of 12 %) and the ejector chatters continuously. The five-minute prediction: take one real production part, hold it on the actual cup, switch the motive air off, and time the decay from −0.7 to −0.6 bar. If that takes under a second, air-saving will not pay on this part; if it takes ten seconds, it will pay handsomely. This test costs nothing, needs one vacuum gauge, and should be a condition of every air-saving purchase order.

On the revised demand the correct machine is roughly 22 kW VSD as the trim unit, with one existing 37 kW as base load and one as standby — and we may find we need no new machine at all. The finding nobody expected: the specific power of 7.27 looked like worn compressors and was used to justify replacement. Eliminating it removes 28 % of the qualified demand, shrinks the skid, and deletes a validation point from the HACCP plan. Ask, of every compressed-air consumer in a regulated process: does this need compressed air, or does it need something moved? The cheapest compliant air is the air you never make.

WE5 — THE FORENSIC: A GRIP WITH ELEVEN TIMES THE REQUIRED FORCE THAT KEEPS DROPPING PARTS STRETCH

A cosmetics plant in Wattala runs a delta robot placing 180 g bottles into cartons at 78 picks/min. Four 20 mm cups, one 0.7 mm ejector, vacuum −0.65 bar. It drops 1.4 bottles per 1,000 picks — about 25 per shift. Maintenance has replaced the cups twice and the ejector once. Your first calculation says the fault is impossible.

THE STATIC CHECK 4 cups × (π/4)(20)² = 1,257 mm² = 1.257e-3 m² F_hold = 1.257e-3 × 65,000 = 81.7 N Load at the robot's mean acceleration of 30 m/s²: 0.180 × (9.81 + 30) = 7.17 N safety factor 11.4 Even at a peak 150 m/s²: 0.180 × 160 = 28.8 N safety factor 2.8 The grip cannot fail. And yet it fails 25 times a shift.
THE INPUT THAT WAS WRONG The static model asks "is the grip strong enough once established?" The failure is in establishing it. Evacuated volume: 4 cups (8 cm³) + 1.8 m of 6 mm hose (51 cm³) + fittings (6 cm³) = 65 cm³ Ejector suction 45 Nl/min = 750 cm³/s t = (V/S) · ln(1/0.35) × 2.5 (real curve) = (65/750) × 1.050 × 2.5 = 0.227 s Grip window in a 0.77 s cycle: 0.29 s Margin: 0.063 s = 22 % Six months ago a fitter replaced the 4 mm hose with 6 mm "to improve flow". old V = 8 + 22.6 + 6 = 36.6 cm³ → t = 0.128 s, margin 55 % The "improvement" cut the margin from 55 % to 22 %. On the vacuum side, bigger hose is slower (page 10).

Why 1.4 per thousand and not all of them? Because 0.227 s is a mean, and the model treated it as a value. Cup landing position varies with bottle presentation, and a cup that lands slightly across a moulding parting line leaks during evacuation, extending the time-to-setpoint. The drop rate is the fraction of the distribution's right-hand tail that crosses 0.29 s. That is why it is intermittent, why it correlates with nothing anyone measured, and why replacing cups helped for a week each time (new cups seal better, then take a set).

THE MEASUREMENT THAT PROVES IT — ONE SHIFT, ONE LOGGER Log the vacuum switch output against the robot's grip-confirm signal at 1 kHz for 10,000 picks. Histogram the time-to-setpoint. Expected: a mean near 0.23 s with a long right tail; the drop events sit entirely in the tail beyond 0.29 s, and they correlate with time-to-setpoint, not with mass, acceleration or cup age. THE FIX — TURN A DISTRIBUTION INTO A CONSTANT 1. Fit a 0.25 l vacuum reservoir with a non-return valve between ejector and cups, held evacuated continuously. The cups are now evacuated from a reservoir 4× their own volume rather than through an ejector, and time-to-setpoint collapses to ~0.03 s regardless of landing quality. LKR 9,500 per head. 2. Revert to 4 mm hose and mount the ejector on the tool, 1.0 m: V = 8 + 12.6 + 6 = 26.6 cm³. 3. Interlock the robot's accelerate command on the vacuum switch, so a slow evacuation becomes a 30 ms delay instead of a dropped bottle.

The transferable finding. When a model says a failure is impossible and the failure keeps happening, the error is almost never in the equation — it is in an input, and specifically in a quantity that is a distribution where the model assumed a value. Ask which input varies pick-to-pick, log it, and look at the tail rather than the mean. Then, wherever you can, engineer the variation away instead of tightening the tolerance on it: a reservoir that makes evacuation time independent of leak rate is a structurally better answer than better cups, because it does not depend on anybody maintaining anything.

Absorber sizing is one of the few problems in engineering where the obvious remedy partially defeats itself, and most people meet it the hard way.

(c) Remove the thrust, not the kinetic energy. Fit a mid-stroke sensor 40 mm before the end and use it to de-energise the valve to an exhaust-centre or vent condition, so the drive pressure is released before the piston reaches the absorber. E_thrust collapses to whatever residual pressure remains — typically under 15 J — and E_total falls to roughly 20 J with a thermal load of 26,000 J/h, comfortably inside a small standard absorber. The alternative, if the machine cannot spare the sensor, is a dual-pressure circuit that drops the extend pressure to the minimum the load actually needs; if 1,200 N is enough, 2.7 bar gives F = 1,357 N, E_total = 42 J and 55,400 J/h. Both fixes cost less than the larger absorber.

P9  [8 marks] — For each actuator, state the behaviour on (i) loss of electrical power and (ii) loss of air, and say whether it is acceptable. 1 Horizontal indexing pusher, 5/2 single solenoid, spring to retract. 2 Vertical clamp holding a 60 kg die, 5/3 closed centre. 3 Robot gripper holding a 3 kg part over a walkway, 5/2 double solenoid detented. 4 Powered guard door, 3/2 NC spring to close. 5 Blow-off nozzle, 2/2 NC.
Tap to reveal 1 — Acceptable. Power loss: retracts under spring, a defined and generally safe state. Air loss: stops where it is, then drifts as pressure bleeds. Confirm nothing is trapped in the retract path, and that the machine's restart logic does not assume a known position.

2 — Not acceptable. Power loss: valve centres and the trapped air holds the die briefly, then it descends as the spool lands and cylinder seals leak — minutes to hours later, unwitnessed. Air loss: same, faster. Fix: pilot-operated check valve on the cylinder port plus a spring-applied mechanical rod lock, and a written requirement for a positive mechanical prop before maintenance access.

3 — Not acceptable. Power loss: the detent holds the valve position, so grip is retained as long as the air lasts. Air loss: grip is lost immediately and 3 kg falls on a walkway. Fix: a spring-closed (fail-clamped) gripper or a mechanical latch, plus routing the path so it does not cross a walkway — and if it must, guarding beneath it. Do not rely on a vacuum reservoir alone.

4 — The interesting one. Power loss: the door closes under spring. That is “fail safe” for machine access and simultaneously a trapping hazard for anyone in the doorway. The two safety requirements are in direct conflict and the mark is for saying so. Fix: limit the closing force and speed (a force/energy limit as in A35), fit a safety edge or light curtain in the aperture, or make the door fail open and interlock the machine instead. Decide by risk assessment, not by convention.

5 — Acceptable. Both failures stop the air, which is safe. The residual issue is restoration: without a soft-start valve, air returning to a de-energised 2/2 NC does nothing, but elsewhere on the machine it will slam actuators to their rest positions. The soft-start is a machine-level requirement, not a per-actuator one.
P10  [8 marks] — Three requests arrive in one morning. Answer each, with the reasoning you would actually give. (a) Production wants the plant setpoint raised from 6.3 to 7.0 bar for one week to get a rush order through a slow station. (b) A supervisor wants the meter-out throttle removed from a cylinder that lowers a 40 kg fixture, to save 0.4 s per cycle. (c) An operator asks for a blow gun to clean dust off his overalls.
Tap to reveal (a) Conditional yes, with instrumentation and a dated reversion — and a better alternative on the table. First measure: log the station's port pressure during the stroke. If it is conductance-limited, which it almost certainly is, the extra 0.7 bar buys roughly 10–15 % of stroke time while costing about 5 % of the entire plant's compressor energy for as long as it lasts — and “one week” setpoint changes have a documented habit of becoming permanent (failure mode 3). Agree only with: a written reversion date, a raised work order for the real fix, and a measured before/after. Better still, offer the alternative: a small local receiver and booster, or simply a temporary larger tube and valve at that one station — a few thousand rupees, delivered the same day, with no plant-wide cost.

(b) Refuse, and explain the physics rather than citing a rule. On a lowering stroke the load is driving, and the meter-out restrictor on the supporting side is the only thing metering the descent. Remove it and the fixture runs away: the cylinder cannot resist, the piston outruns the supply, a partial vacuum forms behind it, and 40 kg arrives at the end stop at free-fall velocity — E_kin alone would be about 100 J from a 250 mm drop, against a cushion rated for a fraction of that. Offer the legitimate route to the 0.4 s: check the raise stroke's conductance chain, check the silencer, and check whether the cycle can overlap this move with another.

(c) Refuse absolutely, and do not negotiate. Compressed air directed at skin can enter the bloodstream through a pore or a small cut and cause a fatal embolism at pressures as low as 2 bar; directed at an ear or an eye it causes permanent injury; and it aerosolises the dust you were trying to remove into everyone's breathing zone. This is one of the very small number of requests in this module that has no engineered compromise. Provide the alternative immediately, because a refusal without a substitute simply moves the behaviour out of sight: a personnel vacuum unit, a brush-down station, or a change of workwear. If blow guns are needed for equipment, specify OSHA-style guns that are pressure-limited to 2 bar dead-ended, with relief ports and chip guarding, and label them for equipment use only.

Four results this module owns. Every number on pages 1–14 traces back to one of them. Work through each with a pen; the algebra is short, and in three of the four something cancels that you did not expect.

D1 — THE CHOKED-ORIFICE CONSTANT, AND WHY S = 5C CORE

Start from steady isentropic flow of an ideal gas from a stagnation reservoir (p₀, T₀) through a converging nozzle of throat area A.

Energy: c_p T₀ = c_p T + v²/2 → v = √( 2 c_p T₀ (1 − T/T₀) ) Isentropic: T/T₀ = (p/p₀)^((γ−1)/γ) Mass flow: ṁ = ρ v A = (p/RT) v A Maximising ṁ over p/p₀ gives the critical ratio p*/p₀ = ( 2/(γ+1) )^(γ/(γ−1)) = 0.5283 for γ = 1.4 At that point the throat velocity equals the local speed of sound, and T* = T₀ · 2/(γ+1) v* = a* = √(γ R T*) ṁ_max = ρ* a* A = p* A √( γ / (R T*) ) Substituting p* and T*: ṁ_max = A · p₀ / √T₀ · √(γ/R) · ( 2/(γ+1) )^((γ+1)/(2(γ−1))) For air: √(1.4/287) = 0.069843 (2/2.4)³ = 0.578704 ṁ_max = 0.0404 · A · p₀ / √T₀ [SI]

Now convert to the volume units the industry actually uses. ANR reference (ISO 8778) is 20 °C and 1.000 bar, giving ρ_ANR = 100,000/(287 × 293.15) = 1.1886 kg/m³. With A in mm², p₀ in bar absolute and T₀ = 293.15 K:

ṁ = 0.0404 × (A×1e-6) × (p₀×1e5) / 17.1216 = 2.3596e-4 · A · p₀ kg/s Q = ṁ/ρ_ANR = 1.9852e-4 · A · p₀ m³/s = 0.19852 · A · p₀ dm³/s Q [Nl/min] = 11.91 · S [mm²] · p₁ [bar abs] and since ISO 6358 defines C by Q [dm³/s] = C · p₁ when choked, C = 0.19852 · S → S = 5.04 · C ≈ 5C

Three remarks that matter in practice. First, this module uses 12 rather than 11.91 — a 0.7 % error, which is an order of magnitude smaller than the uncertainty in the discharge coefficient of any real leak or fitting (C_d typically 0.6–0.85), so it is free precision to give away. Second, the temperature dependence is 1/√T, so a hot summer day at 40 °C reduces choked flow by only 1.7 % against 20 °C — negligible, which is why ISO 6358's temperature correction is so rarely applied. Third, and most importantly for economics, the result is exactly linear in p₁ and contains no downstream pressure at all. That is the artificial-demand law, and it is the reason a plant's leaks get bigger the moment somebody turns the setpoint up.

D2 — SPECIFIC POWER, THE PER-BAR LAW, AND THE TWO EFFECTS THAT MULTIPLY CORE
Isentropic work per unit mass: w = c_p T₁ [ (p₂/p₁)^κ − 1 ] κ = (γ−1)/γ Per unit free-air volume, using ρ₁ = p₁/(RT₁) and c_p/R = γ/(γ−1) = 3.5: W/V₁ = ρ₁ w = (p₁/RT₁) · c_p T₁ · f = 3.5 · p₁ · f(p₂) where f(p₂) = (p₂/p₁)^0.2857 − 1 — T₁ has cancelled Dividing by isentropic efficiency and converting to kW per m³/min: k = 3.5 × 101.3 × f / (60 η) = 5.909 · f / η Ekala: f(7.5 bar g) = 0.8371, η = 0.773 → k = 6.40 ✓

Now the part that is usually missed. Total compressor power is k multiplied by flow, and flow itself depends on pressure because every unregulated consumer is a choked orifice obeying D1. Write the unregulated share as u:

This closed form is the whole argument of the module in one line. D3 — THE OPTIMUM TUBE BORE, AND WHY IT EXISTS AT ALL STRETCH

Tube bore acts on stroke time twice, in opposite directions: it raises conductance (good) and it adds dead volume (bad). Both effects are power laws, so there is a minimum. Set it up with the empirical tube law from page 6, C_tube = c d2.5/√L with c = 0.05897.

t ∝ ( V₀ + γ L d² ) · √G where G = 1/C_total² = R + βL/d⁵ V₀ swept volume + dead volume outside the tube [dm³] γ = 7.854e-4 dm³ per (mm²·m) — tube volume coefficient R = 1/C_rest² — everything except the tube β = 1/c² = 287.6 Set dt/dd = 0: 2γLd√G + (V₀+γLd²) · (−5βL/d⁶)/(2√G) = 0 Multiply by 2√G d⁶/L and substitute G: 4γ d⁷ (R + βL/d⁵) = 5β(V₀ + γLd²) 4γR · d⁷ − βγL · d² − 5βV₀ = 0
Ekala clamp: γ=7.854e-4, R=0.2667, L=2.5, V₀=0.3267, β=287.6 8.378e-4 d⁷ − 0.5647 d² − 469.8 = 0 → d_opt = 6.68 mm Numerical check, stroke time against bore: 4.0 mm 0.331 s 6.5 mm 0.2208 s 6.0 mm 0.224 s 7.0 mm 0.2212 s 8.0 mm 0.229 s 10.0 mm 0.257 s The standard 8/6 tube (6 mm bore) is within 1.4 % of the true optimum. The "better" 12/10 tube is 15 % slower.

Two results worth more than the number itself.

  • 1The optimum exists only because the rest of the chain is finite. Set R = 0 — a perfect valve, perfect fittings, perfect port — and t ∝ (V₀+γLd²)/d2.5, which decreases monotonically: bigger tube is always better. The optimum is created by the components you are not changing. That is a general and slightly unsettling property of optimisation in series systems, and it means the “best” tube size is not a property of the tube.
  • 2When V₀ dominates, d_opt ≈ (1.25 β V₀ / (γ R))1/7 — giving 6.63 mm here against the exact 6.68. The seventh root is the practical message: doubling your estimate of V₀ moves the optimum by only 10 %. The minimum is broad and flat, so you do not need good data to land near it — but it is a minimum, and two sizes above it costs real time. The failure mode this protects you from is the well-meaning fitter who upsizes everything.
D4 — THE EXHAUST LIMIT CONTAINS NO PRESSURE CORE

The result that closes the story of page 1, and the one most engineers have never seen written down.

During a stroke at piston velocity v, in free-air terms: supply side must deliver Q_in = A_p · v · p_in exhaust side must expel Q_out = A_a · v · p_back Capacities, from ISO 6358 (the exhaust is choked while p_back > p_atm/b, i.e. above about 2.9 bar absolute for b = 0.35): A_p · v · p_in ≤ C_in · p₁ · Ψ → v ≤ C_in · p₁ · Ψ / (A_p · p_in) A_a · v · p_back ≤ C_out · p_back → v ≤ C_out / A_a ↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑ p_back appears on both sides and cancels.

Why it cancels, in words: raising the pressure on the exhaust side gives the exhaust path more driving pressure — and in exactly the same proportion it makes the air being expelled denser, so there is more of it to move. The two effects are both first-order in p_back and they annihilate. The exhaust-limited velocity is a pure ratio of a conductance to an area.

The same cancellation, seen as a time constant. Blowing a fixed volume V down through a choked restriction C: V dp/dt = −C p → p(t) = p₀ e^(−Ct/V) τ = V / C — independent of the starting pressure. General principle: any process whose demand and whose capacity both scale linearly with the same state variable is independent of that variable. It appears again in receiver blow-down, in vacuum evacuation, and in any orifice-limited discharge.
CONSEQUENCE — THE RANKED LEVERS FOR AN EXHAUST-LIMITED STROKE 1. Quick-exhaust valve at the cylinder port — removes the valve, the tube and the fittings from the exhaust path entirely. Usually 3–6×. 2. Delete a throttle that is controlling nothing — free, and often the largest single term (WE2: 63.6 % of the resistance). 3. Upsize the silencer — cheap, and silencers foul. 4. Upsize the valve — expensive, and usually the smallest term. 5. Raise the supply pressure — changes nothing at all. Most plants do these in exactly the reverse order.
