🔌IO-Link

It is not a fieldbus. It is a private, three-wire conversation between one master port and one device, running on the same cable a dumb sensor used yesterday — and that single design choice explains everything IO-Link is brilliant at, and the three things it is quietly terrible at: cyclic data is cheap, acyclic data is expensive, and nothing in the chain is synchronised with anything else.

A Glaze Line That Lost LKR 0.94 Million a Month on a Network Nobody Suspected

COLD OPEN — HORANA, SANITARYWARE GLAZING CELL, LINE 2 CORE

The line makes glazed sanitaryware on a 45-second takt, two shifts a day: 640 pieces a shift, 1,280 a day, 25 days a month. Four IO-Link masters, eight ports each, twenty-nine devices — photoelectric part detectors, glaze pressure and flow transmitters, a valve-manifold interface, an I/O hub — all reporting into a PROFINET PLC on a 2 ms update with a 10 ms scan. The plant is proud of it. It was sold as “Industry 4.0 ready”, and by every indicator on the master's web page it is perfectly healthy: every port green, every device in OPERATE, no communication errors on any of the twenty-nine links.

It is also giving away about LKR 940,000 every month, and it has just thrown away a further LKR 1.25 million in scrapped pieces over nineteen days. Three separate faults, at three different layers, none of which produces a single red light.

FAULT 1 — THE CHANGEOVER THAT TAKES FIFTY-ONE SECONDS CORE

Line 2 runs nine product changeovers a shift. On each one, the PLC's recipe block writes fourteen parameters into each of twenty-two devices: spray-window teach points, pressure setpoints, filter times, output logic, units. Three hundred and eight parameter writes. Then, because the commissioning engineer was careful, it reads all three hundred and eight back to verify them.

Everyone involved believed this was instant. IO-Link, after all, runs at 230.4 kbit/s. Three hundred and eight small numbers is a few hundred bytes; a few hundred bytes at 230 kbit/s is about ten milliseconds. That is the arithmetic in everybody's head, and every part of it is wrong.

The devices are COM2 (38.4 kbit/s), not COM3 — cheap photoelectrics and pressure sensors usually are. They declare MinCycleTime = 4.8 ms, and the master runs at it. A four-octet parameter write is an ISDU. On the wire it is: request I-Service + Index + Subindex + Length + 4 data + Checksum = 9 octets response I-Service + Checksum = 2 octets total 11 octets But in OPERATE the on-request channel carries ONE octet per cycle, riding alongside the process data. So those 11 octets take 11 cycles: t_wire = 11 × 4.8 ms = 52.8 ms t_hand = PLC acyclic call + master ISDU state machine ≈ 30 ms t_ISDU = 82.8 ms per parameter 308 writes × 2 (write + read-back) = 616 transactions, issued one at a time by a single function-block instance: T = 616 × 82.8 ms = 51.0 seconds of dead line, nine times a shift

459 seconds a shift. At a 45-second takt that is 10.2 pieces a shift, LKR 14,790 a shift, LKR 739,600 a month. And the line is not broken. It is doing exactly what it was told, at exactly the speed the protocol allows.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

The wire runs at 38,400 bit/s and is idle most of the time. Three hundred and eight four-byte parameters is 1,232 bytes — under 10 kbit — which the wire could carry in a quarter of a second. Why does it take fifty-one?

B, and it is the single most useful idea in this module. IO-Link's frame is built around cyclic process data; acyclic parameter data is a trickle squeezed into whatever room is left — commonly one octet per cycle. So the parameter channel runs at 8 × n_OD / T_cycle bit/s — here 1,667 bit/s out of a 38,400 bit/s wire, 4.3 %. C is the trap: COM3 would cut the wire time roughly sixfold, but then the 30 ms handshake dominates and the total only falls from 51 to about 21 seconds — a real improvement, and still the wrong lever. D is a genuine contributor (it is the 30 ms) but not the main term at COM2. A is what the plant tried: they bought faster masters. Page 7 derives the law, and page 14 gets this changeover to 0.99 seconds without buying anything.
FAULT 2 — THE SENSOR THAT REMEMBERED THE WRONG YEAR CORE

The piece-present photoelectric at the centring station drifts as its reflector picks up glaze mist. Over eight months three different technicians pressed its teach button and re-taught it — entirely correct practice, and exactly what IO-Link is supposed to capture. A v1.1 device that has been re-taught locally raises its data-storage upload flag, and a master with Data Storage enabled takes a fresh copy so the plant's backup always matches reality.

This port's Data Storage mode was set to Download only. The engineer who set it was being careful too: he did not want the field changing his commissioned parameters. What he actually built was a master holding a photograph from the day of commissioning, kept scrupulously up to date with nothing.

The sensor failed. A spare went in. The master did precisely what it was configured to do — it downloaded the eight-month-old parameter set — and the switch point moved about nine millimetres. On thin-glaze pieces the sensor no longer saw the edge in time, the spray gun started before the piece was centred, and the plant scrapped 389 pieces over nineteen days at LKR 3,200 of accumulated cost each: LKR 1.245 million. Nothing anywhere reported an error. Data Storage worked flawlessly, in the direction it was told to work.

FAULT 3 — 440 MILLIAMPS THROUGH A 200 MILLIAMP PORT, DOWN 25 METRES OF CABLE CORE

The glaze valve manifold — eight solenoids at 55 mA each — hangs off a Class A port. In normal spraying two or three coils are energised at a time: 165 mA, comfortably inside the port's 200 mA supply. During the clean-in-place purge, all eight fire together: 440 mA. The master's port supply current-limits, the port drops, the device restarts, the master re-runs wake-up and re-downloads parameters, and the purge aborts. Twelve minutes of manual flushing, roughly twice a week. LKR 200,900 a month.

And when the manifold was moved during a layout change, its 20 m drop was extended with a 5 m M12 patch lead. Twenty-five metres of standard sensor cable is about 3.75 nF of line capacitance against the specification's 3 nF ceiling. The manifold interface is a COM3 device, its edges are now slow, and it collects occasional checksum errors that the master silently retries. Sporadic port events, logged nowhere anyone reads.

What IO-Link Is: One Master Port, One Device, One Private Conversation

THE DEFINITION, STATED PRECISELY CORE

IO-Link is a point-to-point, digital, bidirectional, serial communication interface between a single master port and a single device, standardised as IEC 61131-9 under the name SDCI — Single-drop Digital Communication Interface. It runs over the same three unshielded wires, the same M12 connector and the same ≤20 m of ordinary sensor cable that a dumb switching sensor uses, and when it is not communicating it becomes that dumb switching sensor.

Read “single-drop” twice. It is the whole design. There is no bus, no shared medium, no addresses, no arbitration, no collision, no token, no schedule shared with anyone else. Each port owns its wire completely. Twenty-nine devices on four masters is not one network of twenty-nine nodes — it is twenty-nine networks of one node each, plus a master that aggregates them.

The one idea: give every sensor its own private wire and its own private cycle, and put ALL the sharing problems in the master. Everything IO-Link is good at, and everything it is bad at, follows from that sentence. There is no contention — so latency is trivially bounded and the whole apparatus of A04's response-time analysis simply does not apply. And there is no sharing — so scale, cost and aggregation all become the master's problem instead.

WHERE IT CAME FROM, AND WHY IT WON CORE

The IO-Link consortium formed in 2006 around a problem that had defeated every previous attempt: the last three metres. Fieldbuses had reached the terminal box — PROFIBUS-DP, DeviceNet, later PROFINET and EtherCAT — and then stopped, because putting a fieldbus node in a 12 mm photoelectric sensor is absurd on cost, on power, and on connector size. Below the fieldbus, the plant was still 4–20 mA and 24 V switching contacts: no identity, no diagnostics, no parameters, no configuration.

Everything before IO-Link tried to solve this by making the sensor a bus node. IO-Link solved it by making the master the bus node and giving each sensor a dedicated, dumb, cheap, short link. That is why it succeeded where AS-Interface, HART-for-discrete and various proprietary schemes stalled: an IO-Link device needs about the silicon of a UART, a 24 V line driver and a small state machine. It costs a few dollars more than the dumb version, not a few tens.

WHAT IO-LINK IS NOT — SIX HONEST LIMITS CORE

Every one of these has cost a Sri Lankan plant money, and every one is invisible in a vendor presentation.

1 · It is not a fieldbus

You cannot daisy-chain devices, you cannot address them, you cannot put twelve on a trunk. One port, one device, full stop. A “20-device IO-Link network” is 20 ports and at least three masters.

2 · It is not long

Twenty metres, unshielded, and derivation 3 shows the limit is line capacitance, not voltage drop — so “I used thicker cable” buys you nothing at all.

3 · It is not fast for parameters

Cyclic process data is quick. Acyclic parameter data crawls at a few percent of the wire. The cold open is entirely this.

4 · It is not synchronised

The port cycle free-runs against the fieldbus cycle, which free-runs against the PLC scan. Nothing lines up. Page 12 quantifies the jitter you inherit.

5 · It is not safe

Standard IO-Link carries no safety integrity whatsoever. A safety function needs IO-Link Safety (IEC 61139-2) or conventional hard-wiring — see A52.

6 · It is not secure

No authentication, no encryption, no integrity beyond a checksum against noise. Its security is that it is 20 m of cable inside a machine. Treat the master, not the link, as the security boundary.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A plant has 96 IO-Link sensors. A vendor quotes twelve 8-port masters. A consultant says “use one master and a longer cable, it is a network”. Who is right, and what is the deciding fact?

B. One port serves exactly one IO-Link device — there is no sharing mechanism at all, because there is no addressing. A and D describe a bus, which this is not. C is the interesting wrong answer, and worth understanding precisely: an IO-Link hub really does put 16 channels on one port, but they are dumb digital channels reported as bits inside the hub's process data — the hub is the IO-Link device, and the 16 things hanging off it have no identity, no parameters and no diagnostics of their own. Hubs are excellent economics for dumb I/O in the field (page 13) and are not a substitute for ports when you actually want IO-Link devices.

Three Wires, Two Port Classes, and One Line With Two Personalities

THE PHYSICAL LAYER IN ONE PARAGRAPH CORE

An IO-Link link is L+ (24 V), L− (0 V) and C/Q — a single signal wire that carries the whole conversation, half-duplex, by switching between 0 V and 24 V. There is no differential pair, no shield, no termination, no impedance to match. It is deliberately, aggressively ordinary: the cheapest unshielded M12 sensor lead in the store cupboard is a valid IO-Link cable, and that is the entire point of the technology.

Master supply 20 … 30 V DC Device supply 18 … 30 V DC Signalling 24 V pulse modulation on C/Q, NRZ, half-duplex Cable unshielded, standard sensor cable, ≤ 20 m, ≤ 3 nF line capacitance Connector M12 A-coded (or M8) — the same one the dumb sensor used Device start-up must be operational within 300 ms of L+ being applied Port current Class A: ≥ 200 mA specified minimum. Read the master's datasheet for the real figure — it is a design input, not folklore.

Nothing in that block is impressive, and every line of it was chosen so that an existing plant can be upgraded one sensor at a time without touching a single cable. That is a far more valuable property than speed.

THE 20-METRE RULE IS A CAPACITANCE RULE STRETCH

Almost everyone believes the 20 m limit is about voltage drop. Run the numbers and it collapses. Twenty metres of ordinary 0.34 mm² sensor cable, out and back, at the full 200 mA of a Class A port:

R_loop = 2 ρ L / A = 2 × 0.0178 × 20 / 0.34 = 2.09 Ω V_drop = 0.200 A × 2.09 Ω = 0.42 V A device specified down to 18 V, fed from a 24 V master port, has 6 V of headroom. The resistive drop uses 7 % of it. Voltage drop is not the constraint and never was.

The real constraint is charge. The C/Q driver has to slew an unshielded, unterminated cable between 0 V and 24 V, and that cable is a capacitor of roughly 150 pF per metre. At COM3 one bit is 4.34 µs, so the edge has to be finished in a few hundred nanoseconds — and the current needed to do that is I = C dV/dt. Derivation 3 works this through and produces the number 20 m directly out of the 3 nF ceiling.

The practical consequences are sharp, and they are the opposite of what people assume:

  • 1Thicker cable does not buy length. It lowers R, which was never the problem, and usually raises capacitance per metre.
  • 2Two 12 m leads coupled together are 24 m. Capacitance adds. The connector in the middle does nothing to help.
  • 3A slower COM mode really is more tolerant of a long or capacitive cable — the edge has 48 × longer at COM1 — but the specification gives you one cable rule for all three modes, and a device that is COM3 cannot be talked down.
  • 4Shielded cable is worse, not better. A screen raises capacitance to ground substantially. IO-Link is specified unshielded on purpose.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A commissioning engineer needs 28 m from the master to a COM3 pressure transmitter. He uses 1.0 mm² cable “to be safe” and the link works perfectly on the bench and for the first three weeks. What is the most likely thing that happens next?

C, and the “works for three weeks” part is the diagnosis. Excess capacitance does not break a link, it erodes the margin on every edge. The receiver still resolves most bits, the checksum catches the ones it does not, and the master retries — so the fault is invisible until something else spends the remaining margin: a VFD starting nearby, a damp morning, a warm panel. A is what the engineer will say for those three weeks. B is the reflex answer, and the previous card just showed the drop is 0.4 V at 20 m. D is not how it fails — SIO fallback happens when wake-up fails twice at start-up, not when a running link degrades. The honest fix is to move the master, not the cable.

Wake-Up, UART Frames and the M-Sequence: What Actually Travels on C/Q

THE UART FRAME — ELEVEN BITS PER OCTET, ALWAYS CORE

IO-Link's data link is startlingly conventional: it is a UART. Every octet on the wire is sent as 1 start bit + 8 data bits + 1 even-parity bit + 1 stop bit = 11 bits. There is no bit stuffing, no 8b/10b, no scrambling, no manchester coding. If you have ever debugged an RS-232 link you already understand the physical framing.

t_bit = 1 / R t_octet = 11 / R COM1 R = 4,800 bit/s t_bit = 208.33 µs t_octet = 2,291.67 µs COM2 R = 38,400 bit/s t_bit = 26.04 µs t_octet = 286.46 µs COM3 R = 230,400 bit/s t_bit = 4.34 µs t_octet = 47.74 µs COM2 / COM1 = 8.000 exactly COM3 / COM2 = 6.000 exactly

Those two ratios are exact and they never change, whatever the payload — a fact derivation 1 makes precise and which turns out to be surprisingly useful when someone asks “how much faster would COM3 be?” The answer is always six times on the wire, and, as the cold open shows, almost never six times on the job.

A device supports exactly one COM rate. It is not negotiable, not configurable, and not a feature you can buy later. The master discovers it during start-up by trying COM3, then COM2, then COM1. When a datasheet says “IO-Link 1.1” and does not say COM3, assume COM2 — and the cold open is what that assumption costs.

WAKE-UP: HOW A DUMB SENSOR BECOMES A TALKING ONE CORE

At power-up, and after any communication loss, the port is in SIO — C/Q is behaving as an ordinary 24 V switching line. To start a conversation the master sends a wake-up request (WURQ): not a data pattern, but a deliberate current event on C/Q.

WURQ master drives C/Q with a current pulse of ≥ 500 mA for ≈ 80 µs (spec window 75…85 µs). If the line is low it sources; if high it sinks. Device sees either the current, or the enforced low-to-high / high-to-low transition t_REN the device must be ready to receive within 500 µs Then master tries COM3, then COM2, then COM1, until the device answers Retries the master may retry the whole wake-up sequence twice. If it still fails, the device must revert C/Q to SIO and behave as a plain binary sensor — the machine keeps running, silently degraded.

That last line is one of IO-Link's best design decisions and one of its most dangerous. A failed link does not stop the machine; the device becomes a dumb switch and the line carries on. In an upgrade project that is a gift. In a plant where somebody has quietly relied on IO-Link diagnostics, it means the most important failure mode of the technology is a silent, successful downgrade. Configure the master to report a device that falls to SIO, and route it somewhere a human reads. That is not a default.

After wake-up the master reads the Direct Parameter Page to learn what it is talking to — minimum cycle time, M-sequence capability, revision, process-data sizes and identity — then compares that against its configured expectation before allowing the port into OPERATE. That comparison is the whole of Data Storage's safety net, and it is only as strong as what you told the master to expect.

M-SEQUENCE TYPES — AND THE ONE FACT THAT EXPLAINS THE COLD OPEN STRETCH

An M-sequence (message sequence) is one complete master-request/device-response exchange, between 1 and 66 UART octets in total. Its type determines how the room inside it is divided between process data and on-request data. The device declares which types it supports in its M-sequence Capability byte, and the master picks one for each communication state.

FamilyUsed in stateProcess dataOn-request data per cycleWhat it is for

There it is. The same device transfers parameters far faster during start-up than it ever will at run time, because start-up happens in PREOPERATE with a wide on-request field and no process data competing for space, whereas run-time parameter writes happen in OPERATE where the on-request field has shrunk to an octet.

This is not a defect. It is the correct engineering trade for a sensor bus: the thing you do continuously is made cheap, and the thing you do once at commissioning is allowed to be expensive. The defect is a plant that does the once-at-commissioning thing nine times a shift.

⚠ Verify, do not assume

The exact octet counts per M-sequence type are set by the device's declared M-sequence Capability, not by a rule of thumb. Read it from the Direct Parameter Page of the actual device before you build a timing argument on it — and if a supplier cannot tell you, that is itself a specification answer.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A COM3 photoelectric sensor reports a single switching bit. How much of the wire's 230.4 kbit/s does that one bit of useful information actually get?

C. MC + CKT + CKS is three octets that are present every cycle no matter how small the payload, plus the device's process-data octet and the line turnaround — call it six octet-times, 66 bits, to move one bit. That is 1.5 %. This looks appalling and is completely irrelevant, which is the real lesson: on a private point-to-point wire there is nobody else waiting for the bandwidth you are wasting. Efficiency arguments that matter enormously on CAN or Modbus — where every wasted bit delays somebody else's message — carry almost no weight here. Know which arguments transfer between technologies and which do not.

The Cycle-Time Model: One Number That Governs Everything Else

WHERE THE CYCLE TIME COMES FROM CORE

Every port has a cycle time, and almost every performance question about IO-Link — how fresh is my data, how long does a parameter write take, will I catch that pulse, how many devices can this master carry — reduces to it. It is worth being able to compute it from first principles rather than reading it off a screen, because the screen tells you what the port is doing and the arithmetic tells you what it could.

t_octet = 11 / R N_master = 2 (MC + CKT) + PD_out + n_OD ← octets the master sends N_device = 1 (CKS) + PD_in + n_OD ← octets the device sends T_cycle ≈ (N_master + N_device + k) × t_octet where k (≈ 2 octet-times) allows for the device turnaround t_A and the idle gap between messages. This is a first-order model: the true t_A is device- specific and the authoritative number is always the device's own declared MinCycleTime. The model exists to let you predict, sanity-check and argue.

Work the glaze line's pressure transmitter: COM3, two octets of process data in, none out, one on-request octet.

N_master = 2 + 0 + 1 = 3 N_device = 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 k = 2 N_total = 9 octets T_cycle = 9 × 47.74 µs = 429.7 µs ≈ 0.43 ms Same device, same payload, at COM2: 9 × 286.46 µs = 2.578 ms (×6.000) Same device, same payload, at COM1: 9 × 2291.67 µs = 20.63 ms (×8.000)

Those figures land exactly where real devices declare theirs — COM3 sensors typically publish 0.4–0.5 ms, COM2 sensors 2.3–4.8 ms, COM1 devices 10–20 ms. The model is crude and it is right, which is the best kind.

THE MinCycleTime BYTE — READING THE DEVICE'S OWN ANSWER STRETCH

The device publishes its minimum cycle time in a single octet of the Direct Parameter Page, and the master publishes the cycle it has chosen in another. Both use the same coding, and it is worth being able to read it by hand because it appears in traces, in master web pages and in exam questions.

bits 7…6 = time base bits 5…0 = multiplier (0…63) base 00 → t = 0.1 ms × mult range 0.4 … 6.3 ms base 01 → t = 6.4 ms + 0.4 ms × mult range 6.4 … 31.6 ms base 10 → t = 32.0 ms + 1.6 ms × mult range 32.0 … 132.8 ms base 11 → reserved Worked: 0x05 = 00 000101 → base 0.1, mult 5 → 0.5 ms 0x30 = 00 110000 → base 0.1, mult 48 → 4.8 ms (the cold open's sensors) 0x49 = 01 001001 → 6.4 + 0.4×9 → 10.0 ms 0x64 = 01 100100 → 6.4 + 0.4×36 → 20.8 ms 0x9F = 10 011111 → 32 + 1.6×31 → 81.6 ms

Three things follow from the coding that are worth carrying. The resolution is coarse and it gets coarser — 0.1 ms steps below 6.3 ms, then 0.4, then 1.6 — so a computed 429.7 µs is declared as 0.5 ms and the port really runs at 0.5. The floor is 0.4 ms; no IO-Link port is faster than that, ever, whatever a marketing sheet implies. And the ceiling is 132.8 ms, which is a genuinely useful fact when someone proposes an IO-Link device for a very slow, very low-power measurement.

The master must run at or slower than the device's declared minimum. It may deliberately run slower — and on a heavily loaded 8-port master it sometimes must, because the master's own processing is shared across ports. Always read back the actual cycle the master chose; do not assume it took your request. This is precisely the mistake A06 documents on OPC UA subscriptions, in a completely different technology, for exactly the same reason: a requested interval and a revised interval are different numbers, and only one of them is real.

Process Data: The Fast Path, and the Silent Way It Reports the Wrong Number

WHAT PROCESS DATA IS CORE

Process data is the payload that travels in every single cycle, in both directions, whether or not it has changed: up to 32 octets in and 32 octets out. It is a fixed image, exactly like a PROFINET or EtherCAT process image (A02, A03), and it has the same three consequences: the frame length is constant, the bandwidth cost is constant, and nothing anywhere tells you whether a value is new.

The length is declared by the device in the Direct Parameter Page and is not negotiable. A switching sensor declares a single bit; a pressure transmitter declares two octets; a smart camera or an RFID head might declare all thirty-two. Sub-byte devices really do cost sub-byte space in the frame — a one-bit sensor does not waste a whole octet on the wire — which matters not at all for one device and matters a great deal to a 16-channel hub packing sixteen bits into two octets.

⚠ Large process data is not always one cycle

Some M-sequence types move a large process image in a single cycle; others interleave it across several. Where a device interleaves, its effective process-data update is a multiple of the cycle time, and the cycle-time calculator on page 5 will flatter it. Read the device's M-sequence capability. If the datasheet does not state it, ask — and treat the silence as information.

PD VALIDITY — THE FLAG NOBODY READS CORE

The device's status octet (CKS) carries a process-data-invalid flag. The device raises it when it knows its own measurement is not trustworthy: during warm-up, while a teach is in progress, when a sensor is saturated or blinded, during an internal self-test, when a heater has not reached temperature. The master forwards this to the controller as part of the Port Qualifier Information (PQI), sitting alongside the process data in the fieldbus image.

In perhaps four plants out of five, the PLC program ignores it. The process data arrives as a number, the number is used, and a value the device explicitly disclaimed drives a control decision. This is the IO-Link equivalent of the mistake A06 documents on OPC UA — using a value whose StatusCode is not Good — and it costs the same kind of money for the same reason: the protocol went to the trouble of telling you, and the application threw the message away.

The rule, and it is one line of ladder: IF PQI says the port is not in OPERATE, or PD is invalid THEN hold the last good value, flag it, and do not let it close a loop or make a reject decision. Devices tell you when they are lying. Very few programs listen.

THE SCALING TRAP, WORKED STRETCH

IO-Link process data is a raw integer with no units. The meaning — datatype, bit offset, bit length, signedness, gradient, offset and unit code — lives in the device's IODD (page 9). Convert with the IODD and you get engineering units. Convert with a habit and you get a plausible lie.

value = raw × gradient + offset Correct 6382 × 0.001 + 0 = 6.382 bar Habit treat it as a Siemens analogue word: (6382 / 27648) × 10 bar = 2.308 bar Error 4.074 bar — the reading is 63.8 % low, permanently, and the trend on the HMI looks entirely healthy.

  • 1Use an engineering tool that imports the IODD and generates the mapping. Every mainstream master vendor has one. Hand-mapping IO-Link process data in ladder is a decision to own this class of fault forever.
  • 2Verify against physics at commissioning, not against the screen. Vent the transmitter to atmosphere and confirm the tag reads about 1.0 bar absolute or 0 gauge. Sixty seconds, once, per device type.
  • 3Record the gradient, offset and unit in the loop folder next to the tag. When the device is replaced by someone else in three years, that sheet is the only thing standing between the plant and this page.

ISDU and the Narrow Channel: Why a Parameter Write Costs Eighty-Three Milliseconds

THE ON-REQUEST CHANNEL CORE

Everything that is not process data — every parameter, every identity string, every diagnostic register, every event, every Data Storage octet — travels on the on-request data (OD) channel, and the OD channel is a few octets wide inside a frame designed around process data. The mechanism that carries parameters over it is the ISDU: Indexed Service Data Unit.

An ISDU is addressed by Index (0…65535) and Subindex (0…255). Index says which parameter; Subindex says which element of a structured one. Request (write) I-Service | Index | Subindex | Length | data… | Checksum Response (write) I-Service | Checksum (positive) Response (error) I-Service | ErrorCode (2 octets) | Checksum (negative) A four-octet parameter write: 9 octets out, 2 back = 11 octets. Not 4 octets. Not 32 bits. Eleven octets, and they do not get the wire — they get n_OD octets per cycle, alongside the process data.

The ISDU is also flow-controlled and one-at-a-time per port. A device that is busy answers with a busy indication and the master retries; there is no pipelining, no windowing, no outstanding-request count. One ISDU, start to finish, then the next.

THE TWO LAWS STRETCH

Everything on this page comes from two short expressions, both derived properly in derivation 2.

1 — The parameter-bandwidth law B_OD = 8 × n_OD / T_cycle bit/s Read it twice. R does not appear. The rate at which you can move parameters depends on the number of on-request octets per cycle and on the cycle time — and only on the COM rate insofar as it changed the cycle. Two devices with the same cycle time have the SAME parameter bandwidth even if one of them is six times faster on the wire. 2 — The ISDU time law T_ISDU = ( ⌈(5 + d) / n_OD⌉ + ⌈2 / n_OD⌉ ) × T_cycle + H where d = data octets and H is the controller-side handshake: the PLC's acyclic call, the master's ISDU state machine, and the polling the function block does while it waits. H is typically 20…50 ms and it is completely independent of the IO-Link link. Consequence: when cycles × T_cycle < H, the link has stopped mattering and you are timing the PLC. Events: How a Device Raises Its Hand, and Why Nobody Hears It

THE EVENT MECHANISM CORE

A device cannot interrupt the master — the master owns the conversation and the device only ever answers. So IO-Link gives the device one bit: the EVENT flag in the status octet it returns every cycle. Setting it means “I have something to tell you”. The master then collects the event itself over the on-request channel: an event qualifier plus a two-octet event code.

Device sets EVENT in CKS → master reads the event via OD → master decides what to do with it Event qualifier carries three orthogonal things — and all three matter: MODE single shot | appears | disappears TYPE notification | warning | error INSTANCE which part of the device is speaking Appears / disappears is the pair people ignore. An IO-Link event is usually a STATE, not a pulse: the device says “this condition has started” and later “this condition has ended”. A system that logs only appearances accumulates a list of faults that were all cleared hours ago.

The latency of an event is bounded by the cycle plus the on-request transfer — typically a handful of cycles. On a COM3 port that is a couple of milliseconds; on a COM1 port it is the better part of a tenth of a second. Events are diagnostic traffic, and IO-Link is honest that they are not an interrupt.

THE IODD — A DATASHEET A MACHINE CAN READ CORE

The IODD (IO Device Description) is an XML file, published by the manufacturer, that describes one device: its identity, every parameter it has with datatype, range and access rights, the structure of its process data down to bit offsets with gradient, offset and unit, its event codes with human text, its menu structure for engineering tools, translations, and a picture of the device.

It is worth being precise about what the IODD is for, because the analogy people reach for is wrong. A GSDML or an ESI file describes a device to the fieldbus — it tells the network how to configure and exchange data with a node. An IODD describes a device to a human being's tool. The master does not need the IODD to run the link; the port works perfectly without one. What you lose without it is meaning: raw integers instead of pressures, numeric event codes instead of sentences, and hexadecimal indices instead of named parameters.

Where to get one

IODDfinder, the free central repository run by the IO-Link community, plus the manufacturer's own site. Download it at specification time, not at commissioning time — and keep it with the project.

Version it like code

An IODD has a version, and it must match the device's firmware. A mismatched IODD produces parameters that are not there and process data that unpacks wrongly — the page 6 failure, with a file to blame.

Read it before you buy

Open the XML. Does it expose a filter time? A teach command? A temperature? A counter? The datasheet lists features; the IODD lists what you can actually reach from the PLC.

It is not the network

The port runs without it. If somebody says “the IO-Link is down, we do not have the IODD”, that is a tooling problem being described as a network problem.

Data Storage: The Feature That Pays For The Technology, and the Four Ways It Lies

WHAT IT ACTUALLY DOES CORE

Data Storage is the reason IO-Link exists commercially. The master keeps a copy of each device's parameter set. When a device is replaced, the master recognises that the new one is the same kind of device and pushes the plant's parameters into it automatically. A technician with no laptop, no software, no licence and no engineering knowledge unplugs the dead sensor, plugs in the spare, and the machine runs correctly. That is the whole promise, and when it is configured properly it is completely real.

Requires ALL of: • device is v1.1 or later (RevisionID 0x11 — direct parameter 0x04) • device declares DS support • the port has Data Storage enabled, in the right direction • the replacement's VendorID and DeviceID match the stored set • the parameter is marked data-storage-relevant in the IODD (not every parameter is — a serial number obviously is not) Miss any one and the mechanism does nothing, usually without stopping anything.

The device also participates actively. A v1.1 device that has a data-storage-relevant parameter changed locally — someone presses its teach button, someone turns its potentiometer, someone uses its display — raises a data-storage upload flag. A master configured to listen takes a fresh copy, and the plant's backup stays true to the plant. This is the elegant part of the design, and it is the part the glaze line switched off.

THE FOUR WAYS DATA STORAGE QUIETLY DOES THE WRONG THING STRETCH
⚠ 1 · The wrong direction

Cause: the port is set to download only, because “we do not want the field changing our settings”. Effect: the master's copy is frozen at commissioning while the real device drifts away from it through legitimate local teaches. Presents as: a replacement that works, runs, reports no fault, and behaves like the machine did last year. Test: read a DS-relevant parameter from the device and compare it with the master's stored set. Fix: download + upload, and a written rule that field teaching is allowed.

⚠ 2 · The firmware variant

Cause: the manufacturer assigned a new DeviceID to a functionally identical device with newer firmware — same order code, same picture, same datasheet. Effect: the identity gate refuses, the download is skipped, and depending on the validation mode the port either faults or runs on factory defaults. Presents as: “the new one is faulty, the old one worked”. Test: read DeviceID from both. Ninety seconds. Fix: re-commission the port deliberately — clear DS, parameterise, re-enable — and record the two DeviceIDs in the spares list.

⚠ 3 · The device that was never in the plant

Cause: the spare came from a machine builder's kit, or a test rig, pre-parameterised for something else, and the port has DS inactive or validation set to no check. Effect: the port accepts it, the parameters are somebody else's, and nothing anywhere disagrees. Presents as: a process that shifted on a date that matches a maintenance record. Test: compare the device's parameters with the IODD defaults and with the master's set. Fix: validation = type compatible, everywhere, as a standard.

⚠ 4 · The backup that is not a backup

Cause: believing Data Storage is the project backup. Effect: a master is replaced or factory-reset, and every port's stored set goes with it — along with the cycle times, PD mapping, validation modes and fieldbus configuration that DS never held in the first place. Presents as: a plant that cannot be rebuilt after a master failure. Test: ask where the master configuration file is. Fix: export the master project on every change, and store it with the PLC backup.

Notice the shape of all four: Data Storage does not fail loudly, it succeeds at the wrong thing. There is no mechanism in the protocol that can tell you your stored parameter set is stale, because from the master's point of view a stale set and a current set are identical — both are just octets it was told to keep.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A plant sets every port to Download + Upload. Six months later an operator, troubleshooting a jam, holds a sensor's teach button and teaches it on an empty conveyor. What happens?

B, and this is the honest cost of the correct setting. Upload means the master believes the device; the device believes the last person who pressed a button; therefore the master believes the last person who pressed a button. Data Storage propagates good field practice and bad field practice with exactly equal fidelity. Three real defences: set DeviceAccessLocks (index 12) to disable local teaching on ports where field teaching is not part of the job; route the 0x6350 parameter changed event somewhere a human reads (page 8); and export the master's stored sets periodically so “what did this port look like in March” is answerable. THE OPC UA FAST LANE — AND ITS ONE REAL COST STRETCH

Most modern masters can act as an OPC UA server in their own right, exposing each port as an object with the device's identity, parameters, events and process data — and many additionally offer MQTT publishing and a JSON/REST interface. Architecturally this is a genuinely important capability. For a plant trying to do anything under the Industry 4.0 heading (A33), it is often the single cheapest route from field data to a database.

⚠ The cost nobody mentions

Cyclic process data published this way is free — the master already has it. Parameter reads are not. Every parameter an OPC UA client browses or polls becomes an ISDU on that port's one-octet-per-cycle on-request channel, competing directly with the PLC's parameter access and with Data Storage. An analytics tool set to poll twenty parameters per device per minute across forty devices is issuing 800 ISDUs a minute into a channel page 7 showed runs at a few thousand bits per second. Read parameters on change or on demand; put anything you need continuously into the process data instead.

Latency and Jitter: Four Clocks, None of Them Talking to Each Other

THE CASCADE CORE

A measurement leaves the physics and reaches a decision in the PLC through four stages, and not one pair of them is synchronised. The sensor's internal filter runs on its own clock. The IO-Link port cycle runs on the master's. The fieldbus update runs on the fieldbus's. The PLC scan runs on the CPU's. Each stage samples the one before it whenever it happens to come round, which means each stage adds a delay somewhere between zero and its own full period, and you do not get to know which.

A_min = t_filter (everything arrived just in time) A_max = t_filter + T_port + T_fieldbus + T_scan (everything just missed) Jitter J = T_port + T_fieldbus + T_scan — the peak-to-peak uncertainty in the age of every value you use The glaze line, as built: t_filter 1.5 ms T_port 2.3 ms T_fieldbus 2 ms T_scan 10 ms A_min = 1.5 ms A_max = 15.8 ms J = 14.3 ms RMS jitter = √( ΣT²/12 ) = √(109.29/12) = 3.02 ms That last line is why nobody ever finds this fault: the TYPICAL error is 3 ms and the WORST is 14.3 ms, so the system behaves beautifully almost all the time and misbehaves rarely, unrepeatably, and always when you are not watching.

Nothing in this picture lines up, and nothing in IO-Link can make it line up. That is the honest position.

WHY IT CANNOT BE COMPENSATED STRETCH

This is the point where IO-Link differs fundamentally from the deterministic buses in this library, and it is worth stating precisely rather than apologetically.

EtherCAT solves this with distributed clocks: every slave shares one time base, so a fixed transmission delay can be measured once and subtracted forever — A03's line is “delay you subtract, jitter you cannot”. PROFINET IRT solves the fieldbus stage with a scheduled send clock. IO-Link has neither. There is no clock distribution, no timestamping in the base specification, no phase relationship between the port cycle and anything else. The delay is not merely unknown — it is differently unknown on every sample, which means there is no constant to subtract.

The port cycle and the fieldbus cycle also BEAT against each other: beat period = 1 / |1/T_port − 1/T_fieldbus| = 1 / |1/2.3 − 1/2.0| = 15.3 ms Every 15.3 ms the sampling phase walks through a complete cycle. So a fault that depends on phase — a marginal pulse, a race between two signals — appears and disappears on a rhythm that has NOTHING to do with the process, the product, the operator or the time of day. This is why these faults are described as “random”. They are perfectly deterministic and completely unrelated to anything anyone is looking at.

None of this makes IO-Link a bad technology. It makes it a sensor and parameter technology, not a motion technology, and a specification that asks it to be the latter is a specification that has not been read.

The Extended Family: Safety, Wireless, Hubs — and an Honest Word About Security

IO-LINK SAFETY — IEC 61139-2 CORE

Standard IO-Link has no safety integrity whatsoever. Not low, not partial — none. The checksum protects against noise, not against systematic failure, and there is no diagnostic coverage, no black-channel envelope, no redundancy and no defined failure behaviour. A safety function on a standard IO-Link port is not a weak safety function; it is not a safety function.

IO-Link Safety, standardised as IEC 61139-2, adds a safety protocol layer that rides over the ordinary IO-Link link — the same black-channel principle used by PROFIsafe (A02) and FSoE (A03). The link underneath is untrusted and unchanged; the safety layer above it detects corruption, loss, delay, repetition, insertion and misdirection of its own telegrams, and it supports applications up to SIL 3 and PL e.

Its most practically important contribution is OSSDe — a standardised way to connect switching safety devices (light curtains, interlocks, e-stops of the OSSD type) so that a class of device which used to demand bespoke wiring per manufacturer becomes uniform. For a machine builder that is a genuine cost reduction; for a maintenance department it is a genuine reduction in the number of things that must be understood.

⚠ The three sentences to hold onto

A safety function needs an FS-Master and FS-Devices — safety hardware end to end, not a setting. The safety response time is not the IO-Link cycle; it is a computed budget that then feeds the ISO 13855 separation distance, exactly as A02 derives for PROFIsafe and A35 for collaborative robots. And IO-Link Safety is about functional safety, not security — they are different disciplines with different failure models, and conflating them is a serious professional error. Start at A52.

The problem IO-Link Wireless solves is not convenience. It is the rotating table, the moving gantry, the tool changer, the AGV and the quick-change format part — places where a cable is not inconvenient but impossible, and where the alternative is a slip ring or a drag chain that is the machine's most frequent failure.

HUBS — THE BEST ECONOMICS IN THE TECHNOLOGY STRETCH

An IO-Link hub is a single IO-Link device with, typically, sixteen ordinary digital channels. The hub is the device the master talks to; the sixteen sensors hanging off it are dumb, and appear as sixteen bits inside the hub's process data.

Sixteen dumb sensors, conventionally: 16 × individually-wired 24 V inputs back to a cabinet, in a multicore, in trunking, terminated at both ends, on an input card. Sixteen dumb sensors, via a hub: 16 short M12 leads to a hub in the field + ONE IO-Link cable back. What you gain: cable, trunking, terminations, cabinet space, and — the part people forget — the hub reports wire break per channel (event 0x7700), which a plain input card cannot do. What you lose: the 16 channels have no identity, no parameters and no diagnostics of their own. They are bits. The hub is the only device on that port, and everything on page 10 applies to the hub, not to what is plugged into it.

Hubs are also where a Class B port earns its keep a second time: a 16-channel hub driving outputs needs real current, and the isolated auxiliary supply is exactly the mechanism for it.

SECURITY — SAYING THE TRUE THING CORE

IO-Link has no authentication, no encryption, no integrity protection against a deliberate adversary, and no concept of an identity that can be trusted. Its checksums are there to detect noise. Anyone with physical access to the C/Q wire can read everything and write anything.

This is not a defect to be apologised for, and it is not something a firmware update will change. It is the correct engineering trade for a 20 m link inside a machine, made by people who understood that the security boundary belongs somewhere else. State it in exactly those terms, then draw the boundary properly:

  • 1The master is the boundary, not the link. Everything an attacker could want — every port, every parameter, every device — is reachable through the master's fieldbus interface, its web server, its REST API and its OPC UA server. Harden those. Change the default password on every master, on every commissioning, without exception.
  • 2Disable what you do not use. Web server, REST, MQTT, discovery protocols, and any cloud agent. A master's factory state is designed for a bench, not a plant.
  • 3Segment. The master belongs in the machine's cell network, behind whatever separates the plant from the office — the ordinary OT segmentation argument in A33, applied consistently.
  • 4Lock the device where it matters. DeviceAccessLocks (index 12) can disable local teaching and parameter writing. It is a safeguard against accidents and casual interference — not against an attacker — and it is worth using for exactly that.
  • 5Refuse the wrong questions. “Can we encrypt IO-Link?” has one honest answer: no, and the fact that you want to means the boundary is drawn in the wrong place. Move the boundary.
WHERE THE FAMILY SITS RESEARCH

Two adjacent developments are worth watching rather than specifying. Single-pair Ethernet and Ethernet-APL are pushing switched Ethernet further toward the field, and one plausible future has APL taking the process-instrument role while IO-Link keeps the discrete-sensor role — they are complementary rather than competing, because the economics differ by an order of magnitude. And the master as a first-class data source, publishing over OPC UA and MQTT without a PLC in the path, is quietly changing what a “control system” boundary means; the interesting engineering question there is not technical but organisational, namely who owns a data path that no controls engineer has to approve.

Recap, Six Failure Modes, and the Quiz

EIGHT IDEAS WORTH KEEPING CORE
  • 1Point-to-point, not a bus. One port, one device, one private cycle. No addresses, no arbitration, no queueing term — and therefore none of the response-time analysis that governs CAN or Modbus transfers here. Scale is the master's problem, not the wire's.
  • 2T_cycle ≈ (N_master + N_device + k) × 11/R. Nine octets at COM3 is 430 µs. The floor is 0.4 ms, the ceiling 132.8 ms, and the COM ratios are exactly 6 and 8.
  • 3B_OD = 8 n_OD / T_cycle. The parameter channel is a few percent of the wire and the COM rate does not appear in the formula. Two devices with the same cycle have the same parameter bandwidth however fast their wires are.
  • 4T_ISDU = cycles × T_cycle + H. On a fast port the controller handshake H dominates completely — so at COM3 you are timing your own PLC, not the network. Measure H before optimising anything.
  • 5Twenty metres is a capacitance limit, not a voltage-drop limit. 3 nF at ~150 pF/m. Thicker cable buys nothing; screened cable is worse; two leads coupled together add.
  • 6Data Storage is identity plus direction. VendorID and DeviceID are the gate; download-only is the setting that silently freezes your backup at commissioning. Validation “Identical” switches plug-and-play off by definition.
  • 7Four unsynchronised stages. A_max = filter + port + fieldbus + scan, and the slowest one is almost always the PLC scan. IO-Link has no distributed clock, so the delay cannot be subtracted — only bounded.
  • 8The device tells you when it is lying. PQI, process-data validity, events, DeviceStatus and DetailedDeviceStatus all exist, and in most plants all of them are discarded before anyone sees them.
SIX FAILURE MODES — HOW EACH ONE PRESENTS CORE
⚠ The parameter storm

Cause: a recipe block writing every parameter to every device on every changeover, serialised through one function-block instance. Effect: tens of seconds of dead line, several times a shift. Presents as: “slow changeovers” blamed on the mechanics, the operators or the PLC. Test: count the ISDUs and multiply by T_ISDU; compare with a stopwatch. Fix: write only what changes, delete the read-back, parallelise per master.

⚠ Data Storage pointing the wrong way

Cause: the port set to download-only, usually deliberately and for a plausible reason. Effect: the master's copy is frozen at commissioning while the plant drifts. Presents as: a replacement device that works perfectly and behaves like last year. Test: compare a live DS-relevant parameter with the master's stored set. Fix: download + upload, plus a rule about who may teach in the field.

⚠ The silent SIO downgrade

Cause: wake-up failed twice — cable, capacitance, power, or the wrong device — so the device reverted to being a plain 24 V switch. Effect: the machine keeps running with no diagnostics, no parameters and no identity. Presents as: nothing whatsoever, until someone needs the data. Test: read the port's actual state, not its output bit. Fix: repair the cause, and configure the master to report SIO fallback as an alarm.

⚠ The port that is a power supply

Cause: an actuator load on a Class A port, drawing more than the port can give when several coils energise together. Effect: the port current-limits, the device restarts, the master re-parameterises. Presents as: a fault correlated with a machine sequence — a purge, a changeover, a full-open command — and with nothing else. Test: measure the current with everything energised, not at idle. Fix: Class B, or a separately supplied valve connector.

⚠ The believable wrong number

Cause: process data scaled by habit instead of by the IODD's gradient and offset. Effect: a reading that is stable, repeatable, plausible and wrong by tens of percent. Presents as: a process that has always run “a bit low”. Test: vent, block or load the sensor to a known physical value and read the PLC tag. Fix: import the IODD and let the tool generate the mapping; record gradient, offset and unit in the loop folder.

⚠ The pulse nobody caught

Cause: a flag narrower than the slowest sampler in a four-stage unsynchronised chain — usually the PLC scan. Effect: intermittent missed parts, missed rejects, missed counts. Presents as: a “random” fault whose rhythm matches nothing in the process, because it is following the beat between two cycles. Test: compute P(miss) = 1 − W/T for every stage. Fix: latch or count it in the device; failing that, stretch the pulse or move the signal to a fast task. Speeding up IO-Link is last.

QUIZ — SIX QUESTIONS CORE

1. A COM2 device exchanges 4 octets of process data in and 2 out, with one on-request octet. Estimate its cycle time and give the MinCycleTime byte that would encode it.

tap to revealN_master = 2 + 2 + 1 = 5; N_device = 1 + 4 + 1 = 6; plus k = 2 gives 13 octets. At COM2, t_octet = 11/38,400 = 286.46 µs, so T_cycle ≈ 13 × 286.46 = 3.72 ms. Encoding: base 00 with 0.1 ms steps, multiplier = ⌈3.72/0.1⌉ = 38, giving 3.8 ms and the byte 0x26 (38 decimal). Note the device would legitimately declare 3.8 ms even though it can manage 3.72 — the coding resolution rounds up, never down.

2. Two devices both run a 2.0 ms cycle with one on-request octet. One is COM2, the other COM3. Which transfers parameters faster, and by how much?

tap to revealNeither — they are identical. B_OD = 8 n_OD/T_cycle = 8/0.002 = 4,000 bit/s for both. The COM rate appears nowhere in the expression; it influences parameter throughput only through whatever cycle time it made possible. The COM3 device is using 1.7 % of its wire for parameters and the COM2 device 10.4 % of its own — and both move exactly 4,000 bit/s. This is the single most counterintuitive result in the module and the one worth being able to state cold.

3. An engineer proposes a 25 m run to a COM3 device using 0.75 mm² cable “to keep the volt drop down”. Evaluate the proposal properly.

tap to revealThe volt drop was never the constraint. At 200 mA over 25 m of 0.34 mm², R_loop = 2 × 0.0178 × 25/0.34 = 2.62 Ω and the drop is 0.52 V out of roughly 6 V of headroom — and the thicker cable reduces it to about 0.24 V, solving a non-problem. The binding constraint is line capacitance: 25 m at ~150 pF/m is 3.75 nF against a 3 nF ceiling, and heavier cable typically has more capacitance per metre, so the proposal makes the real problem slightly worse. The correct answers are to move the master, or to accept a slower device. Reject 25 m.

4. A port has Data Storage set to Download + Upload and validation set to Identical. A device fails at 03:00 and a technician fits an identical spare from stores. What happens, and why is this the worst of the plausible configurations?

tap to revealThe port refuses the spare. Validation “Identical” checks VendorID, DeviceID and the serial number, and every spare has a different serial number, so the port will not enter OPERATE and the machine stays down until somebody with engineering access changes the setting — at 03:00. It is the worst plausible configuration because the plant has done the hard part correctly (Data Storage is set the right way round, the parameters are there, ready to be written) and then disabled the benefit with an unrelated setting that sounds more rigorous. Correct answer for almost every port: Type compatible. Reserve “Identical” for ports where an unauthorised swap is itself the hazard.

5. A 6 ms flag from an IO-Link hub must be caught. The device filter is 1 ms, the port cycle 2.3 ms, the fieldbus 4 ms and the PLC task 20 ms. Compute the worst-case age and the miss probability, and choose a fix.

tap to revealA_max = 1 + 2.3 + 4 + 20 = 27.3 ms; jitter J = 2.3 + 4 + 20 = 26.3 ms; RMS jitter = √((2.3²+4²+20²)/12) = √(421.29/12) = 5.93 ms. The binding sampler is the 20 ms task, so P(miss) = 1 − 6/20 = 70 %. Fixes in order: (1) count or latch the edge in the hub or sensor if it supports it — a counter cannot miss an edge at any read rate; (2) stretch the pulse to ≥ 60 ms (3× the slowest sampler); (3) move the signal to a 2 ms task. Speeding up the IO-Link port from 2.3 ms to 0.4 ms changes the miss probability by nothing at all, and it is the fix that gets proposed first.

6. Why does making a device COM3 instead of COM2 improve a bulk parameter download by far less than six times — and under what condition would it improve it by nothing?

tap to revealBecause T_ISDU has two terms and only one of them scales: T_ISDU = cycles × T_cycle + H. The COM rate shrinks the first term by six; the controller handshake H — the PLC's acyclic call, the master's ISDU engine, the function block's polling — is untouched. In the cold open, 11 × 4.8 + 30 = 82.8 ms becomes 11 × 0.5 + 30 = 35.5 ms, a factor of 2.33, not 6. It would improve by nothing in the limit where H ⋙ cycles × T_cycle — which is already 85 % true at COM3 — and also in the case where the download happens at start-up in PREOPERATE, where the on-request field is already wide and the transfer was never wire-limited to begin with.

WE1 — THE SPECIFICATION THAT WAS SIX ORDERS OF MAGNITUDE TOO FAST CORE

Situation. A Hatton tea factory is instrumenting forty withering troughs and two dryers. The consultant's specification says “IO-Link transmitters, 1 ms response”. The offered device is COM2, with four octets of process data in (temperature and relative humidity as two 16-bit words), none out, one on-request octet. Is the specification met? What should you do?

N_master = 2 + 0 + 1 = 3 N_device = 1 + 4 + 1 = 6 k = 2 N_total = 11 octets COM2: 11 × 286.46 µs = 3.151 ms → declared 3.2 ms, byte 0x20 COM3: 11 × 47.74 µs = 0.525 ms → declared 0.6 ms, byte 0x06 So the offered device is 3.2 ms — 3.2× the specified figure. The specification IS achievable, but only with a COM3 device, at roughly twice the unit price, forty-two times over. Minimum rate for a 1 ms cycle: R ≥ 11 × 11 / 0.001 s = 121 kbit/s → COM3 is the only conforming option.

And now the part that earns the fee. A withering trough's relative humidity changes over hours. A dryer's exhaust temperature has a thermal time constant measured in minutes. The fastest thing anyone will do with this data is close a slow humidity loop or log a trend. The specified 1 ms is not merely unnecessary — it is roughly five to six orders of magnitude faster than the process it measures.

The right answer is therefore not to buy COM3. It is to write back: “the 1 ms clause does not correspond to any process requirement we can identify; we propose 3.2 ms COM2 devices and recommend the difference in cost be spent on eight additional measurement points, which will materially improve the withering model.” Two more troughs instrumented is worth more than forty-two devices that are 2.6 ms faster than nothing needs.

The transferable lesson. Whenever you compute that a specification is not met, ask the second question before writing the report: does the process actually need the specified figure? About half the time the honest answer changes the recommendation completely, and it is the half where you are most useful.

Here is the whole forensic argument in one line: the observed miscount is 2.1 %, and the model predicts 50 %. The model is not wrong — the input is wrong. If half the bottles were being missed the line would be visibly, catastrophically miscounting, and it is not. Therefore the flags are not 4 ms; most of them must be comfortably wider than 8 ms, and only about 2 % of them fall below it.

Which relocates the fault entirely. The gap between bottles is not constant — it depends on infeed pressure, on the star wheel, on bottle geometry and on how tightly the bottles are packed at that moment. The 2.1 % is the tail of the bottle-spacing distribution, not a property of the sensor. The sensor is behaving exactly as specified, the network is behaving exactly as specified, and the mechanical infeed is the finding.

Four Derivations

WHY DERIVE ANYTHING CORE

Four results have been used throughout this module as though they were given. They are not given — each falls out of one physical or structural fact, and each produces a consequence that is not obvious until the algebra is written down. Two of them produce results that contradict what almost everyone in the industry believes.

DERIVATION 1 — THE CYCLE-TIME LAW, THE EXACT RATIOS, AND THE 72.7 % CEILING STRETCH

The one fact. Every octet on an IO-Link wire is a UART character of exactly eleven bits — one start, eight data, one even parity, one stop — regardless of payload, regardless of COM rate, always.

t_octet = 11 / R M-sequence octet count: N_master = 2 (MC, CKT) + PD_out + n_OD N_device = 1 (CKS) + PD_in + n_OD N = 3 + PD_in + PD_out + 2 n_OD Adding k octet-times for the device turnaround t_A and the inter-message idle: T_cycle = (3 + PD_in + PD_out + 2 n_OD + k) × 11 / R (1) Result A — the ratios are exact and payload-independent. From (1), for the same device at two rates: T(R₁) / T(R₂) = R₂ / R₁ The bracket cancels completely. Therefore, for EVERY device and EVERY payload: COM3 / COM2 = 230.4/38.4 = 6.000 COM2 / COM1 = 38.4/4.8 = 8.000 COM3 / COM1 = 230.4/4.8 = 48.000 These are not approximations and they never change. Which is why “six times faster” is always the right answer to the wire question — and, as derivation 2 shows, almost never the right answer to the JOB question. Result B — the efficiency ceiling. Useful payload bits = 8(PD_in + PD_out). Total bits on the wire = 11N. η = 8(PD_in + PD_out) / [ 11 (3 + PD_in + PD_out + 2 n_OD + k) ] (2) As PD grows without bound the constant terms vanish and η → 8/11 = 72.73 % No IO-Link link can ever exceed 72.7 % payload efficiency, at any rate, with any device, because the UART frame spends three of every eleven bits on start, parity and stop. This is the protocol's hard ceiling. Worked, for a typical sensor (PD_in 2, PD_out 0, n_OD 1, k 2 → N = 9): η = 16 / 99 = 16.2 % Worked, for a one-bit switching sensor (PD_in 1 octet, n_OD 0, k 2 → N = 6): η = 1 bit / 66 bits = 1.52 %

The consequence that matters. That 1.52 % would be scandalous on a shared medium: on CAN or Modbus, every wasted bit is a bit somebody else is waiting for, and efficiency arguments dominate the design. On IO-Link the wire is private and idle the rest of the time, so efficiency is very nearly irrelevant and latency is everything. Knowing which arguments transfer between technologies and which are local to one is a large part of what separates an engineer from a technician.

The 0.4 ms floor is a protocol choice, not a physical wall. The shortest useful M-sequence at COM3 is around five octets — 239 µs — so physics would permit a faster port. The floor comes from the MinCycleTime encoding, which starts at multiplier 4 in the 0.1 ms base. It is a deliberate limit that keeps master implementations tractable, and no configuration defeats it.

DERIVATION 2 — THE PARAMETER-BANDWIDTH LAW AND THE IRREDUCIBLE FLOOR STRETCH

The one fact. The on-request field carries n_OD octets per cycle and only per cycle. There is no second channel, no pipelining, and no way to send parameter data outside the M-sequence.

Result A — the bandwidth law. B_OD = 8 n_OD / T_cycle bit/s (3) R does not appear. Parameter throughput is a function of the CYCLE and the on-request width, and of the COM rate only through whatever cycle it enabled. Two devices with the same cycle move parameters at the same speed even if one of them is six times faster on the wire. Result B — the wire fraction is a pure frame property. Substituting (1) into (3): B_OD / R = 8 n_OD / (T_cycle × R) = 8 n_OD / (11 N) (4) R cancels AGAIN. For the typical sensor (n_OD = 1, N = 9): B_OD / R = 8 / 99 = 8.08 %, at COM1, COM2 and COM3 alike. A remark worth having. The table on page 7 gives 6.94 % for a COM3 device at a 0.5 ms cycle, not 8.08 %. The difference is the ENCODING: the computed cycle is 429.7 µs, the coding rounds it up to 0.5 ms, and the extra 70 µs is pure idle. The MinCycleTime round-up itself costs 14 % of the port's throughput — a cost nobody budgets for and nobody can avoid. Result C — the two-term ISDU law and its break-even. T_ISDU = ( ⌈(5+d)/n_OD⌉ + ⌈2/n_OD⌉ ) × T_cycle + H (5) ⌜______________ cycles ______________⌞ The link term scales with the wire; H — the PLC's acyclic call, the master's ISDU state machine, the function block's polling — does not scale at all. wire dominates ⇔ cycles × T_cycle > H ⇔ T_cycle > H / cycles For the cold open (cycles = 11, H = 30 ms): T_cycle > 2.73 ms Above 2.73 ms, buy a faster link. Below it, go and read the PLC code. Result D — the irreducible floor, and why the cold open could never have been fixed by any network improvement. Take the limit T_cycle → 0 in (5): T_ISDU → H. So for M transactions issued serially, the total can never fall below T_floor = M × H (6) Cold open: M = 616 transactions, H = 30 ms T_floor = 18.48 s Read that again. With an infinitely fast IO-Link link — zero cycle time, infinite baud — the glaze line's changeover would still have taken 18.5 seconds. The plant bought faster masters and could not have reached even a third of the improvement it needed. Only reducing M works: writing 2 parameters instead of 14 and deleting the read-back takes M from 616 to 44, and the floor with it to 1.32 s. Parallelising four ways takes the effective M to 12 and the floor to 0.36 s, against the 0.99 s actually achieved.

The transferable structure. Equation (5) is a link term plus a host term, and the host term is invariant to everything the network vendor sells you. That structure recurs everywhere in industrial data: A01's wire time plus slave turnaround, A06's round-trip time plus per-node service cost, every SCADA poll loop, every database query behind a dashboard. Whenever a job has a per-transaction cost that the link cannot touch, the only lever that always works is doing fewer transactions — and it is invariably the lever nobody is selling.

DERIVATION 3 — TWENTY METRES FALLS OUT OF THREE NANOFARADS STRETCH

The one fact. C/Q is a single-ended, unterminated, unshielded line that must be slewed between 0 V and 24 V, and an unshielded sensor cable presents roughly c′ ≈ 150 pF per metre between conductors.

Step 1 — the resistive constraint, and its dismissal. R_loop = 2 ρ L / A ρ_Cu ≈ 0.0178 Ω·mm²/m V_drop = I R_loop At L = 20 m, A = 0.34 mm², I = 200 mA: R_loop = 2 × 0.0178 × 20 / 0.34 = 2.09 Ω V_drop = 0.42 V out of a 24 → 18 V budget of 6 V = 7 % Resistance is not the constraint. It is not even close to the constraint. Step 2 — the charge constraint. For the receiver to resolve a bit, the edge must complete in a small fraction β of the bit time. Take β = 0.1 as a reasonable engineering target. I_charge = C ΔV / t_rise , t_rise = β t_bit = β / R ⇒ I_charge = c′ L ΔV R / β (7) Evaluate at C = 3 nF, ΔV = 24 V: COM3 t_bit = 4.34 µs t_rise ≤ 434 ns I = 3n × 24 / 434n = 166 mA COM2 t_bit = 26.0 µs t_rise ≤ 2.60 µs I = 27.6 mA COM1 t_bit = 208 µs t_rise ≤ 20.8 µs I = 3.46 mA At COM3 the driver must source a transient of 166 mA — comparable to the entire port supply current — on every single edge, purely to charge the cable. That is the binding physical constraint, and it scales with L, with R, and with nothing else that a cable specification can improve. Step 3 — the source-impedance corollary. Treating the edge as an RC charge, a 10–90 % rise takes 2.2τ: 2.2 R_s C ≤ t_rise ⇒ R_s ≤ 434 ns / (2.2 × 3 nF) = 65.8 Ω So the transceiver's output impedance must be under about 66 Ω — which is exactly the order of magnitude IO-Link line-driver datasheets specify. The specification's cable limit and the silicon's drive capability are the same number seen from two sides. Step 4 — the famous number. L_max = C_max / c′ = 3,000 pF / 150 pF/m = 20 m (8)

Four consequences, all of which contradict common practice. Thicker cable buys nothing — it reduces R, which was never binding, and typically raises c′. Screened cable is worse — a screen adds substantial capacitance to ground, which is why IO-Link is specified unshielded. Coupled leads add — capacitance is a property of conductor length, so a 15 m drop plus a 10 m extension is 25 m, and the connector between them helps not at all. And a lower-capacitance cable really does buy length physically — at 100 pF/m equation (8) gives 30 m — but the specification states 20 m flatly, so anything beyond it is an undocumented risk you personally own.

Honest caveat. 150 pF/m is a typical figure for ordinary unshielded sensor cable, not a universal constant, and β = 0.1 is an engineering choice rather than a specified number. The derivation is here to show which physical quantity binds and to make the 20 m figure predictable, not to license a longer run. Read the cable datasheet; obey the specification.

DERIVATION 4 — THE ASYNCHRONOUS CASCADE COLLAPSES TO ITS SLOWEST STAGE STRETCH

The one fact. Each stage — device filter, IO-Link port, fieldbus, PLC task — samples the one before it on its own crystal, with no phase relationship to any other. The relative phases are therefore uniformly distributed and mutually independent.

Step 1 — the age of a value. Stage i contributes a delay φᵢ ~ U[0, Tᵢ). A = t_filter + Σφᵢ A_min = t_filter A_max = t_filter + ΣTᵢ E[A] = t_filter + ΣTᵢ/2 Peak-to-peak jitter J = ΣTᵢ (9) Step 2 — the RMS jitter, and why the fault is rare. For a uniform distribution, Var(φᵢ) = Tᵢ²/12. The stages are independent, so variances add: σ = √( ΣTᵢ² / 12 ) (10) Note the asymmetry: the PEAK adds linearly in T while the VARIANCE adds quadratically. So one slow stage dominates σ almost completely: σ ≈ T_max / √12 = 0.289 T_max Glaze line (T = 2.3, 2, 10 ms): J = 14.3 ms, σ = 3.02 ms, 0.289×10 = 2.89 ms J / σ = 4.74 — the worst case is nearly five times the typical case. That single ratio is why this class of fault is described as intermittent, random and unreproducible. The system is well-behaved 95 % of the time by construction, and the tail is where the money is lost. Step 3 — the missed-pulse theorem. A pulse of width W presented to a sampler of period T is captured with probability min(1, W/T). Once captured, it is HELD in that stage's image for one full period, so the width presented to the next stage is Tᵢ, not W. For a chain of samplers T₁, T₂, … Tₙ: P(survive) = min(1, W/T₁) × min(1, T₁/T₂) × … × min(1, Tₙ₋₁/Tₙ) The intermediate terms TELESCOPE. Every Tᵢ that is not a running maximum contributes a factor clipped to 1, and the survivors cancel in pairs: P(survive) = min(1, W / T_max) ⇒ P(miss) = max(0, 1 − W / T_max) (11) The entire cascade reduces to its single slowest sampler. Every other stage, however many there are and however slow, contributes exactly nothing to the probability of losing the pulse. Glaze line: W = 3 ms, T_max = 10 ms (the PLC task) ⇒ P(miss) = 70 %, and reducing the IO-Link cycle from 2.3 ms to the 0.4 ms floor changes it by zero, because 2.3 was never the maximum. Step 4 — why none of it can be compensated. A delay can be subtracted only if it is constant — i.e. only if Var = 0. Here Var = ΣTᵢ²/12 > 0 by construction, and it is differently unknown on every sample. There is no constant to subtract. EtherCAT's distributed clocks achieve Var = 0 deliberately, which is precisely what makes A03's rule “delay you subtract, jitter you cannot” work there and fail here. Step 5 — the beat, and why the fault follows no process rhythm. Two free-running periods drift through their whole phase relationship with T_beat = 1 / |1/T_p − 1/T_f| = T_p T_f / |T_p − T_f| (12) T_p = 2.3 ms, T_f = 2.0 ms ⇒ T_beat = 4.6/0.3 = 15.3 ms A phase-dependent fault therefore appears and disappears on a 15.3 ms rhythm that correlates with the product, the operator, the shift and the weather exactly as much as it correlates with anything else: not at all. These faults are perfectly deterministic and completely unrelated to every variable anyone is recording, which is why they are recorded as “random”.

Equation (11) is the most useful thing on this page. It says that in any chain of unsynchronised samplers — sensor, fieldbus, PLC, SCADA, historian, dashboard — only the slowest one determines whether an event is seen at all. Every optimisation applied to any other stage is, for that purpose, exactly worthless. Being able to state and prove that in a design review will save more money than everything else in this module combined.

SEVEN EXAM TRAPS CORE
#The trapWhat is actually true
1“IO-Link runs at 230.4 kbit/s, so a parameter write is instant”Parameters get n_OD octets per cycle. B_OD = 8n_OD/T_cycle, and R does not appear. A few percent of the wire, always
2“Going from COM2 to COM3 makes the download six times faster”Only the wire term scales. T_ISDU = cycles×T + H, and H is untouched — typically 2.3×, and at COM3 the floor M×H makes further link improvement worthless
3“The 20 m limit is voltage drop, so use thicker cable”The drop is 0.42 V of a 6 V budget. The limit is 3 nF of line capacitance; thicker cable usually has more capacitance per metre, and screened cable is worse still
4“Data Storage is on, so replacement is plug-and-play”Only if the direction includes upload, the device is v1.1, VendorID and DeviceID match, and validation is not set to Identical. Any one of the four silently defeats it
5“The IO-Link cycle is 0.5 ms, so the response is 0.5 ms”Four unsynchronised stages: A_max = filter + port + fieldbus + scan. The PLC task is almost always the largest term and it is the one nobody quotes
6“Speed up the IO-Link port to stop missing pulses”P(miss) = 1 − W/T_max. Unless the port is T_max — it usually is not — this changes nothing at all. Latch it in the device instead
7“The port is green and the device is in OPERATE, so the data is good”PQI and the process-data-invalid flag exist precisely to say otherwise, and the great majority of control programs never read either. A green port with a wrongly scaled tag is the most expensive state in this technology

GLOSSARY — TWENTY-EIGHT TERMS CORE
TermMeaning, and why it matters
SDCISingle-drop Digital Communication Interface — IO-Link's name in IEC 61131-9. “Single-drop” is the whole design: one master port, one device
Master / DeviceThe master owns the conversation and always speaks first; the device only ever answers. There is no device-initiated message, which is why events need a flag
Port Class A / BA uses pins 1, 3, 4 with pins 2 and 5 vendor-specific. B adds an isolated auxiliary supply on 2 and 5 for actuator loads, and needs 5-wire cable end to end
C/QPin 4. The single signal wire. “C” for communication, “Q” for the switching output it becomes in SIO
SIOStandard IO — the plain 24 V switching behaviour (IEC 60947-5-2) that C/Q reverts to when communication is not established. The silent downgrade
WURQWake-up request. A current pulse of ≥500 mA for about 80 µs, after which the device must be ready within 500 µs. Two failed attempts and the port falls to SIO
COM1 / COM2 / COM34.8 / 38.4 / 230.4 kbit/s. A device supports exactly one; the master discovers it. Ratios 8.000 and 6.000, exactly
UART frame11 bits per octet: start + 8 data + even parity + stop. The source of the 72.7 % efficiency ceiling
M-sequenceOne master message plus one device response, 1–66 octets. Its type decides how the room is split between process data and on-request data
MC / CKT / CKSMessage control, checksum+type, checksum+status. Three octets of overhead in every cycle, whatever the payload. CKS carries the EVENT and PD-invalid flags
Process data (PD)Up to 32 octets each way, exchanged every cycle whether or not it changed. The fast path
On-request data (OD)The narrow acyclic channel — typically one octet per cycle in OPERATE. Carries parameters, events and Data Storage. The slow path, and the source of most surprises
ISDUIndexed Service Data Unit — the parameter read/write mechanism, addressed by Index and Subindex, segmented over OD, one at a time per port
Direct Parameter PageSixteen directly-addressable octets holding cycle times, M-sequence capability, revision, PD lengths and identity. Read before OPERATE, and before any ISDU works
MinCycleTimeThe device's floor, encoded as a 2-bit time base plus a 6-bit multiplier. Floor 0.4 ms, ceiling 132.8 ms
MasterCycleTimeThe cycle the master actually chose. Different from what you configured more often than anyone expects — read it back
VendorID / DeviceIDTwo plus three octets that constitute the device's type identity. Together they are the gate that governs Data Storage and device validation
RevisionIDThe IO-Link protocol revision. 0x11 = v1.1. Below it there is no Data Storage, ever
IODDIO Device Description — the manufacturer's XML file giving datatypes, bit offsets, gradient, offset, units, parameter names and event texts. Describes the device to your tool, not to the network
IODDfinderThe free central IODD repository. Download at specification time and keep the file with the project
Data StorageThe master's per-port copy of the device's parameter set, restored automatically into a matching replacement. The commercial reason IO-Link exists
DS upload flagRaised by a v1.1 device when a data-storage-relevant parameter is changed locally, so the master can refresh its copy. Ignored entirely if the port is download-only
Device validationA separate port setting: no check / type compatible / identical. “Identical” includes the serial number and therefore disables plug-and-play by definition
PQIPort Qualifier Information — the status byte accompanying process data into the controller, including process-data validity. Read it, or you are using values the device disclaimed
DeviceAccessLocksIndex 12. Disables local teaching and parameter writing at the device. A safeguard against accidents, not against attackers — and the usual cause of ISDU error 0x8023
ApplicationSpecificTagIndex 24. Free text that travels with the device. Your plant tag, written once, readable forever — and almost universally left empty
IO-Link SafetyIEC 61139-2. A black-channel safety layer over the ordinary link, up to SIL 3 / PL e, with OSSDe for switching safety devices. Requires FS-Master and FS-Device hardware
IO-Link WirelessIEC 61139-3. 2.4 GHz frequency-hopping, 5 ms deterministic cycle, up to 5 tracks × 8 devices = 40 per W-Master, packet error rate 10−9
