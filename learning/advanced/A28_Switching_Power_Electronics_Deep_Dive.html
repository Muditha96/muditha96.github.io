The Switching Idea — Small Signal, Big Power

Precise Definition

A power switch is a device whose control terminal commands a much larger load circuit to conduct or block — letting a 5 V logic signal or a 24 V coil control hundreds of volts and amps it could never touch directly.

Every switch — mechanical or solid-state — lives on the same spectrum. As you move left to right you trade galvanic isolation and zero on-resistance for speed and silent, wear-free life:

One spectrum, two worlds: electromechanical (relay, contactor) physically moves metal; solid-state (MOSFET, IGBT) moves only electrons.

The split that decides everything

Electromechanical

A coil moves contacts. Gives true galvanic isolation & near-zero on-resistance, but is slow (ms), audible, and the contacts erode with every switch.

Solid-state (semiconductor)

A field or base current opens a channel. Switches in nanoseconds, silently, for billions of cycles — but always drops some voltage and needs cooling.

Page 2 · Electromechanical I

The Relay — A Coil That Moves a Contact

A relay is an electromagnet that mechanically throws a switch. Energise the coil → magnetic field pulls the armature → contacts change state. De-energise → a spring snaps it back. That physical air gap gives you galvanic isolation: the control side and load side never touch electrically.

COM rests on NC (normally-closed) when de-energised; energise and it swings to NO (normally-open). Contact arrangements are coded as SPST, SPDT, DPDT… (poles × throws).

POLES & THROWS

The contact map

Pole = a separate switch (COM); throw = positions it can connect to. SPDT = 1 common, 2 outputs (NO+NC). DPDT = two of those, switched together.

COIL VS CONTACT

Two ratings, never confuse them

The coil spec (e.g. 24 VDC, 20 mA) is what your PLC drives. The contact spec (e.g. 250 VAC, 10 A) is what the load may be. Sizing the load by the coil rating is a classic beginner error.

The flyback diode — non-negotiable on DC coils

A coil is an inductor. When the PLC output turns off, the collapsing field tries to keep current flowing and spikes to hundreds of volts (V = −L·di/dt), destroying the transistor that drove it. A reverse flyback (freewheeling) diode across the coil gives that current a safe path. SSRs and MOSFET driver stages share the same lesson — see Page 9.

Page 3 · Electromechanical II

Contactors — Relays Built for Motors

A contactor is a heavy-duty relay engineered to switch motor-level power — tens to hundreds of amps, repeatedly, safely. It adds three things a relay lacks: large arc-quenching main contacts, auxiliary contacts for control logic, and a defined utilisation category.

Main (power) contacts

3 poles for a 3-phase motor (see A08 VFD / 3-phase). Big, silver-alloy, arc-rated.

Auxiliary contacts

Small NO/NC contacts for the control circuit — seal-in (latch), interlocks, “running” feedback to the PLC.

Coil + economiser

Larger coil; many use an electronic economiser to cut holding current after pull-in, reducing heat & buzz.

The classic motor starter: contactor switches power, a thermal overload relay trips on sustained over-current, and a Start/Stop + auxiliary “seal-in” contact latches it on.

Utilisation category — IEC 60947 in one line

AC-1 resistive/heating (easy). AC-3 = squirrel-cage motor, normal switching (the everyday motor rating). AC-4 = plugging/jogging, switching locked-rotor current repeatedly — far harsher. A contactor rated 18 A in AC-3 may be only ~7 A in AC-4. Always size by category, not just amps.

Page 4 · The Enemy of Contacts

The Arc — Why Mechanical Switches Wear Out

When contacts open under load, current does not stop instantly — the air ionises and an arc bridges the gap. The arc carries the inductive energy stored in the load, burns the contact surface, and is the single biggest reason electromechanical switches have a finite life.

Energy that must be dissipated when breaking an inductive load: E = ½ · L · I² (joules — all of it lands in the arc) DC is far worse than AC: AC current naturally passes through zero 100/120 times a second, giving the arc a chance to extinguish. DC has no zero crossing — the arc self-sustains, so DC contact ratings are much lower.
AC LOADS

Self-quenching

The natural current zero every half-cycle snuffs the arc. This is why a 10 A relay may be rated 10 A AC but only 3–5 A DC.

DC LOADS

The hard case

No zero crossing → arc persists. Contactors add arc chutes / blow-out magnets to stretch & cool the arc; DC contactors are physically bigger for the same current.

The fix that bridges to solid-state

To dodge the arc entirely, switch at the current zero (zero-cross SSRs) or replace the mechanical switch with a semiconductor. That is exactly why power electronics took over motor control — which is where the rest of this deep dive lives.

Page 5 · Solid-State Core

The MOSFET — A Voltage-Controlled Channel

The MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) is the workhorse solid-state switch. A voltage on the insulated gate builds an electric field that opens a conductive channel between drain and source. The gate draws almost no steady current — it is a tiny capacitor — so it is trivial to hold on.

Raise VGS above the threshold Vth and a conductive channel forms — the switch turns ON. Note the intrinsic body diode: free-wheeling protection in half-bridges, but slow.

RDS(on)

On-resistance. Conduction loss = I²·RDS(on). Lower = cooler — the headline spec for low-voltage MOSFETs.

Qg

Total gate charge. Sets how hard/fast the driver must work; dominates switching speed & drive loss.

VDS(max)

Breakdown voltage. Never exceed it — even briefly. Derate generously for ringing & spikes.

Conduction loss (ON): P_cond = I_D² · R_DS(on) A logic-level FET fully on at V_GS = 10 V might give R_DS(on) = 5 mΩ. At 20 A: P = 20² × 0.005 = 2.0 W — that is what your heatsink must remove.
Page 6 · The Real Cost

Switching Losses — Where the Heat Comes From

A MOSFET is not free. It loses power in two ways, and the balance flips with frequency. Understanding this trade is the heart of power-electronics design — it sets every drive, inverter and SMPS switching frequency.

During the brief transition, voltage and current are both high at once. That V·I overlap, spent every single switching cycle, is the switching loss.

P_total = P_conduction + P_switching = I² · R_DS(on) · D + ½ · V_DS · I_D · (t_on + t_off) · f_sw ↑ flat with frequency ↑ grows directly with switching frequency f_sw Plus gate-drive loss: P_gate = Q_g · V_drive · f_sw (small, but also ∝ f_sw)

Conduction loss

Set by RDS(on) and duty cycle. Independent of frequency — it is the price of being ON.

Switching loss

Paid once per edge, so it scales directly with fsw. Double the frequency → double the switching loss.

The frequency trade-off

High fsw means smaller inductors/transformers and quieter operation (above audible range), but more switching loss → more heat. The designer's job is to find the sweet spot. Faster devices (lower ton/toff, lower Qg) push that sweet spot higher — which is exactly the promise of SiC and GaN.

Page 7 · Driving the Gate

Gate Drive — The Make-or-Break Detail

A microcontroller pin cannot drive a power MOSFET directly. The gate is a capacitor (tens of nF of effective charge Qg); to switch fast you must dump that charge in and out quickly, which needs amps of peak current — far beyond a logic pin. That job belongs to a dedicated gate driver.

  • 1
    Drive the gate hard. Peak gate current Ig = Qg / tswitch. Switching 30 nC in 30 ns needs ~1 A of peak drive — a driver IC, not a GPIO.
  • 2
    Choose Rgate carefully. A series gate resistor slows the edge to tame ringing & EMI — but slower edges raise switching loss. It is the classic loss-vs-EMI dial.
  • 3
    Beware Miller turn-on. A fast dV/dt on the drain couples through CGD and can falsely switch the FET back on. Negative gate bias or a Miller clamp prevents it.
  • 4
    High-side needs help. The high-side FET's source floats, so its gate must be driven above the bus. A bootstrap capacitor or isolated supply provides that floating gate voltage.

Dead-time is mandatory: never let Q1 and Q2 conduct at once or the bus shorts through them (shoot-through) — an instant destructive failure.

Page 8 · Choosing the Device

MOSFET vs IGBT vs SiC / GaN

No single device wins everywhere. The choice is governed mostly by voltage and frequency. Get the regions right and you can size 90% of converters from memory.

WHY IGBT FOR DRIVES

The 400 V+ motor world

A VFD inverter (see A08) switches a ~565 V DC bus at a few kHz. At that voltage a Si MOSFET's RDS(on) would be huge, so the IGBT's flat ~2 V conduction drop wins — which is why almost every industrial drive uses IGBT modules.

WHY SiC IS WINNING

Both worlds at once

SiC MOSFETs block kilovolts and switch fast with no IGBT tail current. They let drives and EV inverters run at higher frequency & temperature, shrinking magnetics & cooling — the reason SiC is displacing IGBT in premium drives and traction.

Quick selection heuristic: Low voltage (<250 V) · any speed → Si MOSFET High voltage · modest speed (≤20 kHz) → IGBT High voltage · high speed / efficiency → SiC MOSFET Low-V · ultra-high frequency · dense → GaN
Page 9 · Keeping It Alive

Protection & Thermal Design

Power devices fail from heat, over-voltage and over-current. Three habits keep them in their Safe Operating Area (SOA):

Snubber

An R-C (or RCD) across the device clamps the voltage spike caused by stray inductance at turn-off — the solid-state cousin of the relay's flyback diode.

Flyback / freewheel diode

Across every inductive load & in every half-bridge — gives inductor current a safe path so it never spikes the switch. (See Page 2.)

Desat / OCP

Gate drivers monitor VCE/VDS; a desaturation event means short-circuit → soft-shutdown in microseconds before the die melts.

The thermal chain — resistors in series, for heat
T_junction = T_ambient + P_loss · ( R_θJC + R_θCS + R_θSA ) junction→case case→sink sink→air Keep T_j well below T_j(max) (often 150–175 °C). Every °C of margin buys life: the Arrhenius rule of thumb — failure rate roughly doubles per ~10 °C.

This is the same thermal-resistance ladder used to size any heatsink. The total losses from Page 6 (P_total) flow through it; your heatsink and thermal interface set RθCS and RθSA.

The Solid-State Relay (SSR) — where the two halves meet

An SSR packages an opto-isolated input driving a TRIAC/MOSFET output: relay-like isolation and simple control, but solid-state silence and life. Zero-cross SSRs switch only at the AC current zero, killing the arc and EMI — the elegant resolution of Page 4's arc problem. The trade: SSRs always dissipate heat (need a heatsink) and can fail shorted, so safety circuits still favour a mechanical contactor for true isolation.

What actually goes wrong on the floor
⚠️ Contactor won't drop out

Main contacts welded from repeated inrush or AC-4 duty. Dangerous — the motor can't be stopped by the contactor. Re-size by utilisation category.

⚠️ MOSFET dies instantly

Usually VDS exceeded by a turn-off spike (no snubber / stray inductance) or shoot-through from missing dead-time.

⚠️ Driver transistor blown

No flyback diode across a DC relay/solenoid coil — the −L·di/dt spike killed it. Add the diode.

⚠️ FET runs too hot

RDS(on) too high or fsw too high for the heatsink. Re-check the P_total thermal chain & SOA.

Real Industrial Example — full circle to Micro 28 & 29

In a conveyor motor starter, a PLC output (a few mA) energises a 24 VDC contactor coil — protected by a flyback diode — whose three main contacts throw 400 V three-phase onto the motor; a thermal overload watches for jams. Right beside it, the VFD that ramps a second motor uses an IGBT half-bridge switching the 565 V DC bus at 8 kHz, each leg fired by a gate driver with dead-time and a desat trip. Quick Check

(a) A 24 VDC relay coil drives an inductive solenoid load. Name the two diodes you'd add and what each protects. (b) An engineer wants to push a 600 V motor inverter from 8 kHz to 40 kHz to shrink the filter. What loss term explodes, and which device family makes that move practical?

▸ Tap to reveal answer
(a) A flyback diode across the coil (protects the PLC/driver transistor from the coil's −L·di/dt turn-off spike), and a flyback diode across the solenoid load for the same reason on the switched side. (b) Switching loss (P_sw = ½·V·I·(t_on+t_off)·f_sw) scales directly with f_sw, so 5× the frequency ≈ 5× the switching loss with IGBTs (made worse by their tail current). SiC MOSFETs — high-voltage capable but fast with no tail current — make the higher frequency thermally practical.
