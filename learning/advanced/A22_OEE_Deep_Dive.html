Page 1 · Foundations

Why OEE Exists — One Number for a Whole Machine

Precise Definition

OEE is the fraction of Planned Production Time that a machine spends making good parts at its ideal rate — a single dimensionless ratio (0–100%) that fuses three independent loss families into one score.

The micro card stated the result. Let us make it rigorous. A machine can look “busy” and still waste most of its value: it can be running but stopped half the shift (availability loss), running but slow (performance loss), or running fast but making scrap (quality loss). Counting any one of these alone misleads. OEE was coined by Seiichi Nakajima as the central metric of Total Productive Maintenance (TPM) precisely so that one number cannot hide a problem behind another.

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality the master identity = (Good Count × Ideal Cycle Time) / Planned Production Time the “simple” equivalent

The two forms are identical — and the second reveals what OEE really measures: the ratio of fully-productive time to the time you planned to produce. Every minute lost to a stop, a slow-down, or a reject is one minute that does not appear in the numerator.

One honest score

Three loss types cannot mask each other — multiplication exposes the weakest.

Comparable

Dimensionless 0–100% lets you compare lines, shifts and plants fairly.

Actionable

It decomposes — a low score points straight at A, P or Q to fix.

Capacity, free

Lifting OEE adds output with no new machine — hidden capacity unlocked.

The engineer's mindset

OEE is never “a percentage on a dashboard.” It is a capacity argument in disguise: a line at 60% OEE running near-flat already contains the equivalent of a second line, waiting to be recovered through loss reduction. The chapters ahead turn “OEE = 84.6%” into “where exactly did the other 15.4% go, and what is it worth?”

Page 2 · The Time Ladder

The Time Buckets — Where Every Minute Goes

OEE is built on a strict accounting of time. You start from all the time in the calendar and subtract, step by step, until only fully-productive time remains. Each subtraction is a loss bucket, and each bucket maps to exactly one OEE factor. Get the buckets right and the rest is arithmetic.

The OEE waterfall. Start from Planned Production Time (calendar minus schedule loss), then peel off Availability, Performance and Quality losses. What survives — Fully Productive Time — over Planned Production Time is the OEE.

The crucial choice is the top of the ladder. OEE conventionally starts at Planned Production Time — it deliberately excludes time you never intended to run (no orders, weekends, planned PM). That keeps OEE a measure of how well you ran when you meant to, not a measure of demand. Move the top bar up to All Time and you get TEEP instead (Page 6).

Planned Production Time (PPT) = All Time − Schedule Loss Run Time = PPT − Availability Loss Net Run Time = Run Time − Performance Loss (= Good+Bad parts × Ideal Cycle Time) Fully Productive Time = Net Run Time − Quality Loss (= Good parts × Ideal Cycle Time)
Page 3 · The Three Factors

Availability, Performance & Quality — Defined Exactly

Each factor is a clean ratio bounded by 0–1, and each isolates one loss family. Knowing the exact definitions stops the most common OEE arguments before they start.

Availability (A) — did it run when it should?
A = Run Time / Planned Production Time captures every unplanned stop & changeover

Availability falls for down events: breakdowns, jams long enough to log, material starvation, and — importantly — changeovers/setup. Whether planned maintenance counts against A is a definition choice: classic Nakajima OEE excludes it from PPT (so PM does not hurt A); some plants include it. Be consistent and state your rule.

Performance (P) — did it run at full speed?
P = (Ideal Cycle Time × Total Count) / Run Time = Actual rate / Ideal rate captures small stops & reduced speed

Performance is the trickiest factor because it depends on a trustworthy Ideal Cycle Time (ICT) — the fastest sustainable cycle the machine was designed for, often the nameplate rate. It captures losses too small to log as downtime: micro-stops under the logging threshold, and running below design speed. P should never exceed 100%; if it does, your ICT is wrong (set too slow) — a classic data error.

Quality (Q) — was the output good?
Q = Good Count / Total Count captures scrap, rework & startup rejects

Quality counts first-pass good parts only — reworked parts are not good for OEE, because the rework consumed capacity. This is why a part that needs touch-up still costs you twice.

Page 4 · The Math Trap

Why the Factors Multiply — and Why It's Brutal

The single most misunderstood thing about OEE is that the three factors multiply, not average. This is not a convention — it is forced by the time ladder. Each factor is the surviving fraction after the previous loss, so they chain like a series of leaky pipes.

Three “good-looking” 90% factors: Average would say: (90 + 90 + 90) / 3 = 90% ✗ wrong Reality multiplies: 0.90 × 0.90 × 0.90 = 72.9% ✓ correct

The averaging instinct hides a fifth of your capacity. Because losses compound, OEE is always lower than your best-looking factor — and the gap widens fast. This is also why chasing the lowest factor pays most: lifting a 70% factor to 80% multiplies the whole score by 80/70 ≈ +14%, while polishing a 98% factor barely moves it.

Series pipes. The throughput surviving all three stages is the product 0.90×0.90×0.90 = 72.9%, never the average 90%. OEE is a chain — its weakest link governs.
💡 The leverage rule

Differentiating OEE = A·P·Q shows the marginal gain from any factor is proportional to the product of the other two. Translation: improve your lowest factor first — it has the most headroom and the steepest payoff. This is why a Pareto of losses (Page 10) almost always beats a blanket “run faster” order.

Page 5 · The Losses

The Six Big Losses — Mapped to A · P · Q

Nakajima's framework names six big losses, two feeding each factor. They are the vocabulary of every improvement event — name the loss, find its cause, and you know which factor will rise.

FactorLossWhat it isTypical cause & cure
A1 · BreakdownsUnplanned stops logged as downtime.Failure, jam, tool break → TPM, autonomous maintenance, CBM.
A2 · Setup & ChangeoverTime lost switching product/recipe.Long setups → SMED (single-minute exchange of die).
P3 · Minor StopsBrief stops & idling < logging threshold.Misfeeds, sensor blocks → poka-yoke, line balancing.
P4 · Reduced SpeedRunning below ideal cycle rate.Wear, conservative settings → restore design speed.
Q5 · Startup RejectsScrap while warming up / dialing in.Slow stabilisation → recipe control, quick-to-quality start.
Q6 · Production RejectsScrap & rework at steady state.Process drift → SPC, RCA, mistake-proofing.
Six losses, three factors. Every minute of waste belongs to exactly one box — which is how an OEE drop tells you precisely where to look.
Page 6 · The Family

OEE vs OOE vs TEEP — Choosing the Denominator

OEE has cousins that differ only in where the time ladder starts (the denominator). Confusing them is the most common way two reports “disagree.” The factors A·P·Q are the same; the question is what time you measure against.

MetricDenominator (time base)Question it answersTypical use
OEEPlanned Production TimeHow well did we run when we meant to?Equipment & shift improvement (default).
OOEOperating Time (incl. some schedule loss)How well did we use the time we staffed?Operations-level view.
TEEPAll Time (24 × 7 = 168 h/week)What's our total capacity utilisation?Capex/“buy a new machine?” decisions.
Utilisation—Schedule loss only (PPT ÷ All Time)Demand & staffing, not equipment health.
TEEP = Utilisation × OEE = (Planned Production Time / All Time) × (A × P × Q) total effective equipment performance

Worked contrast: a line at OEE 85% but only scheduled 5 days × 1 shift has Utilisation ≈ 30%, so TEEP ≈ 0.30 × 0.85 = 25%. OEE says “the equipment runs beautifully”; TEEP says “you own a machine that sits idle 75% of the week.” Use OEE to improve the process, TEEP to decide whether you even need more iron.

Page 7 · Measurement

Measuring OEE for Real — From Sensor to SCADA

An OEE number is only as good as its data. Three streams must be captured automatically and reconciled: state (running/down + reason), count (parts produced), and reject (good vs bad). This is exactly where the automation stack you already know — sensors, PLC, SCADA/MES — earns its keep.

The OEE data pipeline. Machine sensors → PLC tags (state + count) → SCADA/MES which adds operator-entered or rule-based reason codes. The dashboard is the easy part; trustworthy reason codes are the hard part.

Two engineering truths. First, downtime reason codes are where OEE lives or dies: an automatic stop detector without a reason is just a number; with a reason it becomes a Pareto chart that points at the fix. Second, the Ideal Cycle Time must be governed — locked, audited, and tied to the nameplate — or Performance becomes fiction (see Page 9). The micro card noted “SCADA & MES calculate OEE automatically” (links A14 SCADA/HMI); the catch is that they automate the arithmetic, not the truth of the inputs.

Page 8 · Benchmarks

Benchmarks, World-Class & Sane Targets

The famous “world-class 85%” is not arbitrary — it is the product of three world-class factors, and it is a discrete-manufacturing yardstick, not a universal law.

World-class OEE = A 90% × P 95% × Q 99.9% ≈ 85% Typical (unmonitored) plant ≈ 60% huge hidden capacity Below ~40% → urgent, systemic problem
90%

Availability

World-class — few breakdowns, fast SMED changeovers.

95%

Performance

World-class — runs near design speed, few micro-stops.

99.9%

Quality

World-class — first-pass yield essentially perfect.

Context matters more than the trophy number. A high-mix job shop with constant changeovers will never see 85% and shouldn't be judged against it; a continuous process plant may demand 95%+. The right benchmark is your own trend and a realistic, segment-specific target. Three honest rules:

  • 1
    Compare like with like. Same product family, same definition of PPT and ICT. Cross-plant OEE “league tables” mislead unless the rules are identical.
  • 2
    Trend beats absolute. A line moving 58 → 64 → 71% is winning; a static 80% may be coasting. Watch the slope.
  • 3
    Target the bottleneck. Only the constraint's OEE caps throughput (Goldratt). Lifting a non-bottleneck's OEE often just builds inventory.
Page 9 · Pitfalls

Pitfalls & How OEE Gets Gamed

OEE is a powerful lens and a tempting target. When a number becomes a goal it tends to get manipulated (Goodhart's law). Knowing the failure modes lets you read a suspiciously good dashboard like a forensic report.

🐢 Sandbagged Ideal Cycle Time

Set ICT slower than reality and Performance magically hits ~100%. The classic fudge. Cure: lock ICT to nameplate, audit, flag P > 100% as an error.

🕳️ Hidden / unlogged micro-stops

Stops below the logging threshold vanish from Availability and quietly sink Performance instead — or disappear entirely. Cure: tight stop-detection, low threshold.

📅 Shrinking the denominator

Move losses into “planned” schedule loss (out of PPT) and OEE rises without any real improvement. Cure: a strict, audited PPT definition.

♻️ Counting rework as good

Inflates Quality and ignores the capacity the rework burned. Cure: Q = first-pass good only.

📈 Optimising a non-bottleneck

High OEE on a non-constraint machine just over-produces WIP — a local win, a system loss. Cure: focus on the constraint.

🎯 OEE as a stick

Used to punish operators, it breeds under-reporting and fear. Cure: OEE is a diagnostic for the team, not a scoreboard against them.

🔧 The integrity test

A trustworthy OEE system has three properties: a governed ICT, complete reason-coded downtime, and first-pass-only quality. If any one is loose, the headline number is decoration. Check those three before you ever act on an OEE trend.

Improvement loop — the 5 steps
  • 1
    Measure honestly: governed ICT, reason-coded stops, first-pass quality. No trust, no traction.
  • 2
    Decompose: split OEE into A, P, Q — find the lowest factor (the headroom).
  • 3
    Pareto the losses: rank the six big losses by lost minutes; attack the tallest bar first.
  • 4
    Match the tool: SMED for changeovers, TPM/CBM for breakdowns, SPC/RCA for rejects, line-balancing for micro-stops.
  • 5
    Sustain: standardise the gain, re-baseline, and watch the trend — then return to the new weakest factor.
Quick Check 1

Q: A line ran 7 h of an 8 h planned shift, produced 90% of target output, and scrapped 2%. What is the OEE?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: A = 7/8 = 87.5%. P = 90%. Q = 98%. OEE = 0.875 × 0.90 × 0.98 = 77.2%. Decent, ~8 points below world-class. The biggest single bite was the lost hour (Availability).
Quick Check 2

Q: Why do the three factors multiply instead of averaging — and what does that imply for where to improve?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Each factor is the surviving fraction after the previous loss, so they chain in series (leaky pipes). Three 90%s give 0.9³ = 72.9%, not 90%. Implication: improve the lowest factor first — its marginal payoff (∝ product of the other two) is highest.
Quick Check 3

Q: A machine shows OEE 88% but is scheduled only 40 h of a 168 h week. Roughly what is TEEP, and what does the gap mean?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Utilisation = 40/168 ≈ 24%. TEEP = 0.24 × 0.88 ≈ 21%. The equipment runs beautifully when used, but sits idle ~76% of the week. Before buying a second machine, fill the schedule — the capacity is already paid for.
Quick Check 4

Q: Your Performance factor reads 104%. What is almost certainly wrong, and why does it matter?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: P can't physically exceed 100% — the Ideal Cycle Time is set too slow (sandbagged). It matters because it inflates OEE and hides real speed loss; every Performance gain you “see” is fiction until ICT is corrected to the true nameplate rate.
