Page 1 · Foundations

Why 5S Exists — Order as a Production Tool, Not Housekeeping

Precise Definition

5S is a structured method that builds and sustains an organised, clean, visually controlled workplace so that abnormality becomes instantly visible and waste in motion, searching, waiting and defects is eliminated at its source.

The micro card called 5S "a 5-step method to create a clean, organised, efficient workplace." That is true but undersells it. 5S is not tidying — it is the deliberate engineering of a workplace where the normal state and the abnormal state look different. A missing tool, a leaking seal, an out-of-place part, an over-stocked bin: in a true 5S area, every one of these announces itself in a glance. That visibility is the real product. Tidiness is just the by-product.

5S originated within the Toyota Production System and was codified from the Japanese words Seiri, Seiton, Seiso, Seiketsu, Shitsuke. It is the floor on which the rest of Lean stands: you cannot run TPM autonomous maintenance on a filthy machine, cannot trust an OEE number from a chaotic line, and cannot do reliable problem-solving where the baseline keeps drifting. 5S first — everything else after.

The engineer's mindset

A disorganised workplace is not just unpleasant — it is a continuous, invisible tax on every shift. A technician hunting for a tool 18 minutes a day, an operator stepping around clutter, a part picked from the wrong bin: none of these trip an alarm, yet together they consume hours and breed defects. 5S converts that invisible loss into a visible, fixable problem. The chapters ahead turn "a place for everything" into the visual-management theory, the discipline of each S, and the audit math that proves it.

Page 2 · The Theory

The Visual Workplace — the Science Beneath the Five Steps

5S is one application of a deeper idea: the visual workplace, where information needed to act correctly is built into the environment itself — no asking, no manuals, no memory required. The deciding question for any workplace: can a stranger tell what is normal, what is abnormal, and what to do — in one glance?

The visual-workplace ladder. 5S climbs an area from "ask a person" up to "the workplace explains itself and physically prevents the error." The top rung borders on poka-yoke (mistake-proofing).
LevelWhat it doesShop-floor example
ChaosNo information in the environment; rely on people's memory."Where's the torque wrench?" — ask around.
Visual orderEvery item has a labelled, marked home.Shadow board outline; floor tape for the pallet zone.
Visual displayStandards & status are shown where the work happens.Min/max bin levels; "cleaned by/at" board; andon.
Visual controlThe environment guides or forces correct action.Kanban that won't let you over-produce; colour-keyed fittings that only mate correctly.
💡 Why "make it visible" beats "tell people to be careful"

Human attention is finite and shift-fatigued; instructions decay, memory fails. A visual control moves the burden from the worker's mind to the workplace's design — the difference between a sign that says "return tools" and a shadow board where a missing tool leaves an obvious empty silhouette. 5S is the disciplined, repeatable way to build that environment, area by area. This is the same logic that makes TPM's "cleaning is inspection" so powerful.

🗑️ 1S · SORT (Seiri)

Separate the needed from the unneeded and remove everything that isn't required to do today's work. Red-tag doubtful items and quarantine them before disposal.

Example: A red-tag sweep of a cell clears 3 obsolete fixtures and 40 kg of scrap, freeing a whole workbench.

A useful mnemonic for the English speaker: Sort, Set, Shine, Standardise, Sustain — each word maps one-to-one onto a Japanese pillar. The first three S's are physical actions done once to transform an area; the last two are the systems that hold the gain. Most failed 5S programmes nail S1–S3 and skip S4–S5 — which is why the area reverts within weeks. We give each its own page.

Page 4 · Pillar 1 in Depth

Sort (Seiri) — Red Tags and the Economics of "Just in Case"

Sort is the most resisted S, because it forces a confrontation with the words "but we might need it." It rarely is needed — and the cost of keeping it is real: floor space, search time, obscured abnormalities, and the risk of using an obsolete part. The discipline is a red-tag system: a structured way to remove doubt without throwing away anything valuable.

The red-tag flow. Nothing is thrown away on impulse — doubtful items go to a holding area with a dated tag; if unclaimed within the review window, they leave. This removes the "just in case" fear that stalls Sort.
Frequency of useWhere it belongs
Every hour / shiftAt the point of use, within arm's reach.
Daily / weeklyIn the cell, on a shadow board or labelled shelf.
MonthlyCentral area store, signed out.
Rarely / neverOff-site store, sold, or scrapped.
🏷️ What goes on a red tag

A good red tag records: item name & quantity, reason for tagging, the date tagged, who tagged it, and the disposal decision & date. Open-vs-closed red-tag counts become a live KPI of the Sort effort — the same visual-management logic as TPM's blue/red maintenance tags. Sort is about decisions, not bins: the value is the disciplined judgement on every item, captured visibly.

Page 5 · Pillar 2 in Depth

Set in Order (Seiton) — Shadow Boards and the 30-Second Rule

Set in order gives every retained item a defined, labelled, obvious home — chosen by frequency and ergonomics, not by habit. The acid test is the 30-second rule: any item can be found, used and returned within 30 seconds, by anyone, without asking. This is where motion and searching waste are designed out.

A shadow board: each tool has a painted silhouette home. The genius is the absence — a missing tool leaves an unmistakable empty outline, so it gets noticed and returned (or found before it ends up inside a machine).

Frequency placement

Most-used items closest; reach & bend minimised. Ergonomics, not habit.

Point-of-use storage

Store items where they're used, not in a distant central cupboard.

Visual addressing

Labels, colour-coding, floor tape & signs name every location.

FIFO & flow

Layout enforces first-in-first-out and smooth material movement.

🎨 The grammar of floor marking

A mature Set-in-order area uses a consistent colour code so the floor itself communicates: e.g. one colour for walkways, another for work-in-progress, another for finished goods, red/yellow for hazards and fire equipment. Consistency is everything — an ad-hoc colour scheme is just decoration. Combined with shadow boards and min/max bins, this turns the whole area into a visual display (Page 2). Set in order directly removes the "motion" and "waiting" wastes that drag down OEE Performance.

Page 6 · Pillars 3 & 4

Shine (Seiso) & Standardise (Seiketsu)

Shine is the pillar most misread as "mopping." Its true purpose is inspection through cleaning: when an operator wipes down a machine daily, their hands and eyes pass over every surface and they catch the leak, crack, loose bolt or hot bearing before it becomes a breakdown. This is the exact hinge where 5S becomes TPM autonomous maintenance — AM Step 1, "initial clean," is Shine applied to equipment.

Cleaning = Inspection the central insight of Seiso Clean → see abnormality → tag → fix at source Attack the source of dirt, don't just wipe the symptom (eliminate, don't endure)

A key discipline of Shine is to attack the source, not endure the mess: if a machine leaks oil, the 5S answer is to fix the leak, not to mop faster. This is also where Shine connects to Safety — an oil leak is both a slip hazard and a fire risk, so eliminating it serves Shine, Safety and reliability at once.

Standardise (Seiketsu) is the bridge from "we did a 5S blitz" to "this is just how we work." It makes the first three S's a repeatable routine: written cleaning standards, daily checklists, photos of the ideal state, defined responsibilities, and a schedule. Without S4, S1–S3 are a one-off event that decays. With it, the new normal is documented and assignable.

📸 The "ideal state" photo board

The single most effective Standardise tool is a photo of the area in its perfect 5S state, posted at the area. Anyone — including a new hire — can compare reality against the photo in seconds and restore the standard. It converts an abstract rule into a visual control (Page 2's top rung). Pair it with a short daily checklist and a name against each task, and the standard sustains itself far more easily.

Page 7 · The Hard One

Sustain (Shitsuke) & the Safety 6th S

Sustain is where most 5S programmes die. Sorting, Setting and Shining are visible, satisfying, one-time wins. Sustain is the unglamorous, never-ending discipline of making the first four S's the default behaviour — through audits, leadership presence, training, and accountability. It is culture, not cleaning, and it is the only S that has no "done."

The two destinies of every 5S effort. The same peak; only Sustain decides whether the area holds and keeps improving — or slides back to chaos within weeks. Audits, leader walks and accountability are the difference.
  • 1
    Regular layered audits. Operators, supervisors and managers each audit on a cadence — scored, trended, visible.
  • 2
    Leadership on the floor. When leaders walk the area and ask about 5S, it signals it matters. Delegated-and-forgotten 5S dies.
  • 3
    Onboard every new hire into the standard. The standard only survives if each new person is trained into it from day one.
  • 4
    Make it part of the job, not extra. Ring-fence daily 5S time; recognise good practice. Under production pressure, "when you get a chance" means never.
🦺 The 6th S — Safety (and sometimes a 7th, Security)

Many Western programmes add Safety as a 6th S, making it "6S." Purists note that safety should be woven through all five S's — a sorted, set, shined, standardised, sustained area is inherently safer (no trip hazards, no leaks, clear egress, guards in place). Adding it as an explicit S simply guarantees it is never an afterthought. Some plants add a 7th S, Security (of data, tools or access). The exam point: 6S = 5S + an explicit Safety pillar; the underlying philosophy is unchanged.

Page 8 · The Math

Audit Scoring — Turning "Tidy" Into a Number

5S is a culture, but it is governed by a hard score. A 5S audit rates each S against defined criteria — typically 5 questions per S, each scored 0–5 — giving a per-S and total percentage you can trend over time. The number turns a subjective "looks tidy" into an objective, comparable, improvable KPI.

Per-S score = Σ(question points) / max points e.g. 5 questions × 5 = /25 5S total % = (sum of all 5 S scores / 125) × 100 5 S's × 25 = 125 max Audit cadence: operator (daily) · supervisor (weekly) · manager (monthly)

A worked example: an area scores Sort 22, Set 20, Shine 18, Standardise 15, Sustain 10 out of 25 each. Total = 85/125 = 68%. The shape of the result is more telling than the total: a high Sort/Set but low Sustain is the classic signature of a programme that did the blitz but never built the discipline — exactly the decay curve on Page 7.

Score bandMaturityWhat you'd see
< 40%Reactive / chaoticNo homes, clutter, dirt hides defects. Start here.
40–60%Sorted & setVisible order, but cleaning & standards inconsistent.
60–80%StandardisedRoutines exist; audits running; some drift remains.
> 80%Sustained / world-classSelf-correcting culture; rising trend; visual controls everywhere.
🧮 Watch the trend, not the snapshot

A single audit score is noisy; the trend line is the real signal. A flat or rising trend with a strong Sustain sub-score is the fingerprint of a real programme. Beware Goodhart's law (the same trap as OEE gaming): if the audit becomes a box-ticking ritual, teams optimise the score, not the workplace. Keep the audit honest by rotating auditors and linking the score back to real outcomes — search time, defects, safety incidents.

Page 9 · Pitfalls

Why 5S Programmes Fail

5S failure modes are remarkably consistent across industries — and almost all are about discipline and intent, not technique. Spot these early.

🧽 "5S = a big cleanup"

Treated as a one-time spring clean, not a system. Looks great for a fortnight, then reverts. Cure: build S4 Standardise & S5 Sustain from day one.

📸 Photo-op 5S

Done for an audit visit or a poster, not for the work. Binders thicken, floor doesn't change. Cure: link 5S to real metrics — search time, defects, safety.

🚫 Skipping Sort

"Setting in order" a cell still full of junk just organises waste. Cure: red-tag and remove first — sequence is non-negotiable.

👔 Imposed top-down

Management dictates the layout; operators have no ownership and quietly revert. Cure: the people who do the work decide the homes.

⏱️ No protected time

"Do 5S when you get a chance" under production pressure = never. Cure: ring-fenced daily 5S time, on the schedule.

😶 No leadership presence

Leaders never walk the area or ask about it, so the floor learns it doesn't matter. Cure: layered audits & visible leader engagement.

🔧 The integrity test

A real 5S area has three fingerprints: a stranger can find/use/return any item in 30 seconds, abnormality (a missing tool, a leak) is obvious at a glance, and a rising audit trend with a healthy Sustain sub-score. Missing any one means you have a tidy area today, not a 5S system that will still be tidy next quarter.

Quick Check 1

Q: Why is the order of the five S's non-negotiable — why can't you start with Set in order?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Each S rests on the one before. If you "Set in order" before you Sort, you simply give junk a tidy home — organising waste. You can't Shine a cluttered area, can't Standardise an inconsistent one, and can't Sustain what was never standardised. Sort → Set → Shine are physical actions; Standardise → Sustain are the systems that hold them.
Quick Check 2

Q: An area scores Sort 24, Set 22, Shine 20, Standardise 9, Sustain 5 (out of 25 each). Find the total % and diagnose the programme.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Total = 24+22+20+9+5 = 80/125 = 64%. The shape — strong S1–S3, weak S4–S5 — is the classic "we did the blitz but never built the discipline" signature. Without Standardise and Sustain the area is on the decay curve and will revert within weeks. Fix the systems, not the cleanup.
Quick Check 3

Q: How does the Shine (Seiso) pillar connect 5S directly to TPM (A23)?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Shine's real purpose is "cleaning is inspection" — wiping a machine forces hands and eyes over every surface, catching leaks, cracks, looseness and heat early (point P on the P-F curve). That is exactly TPM's Autonomous Maintenance Step 1, "initial clean." 5S Shine on equipment is the entry point to TPM AM — which is why you can't run TPM on a machine that hasn't been 5S'd.
Quick Check 4

Q: What is the "30-second rule" and which waste does it target?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: The 30-second rule is the acid test of Set in order (Seiton): any item can be found, used and returned within 30 seconds, by anyone, without asking. It targets the wastes of motion and searching (and the waiting they cause) — exactly the losses that drag down OEE Performance. Shadow boards, point-of-use storage and visual addressing are how you achieve it.
