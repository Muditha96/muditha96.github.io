What Six Sigma Really Is

Not a slogan about "3.4 defects" — a disciplined, statistical war on variation.

Definition

Six Sigma is a data-driven methodology to reduce process variation until defects become statistically rare — so rare that, even allowing for normal drift, the process produces no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities.

The name is literal. Sigma (σ) is the statistician's symbol for standard deviation — a measure of spread. A "six sigma" process is one where the nearest specification limit is six standard deviations away from the process mean. The wider that gap (in σ units), the more variation the process can absorb before it makes a bad part.

Born at Motorola in 1986 (Bill Smith & Mikel Harry) to fight pager defects, it was scaled across industry by General Electric under Jack Welch in the late 1990s, who tied it to executive bonuses and reported billions in savings. Today it is a global standard (ISO 13053) and the statistical half of Lean Six Sigma.

Lean removes Waste · Six Sigma removes Variation
Two complementary engines. Lean attacks time and waste; Six Sigma attacks scatter. A fast process that's inconsistent still ships defects; a consistent process that's slow still loses money. You need both.

Why an engineer should care: most "quality problems" aren't a wrong setting — they're variation around the right setting. A shaft diameter isn't 19.98 mm every time; it's a distribution. Six Sigma gives you the maths to quantify that distribution, prove what drives it, shrink it, and then lock it down — so the customer never sees the tail of the curve.

Variation & the Normal Curve

Everything in Six Sigma rests on one idea: a process output is a distribution, not a number.

Mean, Spread & the Bell Curve

Measure a dimension on 1000 parts and plot how often each value occurs. For most stable processes you get the famous normal (Gaussian) distribution: a symmetric bell centred on the mean μ, with width set by the standard deviation σ. Two numbers describe the whole curve — where it sits (μ) and how wide it is (σ).

The "empirical rule" (68–95–99.7): for a normal curve, ~68% of output sits within ±1σ, ~95% within ±2σ, ~99.7% within ±3σ. The few parts in the far tails are where defects live.
The Maths You Actually Use
Mean: μ = ( Σ xᵢ ) ÷ n Std. dev: σ = √[ Σ(xᵢ − μ)² ÷ (n−1) ] Z-score: Z = ( x − μ ) ÷ σ → "how many σ is this value from the mean?"

The Z-score is the workhorse. Convert any spec limit into a Z (its distance from the mean in σ-units), then look up the tail area in a normal table to get the fraction of parts beyond it. More σ between mean and spec = smaller tail = fewer defects. That single chain — Z → tail area → defect rate — is the engine under the whole "sigma level" idea on Page 3.

Common-cause variation

The natural, random scatter built into a stable process — many small influences. Predictable. Reducing it needs a process change (a Six Sigma project).

Special-cause variation

An unexpected, identifiable disturbance — a tool breaks, a new batch of material. Shows as an out-of-control point on an SPC chart. Fix the specific cause.

Shewhart's distinction is foundational: you tune common cause with projects and special cause with firefighting. Treating one as the other ("tampering") actually increases variation — the classic over-adjustment mistake.

Sigma Level, DPMO & the 1.5σ Shift

Where "3.4 per million" actually comes from — and the famous fudge factor behind it.

The Sigma Scale
Sigma levelDPMO (with 1.5σ shift)YieldReality
2σ308,53769.1%Uncompetitive
3σ66,80793.3%Typical "non-Six-Sigma" plant
4σ6,21099.38%Industry average
5σ23399.977%Best in class
6σ3.499.99966%World-class / aerospace

DPMO = Defects Per Million Opportunities. Note an "opportunity" is each chance for a defect on a unit — a unit with 10 critical features has 10 opportunities. This lets you compare a simple bracket against a complex engine block fairly.

Why 6σ Gives 3.4, Not 0.002

Here's the subtlety that trips up most people. A perfectly centred process with spec limits at ±6σ would have a defect rate of about 0.002 DPMO — essentially zero. But real processes drift over time: tools wear, temperature swings, materials vary shift to shift. Motorola observed that, long-term, a process mean wanders by roughly ±1.5σ around its target.

So the convention is conservative: rate the process by its short-term spread (6σ between mean and spec), but report the defect rate assuming a 1.5σ long-term shift. A 6σ process shifted 1.5σ leaves 4.5σ to the near limit → tail area ≈ 3.4 DPMO. That's the number on your micro card.

Process Capability — Cp & Cpk

The bridge between your distribution and the customer's spec window. Every Measure phase ends here.

Cp — Potential Capability (spread only)
Cp = ( USL − LSL ) ÷ ( 6 × σ ) USL/LSL = upper / lower spec limit 6σ = the process's natural "voice" width Cp answers: "Is the curve NARROW enough to fit the spec window — IF it were perfectly centred?"

Cp ignores centring. Cp = 1.0 means the ±3σ spread exactly fills the tolerance (a 3σ process, knife-edge). Cp = 2.0 means the spec window is twice the natural spread — that is precisely a 6σ process. The classic shop-floor target is Cp ≥ 1.33 (a "4σ" minimum).

Cpk — Actual Capability (spread AND centring)
Cpk = min[ (USL − μ) ÷ 3σ , (μ − LSL) ÷ 3σ ] Takes the WORSE of the two sides — penalises a process that is off-centre, even if it's tight.
Cp measures only the curve's width; Cpk also measures how off-centre it is. A tight-but-drifted process can have a high Cp yet a poor Cpk — and it's Cpk that predicts real defects.
Reading the Numbers
  • ①
    Cpk = Cp → the process is perfectly centred. The gap between them is your centring error: close it just by adjusting the mean (often free).
  • ②
    Cpk < 1.0 → the process is producing defects now; the tail crosses a spec limit.
  • ③
    Cpk ≥ 1.33 → typical customer requirement (PPAP / automotive). ≥ 1.67 for safety-critical.
  • ④
    Pp / Ppk are the long-term cousins (use overall σ instead of within-subgroup σ). Cpk vs Ppk gap reveals between-batch drift.

🎯 D · DEFINE

State the problem, the customer, the goal and the scope. Charter the project so everyone agrees what success looks like and what's out of bounds. Translate the customer's voice into measurable Critical-To-Quality characteristics.

Tools: Project Charter · SIPOC · Voice of Customer (VoC) · CTQ tree · Kano model
Tollgate: a signed charter with a quantified problem statement and target.
The Logic of the Loop

DMAIC is deliberately sequential and evidence-gated. You Define the right problem, Measure the baseline truthfully (validating the measurement system first), Analyze with statistics to find the proven drivers (the vital few Xs behind the output Y), Improve by experimenting on those drivers, and Control so the gain can't quietly erode. The governing equation of the whole method is simply:

Y = f( X₁, X₂, … Xₙ ) Y = the output the customer feels (the defect, the CTQ) Xs = the process inputs that drive it DMAIC's job: find WHICH Xs matter, then control them — so you stop chasing the symptom (Y) and fix the cause (X).

DMADV

For designing a new process/product (not fixing one): Define-Measure-Analyze-Design-Verify. Also called DFSS — Design for Six Sigma.

The Belts

Yellow (aware) → Green (part-time projects) → Black (full-time, leads projects) → Master Black Belt (coaches & trains). Champions sponsor.

vs 5 Whys

5 Whys is fast & qualitative for daily issues. DMAIC is statistical & rigorous — reserved for chronic, expensive, variation-driven problems.

Measure — Trust the Data First

Before you analyse a single number, prove the numbers themselves are real. This is where most projects are quietly saved or wrecked.

MSA — Measurement System Analysis

Every measurement has its own variation. If your gauge is sloppy, you'll "fix" problems that are really just measurement noise. MSA quantifies how much of the observed spread is the process versus the measurement system.

σ²total = σ²process + σ²measurement Gauge R&R splits measurement error into: • Repeatability — same person, same part, same gauge (the gauge's own scatter) • Reproducibility — different people / setups (the operator-to-operator scatter)

Acceptance rule (AIAG): Gauge R&R < 10% of total variation = good; 10–30% = marginal (may be acceptable depending on cost & criticality); > 30% = unacceptable, fix the gauge before trusting any data.

Baseline Toolkit

Histogram

Shape of the distribution — is it normal, skewed, bimodal (two processes mixed)?

Pareto chart

The 80/20 of defect types — focus the project on the vital few categories.

Run chart

The metric over time — spot trends, shifts and cycles before charting control limits.

Process map

Detailed flow with inputs/outputs at each step — the menu of candidate Xs.

The Measure phase ends with a validated baseline: a trusted Cp/Cpk, a current sigma level, and a quantified gap to the goal. Data types matter — continuous data (a measured dimension) is far more powerful than attribute data (pass/fail), needing ~10× fewer samples for the same statistical power. Measure variables where you can.

Engineer's habit

Always ask "could this signal be the gauge, not the process?" before launching a fix. A 0.01 mm "drift" on a gauge with 0.02 mm R&R is noise. Measure the measurer first.

Analyze — Prove the Cause

Move from "we think" to "the data says, at 95% confidence." This is the statistical heart.

Hypothesis Testing — the Core Idea

You suspect a factor (say, supplier A vs B) changes the output. Hypothesis testing puts a number on whether the difference you see is real or just sampling luck.

H₀ (null): "no difference / no effect" H₁ (alternative): "there IS a difference" Run the test → get a p-value p < 0.05 → reject H₀ ("statistically significant" — the effect is unlikely to be chance) p ≥ 0.05 → fail to reject H₀ (no proven effect) α = 0.05 = the risk of a FALSE alarm (Type I error) β = the risk of MISSING a real effect (Type II)

The p-value is the probability of seeing your data (or more extreme) IF H₀ were true. Small p = your result would be a freak coincidence under "no effect," so you conclude there is an effect. The 0.05 threshold is convention, not law — safety-critical work uses 0.01.

Which Test to Use
QuestionTestExample
Is this mean ≠ a target?1-sample t-testIs mean fill weight = 500 g?
Do two groups differ in mean?2-sample t-testSupplier A vs B hardness
Do 3+ groups differ?ANOVA4 machines' output means
Are two categoricals linked?Chi-squareShift vs defect type
Does X predict Y (continuous)?RegressionTemp → bond strength
Do variances differ?F-test / LeveneIs line 2 more variable?

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) is the Black Belt's staple — it compares several group means at once by partitioning total variation into "between-groups" vs "within-groups," and the F-ratio tells you if the between-group differences are bigger than random noise. Regression then quantifies how much Y moves per unit of X, giving you the transfer function to optimise.

Significant ≠ important

A huge sample can make a trivially small difference "statistically significant." Always pair the p-value with the effect size — does the difference actually matter to the customer and the cost? Statistics tells you it's real; engineering tells you if it's worth acting on.

Improve — Design of Experiments

Stop changing one thing at a time. DOE is how you find the optimum efficiently — and catch interactions.

Why Not One-Factor-at-a-Time (OFAT)?

The instinct is to hold everything fixed and vary one input. But OFAT is slow, and — fatally — it cannot detect interactions (where factor A's effect depends on factor B's level). Design of Experiments (DOE) varies several factors together in a structured matrix, extracting main effects and interactions from far fewer runs.

A 2² full factorial tests both factors at high/low in just 4 runs. Plotting the result exposes interactions — non-parallel lines — that OFAT would completely miss.
The DOE Family
  • ①
    Full factorial (2ᵏ) — every combination of k factors at 2 levels. Complete picture, but runs explode (2⁵ = 32). Best for ≤4 key factors.
  • ②
    Fractional factorial — a cleverly chosen subset; screens many factors cheaply (at the cost of confounding some interactions). The Measure-phase "which Xs even matter?" filter.
  • ③
    Response Surface (RSM / CCD) — adds centre & axial points to fit a curved model and find the true optimum, not just a direction.
  • ④
    Taguchi methods — orthogonal arrays + the signal-to-noise ratio; design settings that are robust to noise you can't control.

The Improve phase ends with a verified, piloted solution: optimised X settings, a confirmation run that reproduces the predicted gain, and often a Poka-Yoke (mistake-proofing, from the Lean toolbox) so the new setting can't be wrong. Then — and only then — you roll out and hand to Control.

Control — Lock the Gain with SPC

The gain isn't real until it survives without you. Statistical Process Control is the autopilot.

The Control Chart

A control chart plots the metric over time against a centre line (the mean) and control limits at ±3σ. Crucially, control limits are the voice of the process (what it actually does) — totally different from spec limits, the voice of the customer (what's wanted). A point outside ±3σ signals a special cause to investigate.

Points dancing randomly within ±3σ = a process in control (common cause only). One point breaching a limit (or a non-random run/trend) = a special cause that needs action. Don't tamper with the in-control noise.
Choosing the Right Chart
Data typeChartUse
Continuous, subgroupsX̄–R / X̄–SMean & range of small samples (e.g. 5 parts/hour)
Continuous, singlesI–MROne reading at a time (slow processes, batches)
Defective units (p/np)p-chart / np-chartFraction or count of bad units per sample
Defects per unit (c/u)c-chart / u-chartCount of flaws (e.g. scratches per panel)

The Control phase deliverable is a Control Plan: which Xs are monitored, on what chart, by whom, how often, and the reaction plan if a point goes out. Add SOPs, training, and Poka-Yokes, then formally hand the process back to the owner — and bank the verified financial saving. Without Control, gains decay within months (the "saw-tooth" of improvement-then-erosion).

Recap, Pitfalls & Quick Check

Lock it in — then test yourself.

One-Screen Recap
  • ✓
    Six Sigma reduces variation (Lean reduces waste); "6σ" = spec limit is 6 std-devs from the mean.
  • ✓
    Every output is a distribution (μ, σ); Z-score → tail area → defect rate is the core engine.
  • ✓
    3.4 DPMO comes from a 6σ process shifted 1.5σ long-term (leaving 4.5σ to the limit).
  • ✓
    Cp = spread vs spec; Cpk = spread and centring. Target Cpk ≥ 1.33.
  • ✓
    DMAIC: Define → Measure (MSA first!) → Analyze (p<0.05) → Improve (DOE) → Control (SPC). Find the Xs behind Y.
Where Six Sigma Programmes Fail
⚠ Tampering

Adjusting a stable (common-cause) process at every wobble. Over-control adds variation — the opposite of the goal.

⚠ Skipping MSA

Trusting a gauge you never validated. You chase measurement noise and "fix" phantom problems.

⚠ p-hacking

Torturing data until something hits p<0.05. Significance without a pre-stated hypothesis is fishing.

⚠ No Control phase

Declaring victory at Improve. Without SPC + control plan, the gain erodes within a quarter.

⚠ Tool worship

Forcing DMAIC onto a simple problem a 5-Why would solve in an hour. Match the method to the stakes.

⚠ Spec ≠ control limits

Confusing customer specs with process control limits — leading to either false alarms or missed defects.

How Six Sigma Connects to the Rest of Your Library

Six Sigma is the statistics half of Lean Six Sigma: it pairs with Lean (A26) which removes the waste. Its Analyze phase shares tools with RCA (A25) — Fishbone, 5 Whys, FMEA. Its Cp/Cpk capability ties directly to GD&T tolerances (A21), and its defect-rate focus feeds the Quality term of OEE (A22). Lean makes it fast; Six Sigma makes it consistent.

Quick Check · Tap to reveal

Q1. A process has Cp = 2.0 but Cpk = 1.1. What's wrong, and what's the cheapest fix?

Tap to reveal ↓ The spread is excellent (Cp = 2.0 = a true 6σ width) but the process is badly off-centre (Cpk far below Cp). The fix is usually free: shift the mean back to target — adjust the setpoint/offset. No variation reduction needed; just re-centring brings Cpk up toward 2.0.

Q2. Why does a "6σ" process report 3.4 DPMO instead of essentially zero?

Tap to reveal ↓ The conventional 1.5σ long-term shift. Short-term the mean sits 6σ from the limit (≈0.002 DPMO), but processes drift over time. Rating defects at a 1.5σ-shifted mean leaves only 4.5σ to the near limit, whose tail area ≈ 3.4 DPMO.

Q3. A test returns p = 0.001 comparing two suppliers. A colleague says "so supplier B is much better." What's the catch?

Tap to reveal ↓ Statistical significance ≠ practical importance. p = 0.001 only says the difference is very unlikely to be chance — it says nothing about size. With a large sample, a 0.1% difference can be significant yet meaningless. Check the effect size and whether it matters to cost/customer.

Q4. Why must you run MSA / Gauge R&R before analysing baseline data?

Tap to reveal ↓ Because σ²total = σ²process + σ²measurement. If the gauge contributes a big chunk of the scatter, the "variation" you analyse is partly the measuring system. You'd chase phantom causes. AIAG rule: R&R <10% good, >30% unacceptable — fix the gauge first.

Q5. On an X̄–R control chart, all points sit randomly within ±3σ except they keep bouncing. The operator keeps nudging the setpoint. What's happening?

Tap to reveal ↓ Tampering / over-adjustment. The bouncing is normal common-cause variation — the process is in control. Reacting to each wobble (treating common cause as special cause) actually amplifies the variation (Deming's funnel experiment). Leave an in-control process alone; only act on out-of-limit or non-random signals.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals worked end to end — the reasoning an assessor rewards.

WE-1 · Capability & Defect Rate From Raw Numbers

A shaft diameter spec is USL = 25.10 mm, LSL = 24.90 mm (target 25.00). The process runs at μ = 25.04 mm, σ = 0.020 mm. Find Cp, Cpk, the short-term Z to the nearest limit, and the long-term DPMO.

Cp = (USL − LSL) / 6σ = 0.20 / (6 × 0.020) = 0.20 / 0.12 = 1.667 Cpu = (USL − μ)/3σ = (25.10 − 25.04)/0.06 = 0.06/0.06 = 1.00 Cpl = (μ − LSL)/3σ = (25.04 − 24.90)/0.06 = 0.14/0.06 = 2.33 Cpk = min(Cpu, Cpl) = 1.00 Z_st (nearest limit) = 3 × Cpk = 3.0σ Long-term Z (1.5σ shift) = 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5σ → tail ≈ 0.0668 → ≈ 66,800 DPMO

Discussion: spread is excellent (Cp = 1.67) but the process is off-centre — it hugs the upper limit, so Cpk collapses to 1.00 and the nearest-limit performance is only 3σ. The cheapest fix is free: shift μ back toward 25.00. Re-centred, Cpu = Cpl = 1.67, Cpk = 1.67 → Z_st = 5σ, a several-hundred-fold defect improvement with no variation reduction. This is the single most important capability lesson: centre before you narrow.

WE-2 · A 2² Factorial DOE — Main Effects & Interaction

Two factors, temperature (A) and pressure (B), each at low (−) and high (+). Response = bond strength (higher better). Runs: (−−)=40, (+−)=52, (−+)=44, (++)=68.

Effect A = ½[(A+ mean) − (A− mean)] using the ±1 contrast: Effect A = ¼[(+40 sign) …] → A = ½[ ( (+−)+(++) )/2 − ( (−−)+(−+) )/2 ] = ½[ (52+68)/2 − (40+44)/2 ] = ½[60 − 42] = 9.0 Effect B = ½[ ( (−+)+(++) )/2 − ( (−−)+(+−) )/2 ] = ½[ (44+68)/2 − (40+52)/2 ] = ½[56 − 46] = 5.0 Interaction AB = ½[ ( (−−)+(++) )/2 − ( (+−)+(−+) )/2 ] = ½[ (40+68)/2 − (52+44)/2 ] = ½[54 − 48] = 3.0

Discussion: temperature dominates (effect 9), pressure matters (5), and the positive interaction (3) means the two together beat the sum of their parts — the (++) corner (68) jumps above what additivity predicts (40 + 9×2? — check: additive prediction for ++ ≈ grand mean + ½A + ½B). Because AB ≠ 0 you cannot optimise the factors one at a time (OFAT would miss the synergy) — the whole point of factorial DOE over changing one variable at a time.

WE-3 · Gauge R&R — Is the Measurement System Fit?

A study decomposes total variation into components (as standard deviations): σ_repeatability = 0.9, σ_reproducibility = 1.2, σ_part = 6.0 (all in µm). Compute %R&R by the standard-deviation (%study-variation) method and judge it.

σ_R&R = √(σ_rpt² + σ_rpd²) = √(0.9² + 1.2²) = √(0.81 + 1.44) = √2.25 = 1.50 σ_total = √(σ_R&R² + σ_part²) = √(1.50² + 6.0²) = √(2.25 + 36) = √38.25 = 6.185 %R&R = σ_R&R / σ_total × 100 = 1.50 / 6.185 × 100 = 24.3%

Discussion: by AIAG rules <10% is acceptable, 10–30% conditional, >30% unacceptable. At 24.3% the gauge is marginal — usable for some decisions but you'd improve it before a capability study, because measurement noise is inflating your apparent σ (σ²_total = σ²_process + σ²_measurement). Note repeatability (equipment, 0.9) is smaller than reproducibility (appraiser, 1.2), so the biggest win is operator training / a clearer procedure, not a new gauge.

WE-4 · X̄–R Control Limits From Subgroups

Subgroup size n = 5. From 25 subgroups, grand mean X̿ = 200.0 and mean range R̄ = 4.0. Build the control limits (constants for n = 5: A₂ = 0.577, D₃ = 0, D₄ = 2.114).

X̄ chart: UCL = X̿ + A₂R̄ = 200 + 0.577×4 = 200 + 2.308 = 202.31 LCL = X̿ − A₂R̄ = 200 − 2.308 = 197.69 R chart: UCL = D₄R̄ = 2.114 × 4 = 8.46 LCL = D₃R̄ = 0 × 4 = 0 Est. σ = R̄ / d₂ = 4.0 / 2.326 = 1.72 (d₂ for n=5 = 2.326)

Discussion: control limits come from the process's own voice (R̄), never from the customer spec — confusing the two is a classic error (Page 15 trap). The estimated σ = 1.72 lets you now compute capability against any spec. Check the R chart first: if the range is out of control the X̄ limits are meaningless because R̄ (which sets their width) is unstable.

Problem Set

Ten problems from recall to judgement. Solve fully before revealing.

P1. A process has Cp = 1.33 and is perfectly centred. What is Cpk, and what short-term sigma level does that correspond to?

Tap to reveal ↓Perfectly centred ⇒ Cpk = Cp = 1.33. Z_st = 3 × Cpk = 4σ (i.e. a "4-sigma" process, ≈6,210 DPMO long-term after the 1.5σ shift).

P2. Convert a long-term yield of 99.38% (single characteristic) to an approximate sigma level.

Tap to reveal ↓Defect rate = 0.62% = 6,200 DPMO. From the sigma table, 6,210 DPMO ≈ 4σ (with the conventional 1.5σ shift). Rule of thumb: 3σ≈66,800; 4σ≈6,210; 5σ≈233; 6σ≈3.4 DPMO.

P3. Why does adding the 1.5σ shift make a process look worse on paper, and who benefits from that convention?

Tap to reveal ↓The shift models real long-term drift, so it moves the mean 1.5σ toward a limit and inflates the reported defect rate (a "6σ" short-term process is rated at 4.5σ → 3.4 DPMO). It benefits the customer/realism: it stops a lab-perfect short-term study from over-promising field performance. It is a convention, not a law of nature — always state whether a figure is short- or long-term.

P4. A capability study gives Cp = 2.0, Cpk = 2.0, but 8% of parts are scrapped. What's the most likely explanation?

Tap to reveal ↓Cp/Cpk that good implies almost zero defects, so the 8% scrap isn't from the modelled variation. Likely causes: the data is not normal (skewed/bimodal, so the Z→tail calc is invalid), the process was not in statistical control during the study (special causes present), or the measurement system is masking real spread (bad Gauge R&R). Capability numbers are only valid for a stable, normal process measured with a capable gauge.

P5. In DMAIC, why is Measure-phase MSA done before you analyse the baseline, not after?

Tap to reveal ↓Because σ²_total = σ²_process + σ²_measurement. If the gauge is a big contributor, the "variation" you baseline and analyse is partly the measuring system, so you'd chase phantom Xs and mis-estimate capability. Validate the gauge (R&R <10%, ideally) first so every later number reflects the process, not the ruler.

P6. Two suppliers are compared; a t-test gives p = 0.03 and a difference in means of 0.2 units, where anything under 1.0 unit is commercially irrelevant. What do you conclude?

Tap to reveal ↓Statistically significant (p<0.05) but practically meaningless: the effect size (0.2) is well below the 1.0 that matters. With a large sample, trivial differences become "significant." Report both significance and effect size; here, treat the suppliers as equivalent for the decision.

P7. On an in-control X̄ chart the operator "helps" by nudging the setpoint after every point that isn't dead-centre. What happens to the variation, and what is the phenomenon called?

Tap to reveal ↓Tampering / over-adjustment — treating common-cause noise as special cause. It increases variation (Deming's funnel experiment: rules 2–4 all inflate spread). An in-control process should be left alone; act only on genuine out-of-control signals (points beyond ±3σ or non-random runs/patterns).

P8. A 2² DOE gives Effect A = 10, Effect B = 2, Interaction AB = 8. Can you set A and B independently to their best levels? Explain.

Tap to reveal ↓No — the large interaction (AB = 8, comparable to A) means the best level of one factor depends on the level of the other. You must read the response at the corner (combination), not pick each factor's best level in isolation. This is exactly why factorial designs beat one-factor-at-a-time: OFAT is blind to interactions.

P9. Estimate σ from a control chart with subgroup n = 4 and R̄ = 6 (d₂ = 2.059). Then give the X̄ limits if X̿ = 50 (A₂ = 0.729).

Tap to reveal ↓σ̂ = R̄/d₂ = 6/2.059 = 2.91. UCL = 50 + 0.729×6 = 50 + 4.37 = 54.37; LCL = 50 − 4.37 = 45.63. (Check the R chart is in control first, else R̄ — and therefore these limits — is not trustworthy.)

P10. When is DMAIC the wrong tool, and what would you use instead?

Tap to reveal ↓When the cause is already known or the problem is simple and quick — DMAIC's rigour is overkill and slows you down. Use a 5-Why / just-do-it / Kaizen for obvious fixes, PDCA for iterative small improvements, or DMADV/DFSS when you're designing a new process rather than improving an existing one. Match the method to the stakes and to whether the solution is known.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Where the formulas come from, a symbol glossary, and the seven mistakes that cost marks.

D-1 · From Z-Score to DPMO (the core engine)

Every defect-rate number in Six Sigma is a normal-tail area. Standardise the distance from the mean to a spec limit:

Z = (limit − μ) / σ Defect fraction beyond that limit = 1 − Φ(Z) = Φ(−Z) DPMO = Φ(−Z) × 1,000,000 (one-sided)

where Φ is the standard-normal CDF. For a centred 6σ process Z = 6 → Φ(−6) ≈ 0.00000000099 → ≈0.001 DPMO. Apply the 1.5σ long-term shift (mean drifts toward the limit, leaving 4.5σ): Φ(−4.5) ≈ 3.4×10⁻⁶ → 3.4 DPMO. That single substitution is the entire origin of the famous "3.4". Two-sided specs add both tails, but when the process is near-centred one tail dominates.

D-2 · Why Cpk Is a min() — and Its Link to Cp
Cp = (USL − LSL) / 6σ … spread only, ignores where μ sits Cpu = (USL − μ) / 3σ , Cpl = (μ − LSL) / 3σ Cpk = min(Cpu, Cpl) … the worst (nearest) side governs

A part fails at whichever limit the mean is closest to, so capability must be judged by the nearest limit — hence min(). When perfectly centred, μ is equidistant, Cpu = Cpl, and Cpk = Cp. The gap Cp − Cpk measures off-centring: define k = |target − μ|/(½ tolerance); then Cpk = Cp(1 − k). k = 0 (centred) → Cpk = Cp; k = 1 (mean on a limit) → Cpk = 0. This is why WE-1's off-centre process had Cp 1.67 but Cpk 1.00.

D-3 · Variance Adds, Standard Deviation Doesn't
σ²_total = σ²_process + σ²_measurement (independent sources) σ_total = √(σ²_process + σ²_measurement) ≠ σ_process + σ_measurement

Independent variation sources combine in quadrature (variances add), which is why Gauge R&R uses √(rpt² + rpd²) and why a measurement σ that's small relative to the process σ barely moves the total — but one that's comparable inflates it sharply. The same rule underlies tolerance stack-ups (RSS) and control-chart σ estimation. Adding standard deviations directly is a top exam error.

⚠ Seven Exam Traps
  • 1
    Cp vs Cpk. Quoting Cp for an off-centre process. Cp is potential; Cpk is reality. The gap = off-centring.
  • 2
    Short- vs long-term sigma. Not stating whether a DPMO/σ figure includes the 1.5σ shift. 6σ short-term = 4.5σ long-term = 3.4 DPMO.
  • 3
    Significance ≠ importance. A small p with a trivial effect size. Always report the effect size too.
  • 4
    Skipping MSA. Analysing data from an unvalidated gauge — you fix measurement noise, not the process.
  • 5
    Spec limits ≠ control limits. Control limits come from R̄ (the process voice); specs come from the customer. Never draw specs on a control chart.
  • 6
    Adding standard deviations. Variances add, not σ's. Combine in quadrature.
  • 7
    Tampering. Adjusting an in-control process at every wobble — this adds variation (Deming's funnel).

Hands-On Ladder
  • 1
    Take 50 measurements of any repeated part; compute μ, σ, Cp, Cpk against a spec by hand, then in a spreadsheet.
  • 2
    Convert your Cpk to Z and DPMO both ways (short- and long-term). Check against a sigma table.
  • 3
    Run a Gauge R&R: 3 operators × 10 parts × 2 trials; compute %R&R and decide if the gauge is fit.
  • 4
    Build an X̄–R chart from 20 subgroups; interpret the R chart first, then X̄; spot any out-of-control signals.
  • 5
    Design and run a 2² factorial on something real (baking time × temperature); compute both main effects and the interaction.
  • 6
    Do a 2-sample t-test and report both p-value and effect size; write the conclusion in plain language.
  • 7
    Write a one-page DMAIC charter for a real defect you care about — define Y, the CTQs, and the suspected Xs.
  • 8
    Simulate Deming's funnel (rules 1–4) in a spreadsheet and watch tampering inflate the spread.
