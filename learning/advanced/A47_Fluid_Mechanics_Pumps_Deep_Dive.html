What Is a Fluid?

A substance that cannot resist shear — and the handful of properties that decide how it behaves.

Definition

A fluid is any substance that deforms continuously under shear stress, however small. A solid strains a fixed amount and stops; a fluid keeps flowing as long as the stress acts. That single property is the root of everything downstream.

We treat fluids as a continuum — ignoring individual molecules and assigning smooth fields of density, pressure and velocity at every point. Valid whenever the smallest scale of interest is far larger than the molecular mean free path (essentially always in engineering flow, failing only in rarefied gas / MEMS regimes — see A34).

Newton's law of viscosity

The defining constitutive relation: shear stress is proportional to the velocity gradient (strain rate).

τ = μ · (du/dy) τ = shear stress [Pa], du/dy = velocity gradient [1/s] Newtonian fluid: μ constant (water, air, light oils) Non-Newtonian: μ varies with strain rate — shear-thinning (paint, blood), shear-thickening (cornflour), Bingham (toothpaste, slurries)

Most industrial pumping is Newtonian. Slurries, greases and polymer melts are not — and a pump curve measured on water lies to you about them (Page 11).

🧠 Quick Check 1

Why does the Reynolds number use kinematic viscosity ν rather than dynamic viscosity μ?

Tap to reveal Reynolds is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces: Re = ρVL/μ = VL/ν. The density in the inertial term and the density hidden in μ combine — dividing by ρ folds both into ν = μ/ρ. So ν is literally "viscosity per unit inertia," the natural parameter for a force ratio. It also has clean units (m²/s) and is what's tabulated for the Moody chart.

Fluid Statics

No motion, no viscosity — just pressure, depth, and the forces they create.

The hydrostatic law

In a fluid at rest, pressure increases linearly with depth and acts equally in all directions at a point (Pascal). Only gravity and pressure balance.

dp/dz = −ρg → p = p₀ + ρg·h (h = depth below the free surface) Pressure head: h = p / (ρg) [m of fluid] — the language pumps speak Water: 1 bar ≈ 10.2 m head · 1 m head ≈ 0.098 bar ≈ 9.81 kPa

Converting pressure to head (metres of the fluid) is the single most useful habit in pump work — it makes the fluid's density drop out of the pump curve and lets you add suction, static lift and friction as simple metres.

Manometry & buoyancy

A manometer turns an unknown pressure difference into a readable height. Buoyancy is just the net of hydrostatic pressure over a submerged body — higher pressure underneath than on top.

Force on a submerged surface
F = ρg·h̄·A h̄ = depth of the surface centroid, A = area Acts at the centre of pressure, below the centroid: y_cp = ȳ + I_x̄ /(ȳ·A)

This sizes dam gates, tank walls, sluice valves and inspection covers. The force cares only about centroid depth and area; the line of action sits lower because pressure grows with depth — which is why a tank door hinges near the bottom.

🧠 Quick Check 2

A pump datasheet lists head in metres, not pressure in bar. You're pumping brine (ρ = 1200 kg/m³). Does the pump deliver the same pressure as on water?

Tap to reveal No — it delivers the same head (metres), but more pressure and more power. A centrifugal pump is a head machine: it throws the fluid to a height set by geometry and speed, independent of density. But p = ρg·h, so at 1200 kg/m³ the same 30 m head is 1200×9.81×30 = 353 kPa vs 294 kPa on water — and shaft power (∝ ρ) rises 20 %. Check the motor doesn't overload on the denser fluid.

Flow & Continuity

Mass is conserved. Everything a pipe does to a flow starts there.

The control volume & continuity

Draw an imaginary boundary — a control volume — and mass in must equal mass out plus what accumulates inside. For steady, incompressible flow: what goes in, comes out.

Continuity: ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂ (general) Incompressible: A₁V₁ = A₂V₂ = Q (constant volume flow rate) → smaller pipe = faster flow: V₂ = V₁·(A₁/A₂) = V₁·(D₁/D₂)²

A halved diameter quadruples the velocity — and (Page 4) the velocity head goes as V², so it rises sixteenfold. This is why reducers, sudden contractions and half-closed valves cost so much: they force the fluid to sprint, and sprinting stores energy the flow can't fully recover.

🧠 Quick Check 3

A branch reduces from DN100 to DN50 carrying the same flow. By what factor does the velocity head (V²/2g) change?

Tap to reveal Velocity rises with (D₁/D₂)² = (100/50)² = 4×. Velocity head goes as V², so it changes by 4² = 16×. That energy is real: at the reducer the pressure drops (Bernoulli), and any turbulent re-expansion downstream dissipates a good chunk as an irrecoverable minor loss. Contractions are cheap to draw and expensive to run.

Bernoulli & the Energy Equation

The book-keeping of a flow: pressure, velocity and elevation trading places along a streamline.

Bernoulli — the ideal ledger

Along a streamline in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, the sum of three energy-per-weight terms — pressure head, velocity head and elevation head — is constant.

p/(ρg) + V²/(2g) + z = constant = H [metres] pressure head velocity head elevation head total head

Each term is energy per unit weight, so all three share the unit of length — metres of fluid. Speed up (V↑) and pressure must fall; climb (z↑) and something else must give. It is conservation of energy wearing hydraulic clothes.

The real energy equation — with losses & a pump

Real flow has friction, and we usually add a machine. Extend Bernoulli between points 1 and 2:

p₁/ρg + V₁²/2g + z₁ + Hpump = p₂/ρg + V₂²/2g + z₂ + hL Hpump = head the pump ADDS · hL = head friction+fittings REMOVE (Page 5)

Rearranged, the pump must supply the static lift, the pressure/velocity change, and every metre of friction loss. That rearrangement is the system curve (Page 8) — the demand the pump has to meet.

HGL & EGL — seeing the energy

The Energy Grade Line (total head) only ever falls in the flow direction — friction is one-way. The Hydraulic Grade Line (pressure head) sits a velocity-head below it and dips wherever the fluid speeds up. If the HGL drops below the pipe, you have sub-atmospheric pressure — and a cavitation / air-ingress risk.

🧠 Quick Check 4

On a long siphon the HGL dips below the pipe crown at the high point. What's the danger, and what limits how high a siphon can go?

Tap to reveal Below-atmospheric pressure at the crown means dissolved air comes out of solution and, if pressure reaches the fluid's vapour pressure, the water boils cold — the siphon breaks (vapour lock). The theoretical ceiling is one atmosphere of head (≈10.3 m of water at sea level), less friction, less the vapour-pressure margin, less altitude derating. Real siphons top out well under 10 m. Same physics as pump-suction NPSH (Page 9).

Viscous Flow & Head Loss

Where the energy actually goes — Reynolds, the Moody chart, and the two kinds of loss.

Laminar vs turbulent — the Reynolds number

Re compares inertia to viscosity and decides the flow's personality: orderly layers (laminar) or chaotic mixing (turbulent).

Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν Re < 2300 → laminar (friction ∝ V, viscosity rules) 2300–4000 → transitional (unpredictable — avoid designing here) Re > 4000 → turbulent (friction ∝ V², roughness matters)

Most water and process flow is deeply turbulent (Re 10⁴–10⁶). Viscous oils and micro-channels can be laminar — a completely different loss law, which is why hydraulic-line sizing (A13) and water-pipe sizing feel different.

Major loss — Darcy–Weisbach & the Moody chart
hf = f · (L/D) · V²/(2g) friction head loss along a pipe f = Darcy friction factor, from the Moody chart = fn(Re, ε/D) Laminar: f = 64/Re (roughness irrelevant) Turbulent (Colebrook): 1/√f = −2·log₁₀( ε/(3.7D) + 2.51/(Re·√f) )

At high Re the friction factor stops caring about Re and depends only on relative roughness ε/D — the "fully rough" regime where most large water mains live.

Minor losses — fittings & valves
hm = K · V²/(2g) K = loss coefficient per fitting ΣhL = hf + Σhm = ( f·L/D + ΣK )·V²/(2g)

🧠 Quick Check 5

Doubling the flow through a fixed turbulent pipe system multiplies the friction head loss by roughly what?

Tap to reveal ≈ 4×. In turbulent flow hL ∝ V² ∝ Q² (f changes only slightly with Re). This quadratic is the shape of the system curve (Page 8) and the reason pumping energy explodes at high flow — and why trimming flow 20 % with a VFD cuts friction-dominated head ~36 % and power far more (Page 10).

Dimensional Analysis & Similitude

Why a model tugboat and a real one obey the same numbers — and how pump laws are born.

Buckingham Π theorem

Any physical relation among n variables built from k independent dimensions can be rewritten as a relation among just n − k dimensionless groups (Π terms). Fewer knobs, universal curves.

Pipe example: hf = fn(ρ, V, D, L, μ, ε) → 7 vars, 3 dims (M,L,T) → 4 Π groups: f = fn( Re , ε/D , L/D ) ← exactly the Moody structure The friction factor IS a dimensionless group; that's why one chart fits all pipes.
The dimensionless numbers that run fluids

Reynolds Re = ρVL/μinertia / viscouspipe flow, drag, pumps — almost everything
Froude Fr = V/√(gL)inertia / gravityfree surfaces: channels, ship hulls, spillways

Similitude — testing small, trusting big

A scale model predicts the prototype only if the governing dimensionless groups match. Match Re for a submerged pump impeller; match Froude for a ship's wave drag. You usually can't match all groups at once (the classic ship-model dilemma: matching Fr forces a Re mismatch), so engineers match the dominant one and correct the rest — the origin of pump "model laws" and the affinity relations (Page 10).

🧠 Quick Check 6

Why can the entire family of pipe-friction data collapse onto a single Moody chart instead of needing a separate curve for every pipe size and fluid?

Tap to reveal Because dimensional analysis reduces seven dimensional variables to three dimensionless groups — f, Re and ε/D. Two completely different pipes (a 20 mm oil line and a 2 m water main) that share the same Re and ε/D have the same friction factor. The chart plots the universal relationship f = fn(Re, ε/D); dimensional variables like diameter, velocity and viscosity only enter through those groups.

Centrifugal Pump Theory

How a spinning impeller turns shaft power into head — Euler, velocity triangles, and the pump curve.

The idea

A centrifugal pump adds energy by flinging fluid outward: the impeller accelerates it to high velocity at the tip, then the volute decelerates that velocity into pressure (a diffuser — Bernoulli in reverse). Kinetic energy in the eye becomes pressure head at the flange.

Euler's pump equation & velocity triangles

Applying angular-momentum conservation to the impeller gives the ideal head added — Euler's turbomachine equation:

gHEuler = U₂·Vu2 − U₁·Vu1 U = blade speed, Vu = tangential fluid velocity Usually designed with no inlet swirl (Vu1=0): gH = U₂·Vu2 U₂ = π·D₂·N/60 → head rises with impeller diameter² and speed² (the seed of affinity laws)

The velocity triangle at the blade tip resolves the fluid's absolute velocity into blade motion (U) plus flow relative to the blade (W). Backward-curved vanes (the industry standard) give a falling head–flow characteristic that is stable and efficient — as flow rises, Vu2 drops, so head droops gracefully.

From ideal to real — the H–Q curve

Euler predicts a straight falling line; reality bends it down via three deductions: slip (fluid doesn't perfectly follow the vanes), friction (∝ Q²), and shock/incidence losses (minimum at the design point, rising either side). The result is the familiar drooping pump curve, with a Best Efficiency Point (BEP) where the losses are least.

🧠 Quick Check 7

Why do almost all process centrifugal pumps use backward-curved vanes rather than forward-curved ones that would give more head?

Tap to reveal Backward-curved vanes give a steadily falling, stable H–Q curve and the highest efficiency. Forward-curved vanes produce a rising/humped characteristic that can be unstable (two flows for one head → surging) and drives a steeply rising power curve that overloads the motor at high flow. Backward-curved trades peak head for a well-behaved curve and a non-overloading (or gently rising) power draw — the right engineering compromise.

Pump Curve Meets System Curve

The operating point is where supply meets demand — find it, and you know everything.

The system curve

The system demands a head that is static lift (fixed) plus friction (∝ Q²). Plot that against flow and you get a rising parabola sitting on a static-head pedestal.

Hsys(Q) = Hstatic + k·Q² Hstatic = elevation lift + (discharge − suction pressure) head → does NOT change with flow k·Q² = all friction + fittings (Page 5) → grows with the square of flow

Static-dominated systems (lifting water up a tower) have a flat-ish demand; friction-dominated systems (long pipelines, filters) have a steep parabola. Which one you have decides whether throttling or speed control saves money (Page 10).

The operating point
static+kQ² H_static operating point Q_op BEP

The pump can only run where its curve crosses the system curve — one flow, one head. Good selection puts that crossing near the BEP; a pump forced far left or right of BEP runs hot, vibrates and wears (Page 12).

Series & parallel

Series (heads add)

Same Q, H₁+H₂. For high head against a steep system curve. Multistage pumps are series impellers on one shaft.

Parallel (flows add)

Same H, Q₁+Q₂. For high flow against a flat system curve. Beware: two pumps rarely give double flow — the steeper system curve robs it.

🧠 Quick Check 8

You add a second identical pump in parallel expecting double the flow but get only ~30 % more. Why?

Tap to reveal The system curve is steep (friction-dominated). Parallel pumps add flow at equal head, shifting the combined pump curve right — but more flow means the system demands much more head (∝ Q²), so the new operating point climbs the curve and the flow gain is throttled by friction. Parallel pumping only delivers near-double flow on a flat, static-dominated system. On steep systems, series (or a bigger single pump) is the right move.

NPSH & Cavitation

The pump's most misunderstood failure — boiling cold water, and the margin that prevents it.

What cavitation is

If the local pressure at the impeller eye falls to the fluid's vapour pressure, the liquid boils — forming vapour bubbles that collapse violently as pressure recovers downstream, hammering the metal at thousands of bar.

The symptoms: a sound like pumping gravel, dropping head and flow, vibration, and pitted, spongy impeller damage. Left running, it destroys impellers in weeks and wrecks seals and bearings (cross-check with vibration signatures, A42).

NPSH — the two numbers that must not cross
NPSHavailable = (patm ± ptank)/ρg − Hstatic,suction − hf,suction − pv/ρg = what the SYSTEM offers at the pump eye, above boiling [m] NPSHrequired = what the PUMP needs (from its curve, rises with Q²) [m] RULE: NPSHa ≥ NPSHr + margin (≥ 0.5–1 m, or ×1.1–1.3)

NPSHa falls with flow (more suction friction); NPSHr rises with flow. Where they cross, the pump cavitates. Every design decision on the suction side is about keeping NPSHa comfortably above NPSHr across the whole operating range.

Fixing / preventing cavitation — all on the suction side
  • 1
    Raise NPSHa: flood the suction (raise the source, lower the pump), shorten & fatten the suction pipe, remove suction restrictions, pressurise the tank.
  • 2
    Cut suction friction: bigger suction line (0.6–1.5 m/s), fewer fittings, full-bore valves, clean strainers — a blocked suction strainer is the classic field cause.
  • 3
    Lower NPSHr: run slower (NPSHr ∝ ~N²), pick a pump with a low-NPSHr impeller or an inducer, use a larger suction eye, run nearer BEP.
  • 4
    Mind the fluid: hot liquids have high pv (boiling water needs huge NPSHa); volatile liquids too. Temperature is a hidden cavitation driver.

🧠 Quick Check 9

A pump runs fine on cold water but cavitates when the same water is heated to 80 °C. Nothing else changed. Why?

Tap to reveal Vapour pressure rose. At 25 °C pv ≈ 0.32 m head; at 80 °C it's ≈ 4.8 m head. NPSHa = atmospheric − lift − friction − pv, so heating the water subtracts ~4.5 m of margin — enough to drop NPSHa below NPSHr. Hot-liquid pumps are almost always flooded-suction designs for exactly this reason (boiler feed, condensate).

Affinity Laws & Flow Control

How head, flow and power scale with speed — and why the VFD beats the valve.

The affinity (fan/pump) laws

Change a centrifugal pump's speed (or trim its impeller) and its curve scales by simple power laws — the single most useful set of relations in rotating-machine energy work.

Q ∝ N flow scales with speed Q₂/Q₁ = N₂/N₁ H ∝ N² head scales with speed² H₂/H₁ = (N₂/N₁)² P ∝ N³ power scales with speed³ P₂/P₁ = (N₂/N₁)³ (same laws with impeller diameter D for a trim, within limits)

The cube law on power is the money: slowing a pump to 80 % speed cuts flow to 80 % but power to 0.8³ = 51 %. That is why variable-speed pumping dominates modern energy-efficient design — and why oversized pumps throttled by a valve waste so much.

Throttle vs speed — where the energy goes

To cut flow: a valve steepens the system curve, burning the excess head as heat across the valve. A VFD lowers the pump curve, so the pump never makes the excess head in the first place — the difference is the cube law made visible.

Caveat — static head breaks the cube law: the affinity cube on power assumes the operating point tracks a curve through the origin. On a static-dominated system, slowing the pump quickly hits the static head and stalls the flow — VFD savings shrink and there is a minimum speed below which the pump delivers nothing. Know your system-curve shape (Page 8) before promising energy savings.

🧠 Quick Check 10

A friction-dominated cooling loop runs a pump throttled to 70 % flow. Estimate the power saving from switching to a VFD at 70 % speed instead.

Tap to reveal On a purely friction system, 70 % flow ≈ 70 % speed. Power scales as N³: 0.70³ = 0.343 → the VFD draws ~34 % of full-speed power. The throttled pump still runs near full-speed power (it makes full head, wasting the excess across the valve — say ~85–90 %). Saving ≈ 50–60 % of pump energy. On payback that's often < 2 years — the headline case for variable-speed pumping (ties to A08 VFD).

Pump Types & Selection

Centrifugal vs positive displacement, specific speed, and choosing the right machine.

The great divide

Rotodynamic (centrifugal)

Adds velocity then diffuses it. Flow varies with head. High flow, moderate head, cheap, smooth. Cannot self-prime (usually). The default for water.

Positive displacement

Traps a fixed volume & pushes it. Flow ~constant regardless of head — makes whatever pressure the pipe resists. High pressure, viscous fluids, metering, self-priming. Needs a relief valve.

Golden rule: a PD pump MUST have a pressure-relief path — dead-head it against a closed valve and it will burst the pipe or stall the motor (it doesn't "give up" like a centrifugal at shut-off head).

Selection workflow
  • 1
    Define the duty: flow Q and total head H (static + friction at design flow) — the operating point you actually need.
  • 2
    Characterise the fluid: density, viscosity, temperature, solids, abrasiveness, corrosivity, vapour pressure — this eliminates whole families.
  • 3
    Pick the type: centrifugal for water-like high flow; PD for viscous, high-pressure, metered or self-priming duties.
  • 4
    Select for BEP: choose a curve whose duty point sits near BEP (ideally 80–110 %), not far right or left.
  • 5
    Check NPSH: NPSHa (system) ≥ NPSHr (pump) + margin across the whole range (Page 9).
  • 6
    Check power & motor: at the worst-case point on the curve (end of curve, densest fluid) — non-overloading motor sizing.
  • 7
    Materials & seals: wetted parts vs fluid; mechanical seal vs packing; ATEX/hygienic/API as the sector demands.
🧠 Quick Check 11

You must meter 20 L/h of a viscous polymer dose accurately against a variable back-pressure. Centrifugal or PD — and why?

Tap to reveal Positive displacement (diaphragm/metering or peristaltic). A centrifugal's flow changes with head, so variable back-pressure would swing the dose — useless for metering — and its efficiency collapses on viscous fluid. A PD pump delivers a near-constant volume per stroke regardless of back-pressure, handles viscosity well, and is inherently a metering device. Fit a relief valve on the discharge.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

Ten ideas, six ways pumps fail, and the memory hook.

The ten ideas
  • 1
    A fluid can't resist shear: τ = μ·du/dy. Density, viscosity and vapour pressure decide behaviour.
  • 2
    Statics: p = ρgh. Think in head (metres) and the fluid's density drops out of pump curves.
  • 3
    Continuity: A₁V₁ = A₂V₂. Narrow = fast; velocity head ∝ V².
  • 4
    Bernoulli + losses + pump = the energy equation. Rearranged, it's the system curve.
  • 5
    Losses: hL = (fL/D + ΣK)·V²/2g. Turbulent loss ∝ Q² — the system parabola.
  • 6
    Dimensional analysis collapses complexity into Re, Fr, Ma… one Moody chart fits all pipes.
  • 7
    A centrifugal pump adds head via Euler: gH = U₂Vu2. Backward-curved vanes = stable curve.
  • 8
    Operating point = pump curve ∩ system curve. Aim it at BEP.
  • 9
    NPSHa ≥ NPSHr + margin, or the pump boils cold and cavitates. Fix it on the suction side.
  • 10
    Affinity: Q∝N, H∝N², P∝N³. The cube law is why VFDs beat valves — unless static head dominates.
Six classic pump failures
🌫 Cavitation

NPSHa < NPSHr → vapour bubbles collapse, sounds like gravel, pits the impeller. Blocked suction strainer or hot liquid is the usual cause. Fix the suction, not the pump.

↔ Running off-BEP

Oversized pump throttled far left, or a worn system running far right → radial thrust, shaft deflection, seal & bearing wear, recirculation damage. Right-size or trim/VFD.

🔥 Dead-heading

Centrifugal against a closed valve → all power becomes heat, fluid flashes to steam, seals cook. PD pump dead-headed → bursts. Fit min-flow / relief protection.

💨 Air / loss of prime

Air in the suction → the impeller spins in vapour, head collapses, seal runs dry. Vent, flood the suction, fix leaking suction joints (they leak air, not water).

〰 Vibration / misalignment

Coupling misalignment, unbalance, bent shaft, or vane-pass resonance → bearing failure. Diagnose with vibration spectra (A42); laser-align.

🧲 Dry-run seal failure

Mechanical seal run without liquid film → faces overheat & crack in seconds. Low-level / no-flow interlocks and proper priming prevent it.

🧠 Final Quiz

Q1 — A pump's measured head is well below its curve and it draws less power than expected, with a rattling noise. Most likely cause?

Tap to reveal Cavitation (or air ingress). Vapour in the impeller reduces the effective fluid density and flow, so head and power both drop, accompanied by the characteristic gravel rattle. Check suction: strainer, valve, NPSHa, liquid temperature. A worn wear-ring also drops head but without the noise and with normal/high power.

Q2 — Convert: a pump makes 45 m head on water. What is that in bar, and in bar on brine (ρ=1200)?

Tap to reveal Water: p = ρgh = 998×9.81×45 = 441 kPa ≈ 4.4 bar. Brine: 1200×9.81×45 = 530 kPa ≈ 5.3 bar — same head, more pressure (and 20 % more shaft power). The head is the density-independent quantity; pressure and power are not.

Q3 — A friction-dominated system runs at 100 m³/h. To drop to 75 m³/h, estimate the VFD speed and the power fraction.

Tap to reveal Q∝N → 75 % speed. P∝N³ → 0.75³ = 0.42 → about 42 % of full-speed power. (Throttling to 75 m³/h would leave power near ~90 %.) The VFD roughly halves the energy — the cube law at work.

Q4 — Why can two identical pumps in series NOT solve a high-flow problem on a flat, static-dominated system?

Tap to reveal Series adds head, not flow. On a flat (static-dominated) system the demand curve is nearly horizontal — the extra head just pushes the operating point far right at almost the same head, giving little useful flow gain and risking the far-right end of the curve (overload, low NPSHa). High flow on a flat system wants pumps in parallel (flows add). Match the pairing to the system-curve shape.

Q5 — Suction line velocity is 3 m/s and the pump is cavitating. Name two suction-side changes and one operational change.

Tap to reveal Suction-side: (1) upsize the suction pipe to bring velocity to 0.6–1.5 m/s (slashes friction, raises NPSHa); (2) shorten/simplify the suction — remove elbows, clean the strainer, flood the pump. Operational: (3) reduce speed (VFD) — lowers NPSHr (∝N²) and moves the duty left. Also: cool the liquid if pv is the driver.
Memory Hook

"Head not pressure, curve meets curve, guard the suction, cube the speed."

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals, fully worked.

WE 1 — Total head & pump power

Given: lift water Q = 40 m³/h up a static height of 18 m through 120 m of DN80 steel pipe (f = 0.022), fittings ΣK = 9. Pump efficiency 72 %, motor 92 %. Find total head, and electrical input power.

V = Q/A = (40/3600)/((π/4)·0.08²) = 0.0111/0.00503 = 2.21 m/s hL = (f·L/D + ΣK)·V²/2g = (0.022×120/0.08 + 9)×2.21²/19.62 = (33.0 + 9)×0.249 = 42.0×0.249 = 10.5 m Htotal = 18 + 10.5 = 28.5 m Phyd = ρgQH = 998×9.81×(40/3600)×28.5 = 3.10 kW Pelec = 3.10/(0.72×0.92) = 4.68 kW → pick a 5.5 kW motor

Discussion: friction (10.5 m) is a large share of a modest 18 m lift — worth checking whether DN100 (V = 1.4 m/s) would cut friction to ~3 m and shrink the motor. The pipe-vs-energy trade over the pump's life usually favours the bigger pipe.

WE 2 — NPSH available

Given: pump draws 30 °C water (pv ≈ 0.43 m, ρ ≈ 996) from an open tank 2.5 m below the pump centreline; suction friction 1.1 m; atmospheric = 10.33 m. Pump NPSHr = 3.5 m. Safe?

NPSHa = patm/ρg − Hlift − hf,suc − pv/ρg = 10.33 − 2.5 − 1.1 − 0.43 = 6.30 m Margin = NPSHa − NPSHr = 6.30 − 3.5 = +2.8 m → SAFE ✓

Discussion: comfortable now — but re-run it hot. At 70 °C, pv ≈ 3.2 m, so NPSHa falls to 4.6 m (margin 1.1 m — marginal), and at 85 °C it would cavitate. The suction lift is the other lever: flooding the pump 2.5 m instead of lifting adds 5 m of NPSHa outright.

WE 3 — Affinity-law re-rate

Given: a pump at 1450 rpm delivers 60 m³/h at 32 m, drawing 7.0 kW. The duty drops to 48 m³/h on a friction-dominated loop. Find the VFD speed, new head and new power.

Q∝N: N₂ = 1450×(48/60) = 1160 rpm H∝N²: H₂ = 32×(48/60)² = 32×0.64 = 20.5 m P∝N³: P₂ = 7.0×(48/60)³ = 7.0×0.512 = 3.58 kW Energy saved vs throttling (~6.5 kW): ≈ 2.9 kW ≈ 45 %

Discussion: the affinity cube assumes the operating point follows a curve through the origin — valid here because the loop is friction-dominated (Hstatic≈0). Add 15 m of static lift and H₂ can't fall below 15 m, the flow stalls sooner, and the savings shrink — always sanity-check against the system-curve shape.

WE 4 — Reynolds & friction regime

Given: (a) water at 20 °C (ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶) in DN50 at 2 m/s; (b) oil ν = 40×10⁻⁶ in the same pipe at the same speed. Classify each and give the laminar friction factor where applicable.

(a) Re = VD/ν = 2×0.05/1.0e-6 = 100 000 → turbulent → use Moody (f ≈ 0.018–0.021, ε/D dependent) (b) Re = 2×0.05/40e-6 = 2500 → transitional/low-turbulent — design out of this band; if kept laminar (Re<2300, slightly slower): f = 64/Re ≈ 64/2300 = 0.028

Discussion: same pipe, same speed, two universes — the water main is deeply turbulent and roughness-sensitive; the oil line hovers in the treacherous transition zone where friction is unpredictable and pulsation-prone. Viscous services are sized to sit firmly laminar or firmly turbulent, never in between.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Work each before revealing.

Problems 1–10

P1 — Water flows at 25 m³/h in DN65 (D=65 mm). Find velocity and velocity head.

Tap to reveal V = (25/3600)/((π/4)·0.065²) = 0.00694/0.00332 = 2.09 m/s. Velocity head = V²/2g = 4.37/19.62 = 0.22 m.

P2 — A tank is pressurised to 1.5 bar gauge above water 4 m deep. Gauge pressure at the bottom?

Tap to reveal p = ptank + ρgh = 150 000 + 998×9.81×4 = 150 000 + 39 160 = 189 kPa ≈ 1.89 bar g (≈ 19.3 m of water head).

P3 — DN100 pipe (V=1.5 m/s) reduces to DN50. Velocity and velocity head in the DN50?

Tap to reveal V₂ = V₁(D₁/D₂)² = 1.5×(100/50)² = 1.5×4 = 6 m/s. Velocity head = 6²/19.62 = 1.83 m (16× the DN100 value of 0.115 m).

P4 — 200 m of DN100 steel (f=0.02) at 3 m/s. Friction head loss?

Tap to reveal hf = f(L/D)(V²/2g) = 0.02×(200/0.1)×(9/19.62) = 0.02×2000×0.459 = 18.4 m. (Halving V to 1.5 m/s → ¼ the loss = 4.6 m — the V² lever.)

P5 — Pump: 30 m³/h at 25 m head, η=70 %. Shaft power?

Tap to reveal Phyd = 998×9.81×(30/3600)×25 = 2.04 kW. Shaft = 2.04/0.70 = 2.91 kW. (Field shortcut: ρQH/(367η) = 998×30×25/(367×0.70)/1000 ≈ 2.9 kW ✓.)

P6 — NPSHa: atmospheric 10.1 m, flooded suction +3 m, suction friction 0.6 m, 45 °C water (pv≈1.0 m). Value, and OK against NPSHr=4 m?

Tap to reveal NPSHa = 10.1 + 3 − 0.6 − 1.0 = 11.5 m. Margin over 4 m NPSHr = 7.5 m → very safe. Flooding the suction (+3 rather than a lift) is what makes it comfortable even warm.

P7 — Pump at 2900 rpm gives 50 m³/h, 40 m, 9 kW. Trim to 2600 rpm (VFD). New Q, H, P?

Tap to reveal ratio = 2600/2900 = 0.897. Q = 50×0.897 = 44.8 m³/h; H = 40×0.897² = 32.2 m; P = 9×0.897³ = 6.5 kW. A 10 % speed cut → ~28 % power cut.

P8 — Reynolds for glycol (ν=15×10⁻⁶ m²/s) in DN40 at 1.2 m/s. Regime?

Tap to reveal Re = VD/ν = 1.2×0.04/15e-6 = 0.048/15e-6 = 3200 → transitional (2300–4000). Undesirable — nudge velocity down for stable laminar (f=64/Re) or up for firm turbulent; don't design a pulsating dose line to sit at 3200.

P9 — System: Hstatic=12 m, and at 60 m³/h friction is 8 m. Write Hsys(Q) and find head at 90 m³/h.

Tap to reveal k = 8/60² = 0.00222 m/(m³/h)². Hsys = 12 + 0.00222·Q². At 90: 12 + 0.00222×8100 = 12 + 18.0 = 30 m. Friction more than doubled (8→18 m) for a 50 % flow rise — the Q² law.

P10 — A centrifugal pump is dead-headed (discharge valve shut) for 10 minutes. What happens and why is it dangerous?

Tap to reveal At shut-off the pump makes maximum head but zero flow, so all shaft power becomes heat in the trapped liquid. Temperature climbs fast; the water can flash to steam, the mechanical seal cooks and fails, and the casing can be damaged. Protect with a minimum-flow bypass, a thermal/no-flow trip, or auto-recirculation valves. (A PD pump dead-headed simply bursts the weakest component — always relieve it.)

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations, a symbol glossary, and seven traps.

Derivation 1 — Bernoulli from the energy equation
Start: steady flow work-energy on a streamline, unit weight of fluid. Pressure work + kinetic + potential are conserved without friction/machine: ∫dp/ρ + V²/2 + gz = const → (incompressible, ÷g) p/ρg + V²/2g + z = H Each term = energy per unit WEIGHT → units of metres (head). Add machine & loss for the real equation: H₁ + Hp = H₂ + hL
Derivation 2 — the affinity laws from geometry
Blade tip speed U₂ = πD₂N/60 → U₂ ∝ N Flow through the impeller ∝ throughflow velocity ∝ U₂ ∝ N → Q ∝ N Euler head gH = U₂·Vu2, and Vu2 ∝ U₂ ∝ N → H ∝ U₂² ∝ N² → H ∝ N² Power P = ρgQH ∝ N·N² = N³ → P ∝ N³ (same reasoning with D at fixed N gives the diameter-trim laws)

The cube law is not empirical — it falls straight out of "flow scales with speed, head scales with speed², power is their product."

Derivation 3 — NPSH available
Apply the energy equation from the free surface (point 0) to the pump eye (point e): p₀/ρg + z₀ = pe/ρg + Ve²/2g + ze + hf,suc Define NPSHa = (pe/ρg + Ve²/2g) − pv/ρg (total head at eye above vapour) Substitute: NPSHa = patm/ρg − (ze−z₀) − hf,suc − pv/ρg (ze−z₀) is +ve for a lift, −ve for a flooded suction.

Seven exam & field traps
  • 1
    Head vs pressure: a centrifugal makes constant head, not pressure — density changes the pressure and power, not the head.
  • 2
    NPSH is a suction problem: you never cure cavitation on the discharge side. Fix the suction, the fluid temperature, or the speed.
  • 3
    Affinity cube needs Hstatic≈0: on static-dominated systems the N³ saving is optimistic and there's a minimum useful speed.
  • 4
    Series adds head, parallel adds flow — and the system-curve slope decides whether either actually helps.
  • 5
    "Minor" losses aren't: on short fitting-heavy skids ΣK·V²/2g can dwarf fL/D. A globe valve (K≈10) alone can dominate.
  • 6
    Never dead-head a PD pump: it has no shut-off head — it makes whatever pressure it takes to fail something. Always relieve.
  • 7
    Water-curve ≠ viscous-curve: a pump's catalogue curve is on water; on viscous fluid head and efficiency drop and power rises — apply viscosity correction (HI charts).

Hands-on ladder
  • 1
    Take a real pump nameplate + curve. Mark its BEP, shut-off head, and NPSHr line. Where does it actually run?
  • 2
    Measure a real system: suction and discharge gauges → compute total head. Compare with the curve at the measured flow.
  • 3
    Build the system curve for one loop: Hstatic + k·Q² from pipe/fittings. Overlay the pump curve; predict the operating point.
  • 4
    Compute NPSHa for that install and compare with NPSHr. Find the margin; identify the biggest suction-side lever.
  • 5
    Apply the affinity laws: pick a lower duty, predict Q/H/P at reduced speed, and estimate the VFD energy saving vs throttling.
  • 6
    Read a Moody chart for two of your pipes; compute hf by Darcy and by Swamee–Jain — check they agree.
  • 7
    Listen to a cavitating pump (video/plant) and correlate the "gravel" sound with a dropped head reading and vibration spectrum (A42).
  • 8
    Do a full selection: given a duty and fluid, choose type, size to BEP, verify NPSH, size the motor, list wetted materials.
