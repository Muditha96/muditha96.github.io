Why TPM Exists — Maintenance as Production, Not Repair

Precise Definition

TPM is a company-wide system that maximises equipment effectiveness over its whole life by making every employee — operators included — responsible for preventing loss, with the goal of zero breakdowns, zero defects, zero accidents.

The micro card called TPM a “culture and method.” Let us make that precise. Traditional maintenance is a fix-it department: operators run, maintainers repair, and the two rarely meet. TPM rejects that split. It treats the machine's condition as a shared product of the people who run it and the people who service it. The word “Total” carries three meanings at once: total effectiveness (chase profit, not just uptime), total system (prevention designed across the whole life cycle), and total participation (everyone, every level).

TPM was formalised by Seiichi Nakajima at the Japan Institute of Plant Maintenance (JIPM) and first deployed at Nippondenso (a Toyota supplier) in 1971. It grew out of American Preventive Maintenance but added the radical step of operator ownership — and it is no accident that the same company gave us OEE. TPM is the engine; OEE is the gauge.

Loss-elimination, not repair

The target is hidden loss across the whole lifecycle, not just the next breakdown.

Everyone participates

Operators do basic care; specialists do complex work; design prevents loss upstream.

Measured by OEE

Every pillar exists to lift Availability, Performance or Quality (A22).

Culture that lasts

Small-group activity + visual standards keep gains from sliding back.

The engineer's mindset

A breakdown is never a surprise — it is the last event in a chain of ignored signals: a small leak, a loose bolt, a faint vibration, a missed lubrication. TPM's central insight is that the operator standing at the machine all shift is the cheapest, fastest sensor you have — if you teach them to look. The chapters ahead turn “everyone owns every machine” into a defined set of strategies, pillars, steps and equations.

The Four Maintenance Strategies — and Where Each Belongs

The micro card showed three strategies. There are really four, and TPM is not “one of them” — it is a system that deploys the right mix of all four per asset. The deciding question for every machine: what does failure cost, and can I see it coming?

The maintenance maturity curve. Each step needs more data and discipline but avoids more cost. TPM moves an asset up the curve — and, crucially, decides how far up each machine should go.
StrategyTriggerBest forWeakness
Reactive (RTF)Failure has happenedCheap, non-critical, redundant items (a $5 lamp).Unplanned stops, scrap, secondary damage, safety risk.
Preventive (PM)Calendar / run-hours / cyclesKnown wear-out items (filters, belts, oil).Over-maintains some, under-maintains others — fixed interval ≠ real condition.
Predictive (CBM/PdM)Measured condition crosses a limitCritical rotating gear — bearings, motors, gearboxes.Needs sensors, baselines & analysis skill (vibration, oil, thermography).

💡 The P-F curve — why PdM beats PM

Every failure has a P-F interval: the time from when a defect first becomes detectable (P) to functional failure (F). Time-based PM ignores it and services on a fixed clock. Condition monitoring catches the defect at P and schedules the fix inside the P-F window — replacing a bearing the week before it would have seized, not on an arbitrary calendar date. TPM's autonomous inspection is the cheapest P-detector of all: the human eye and ear at point P.

🧹 FOUNDATION · 5S + Visual Management + Standard Work

👷 Pillar 1 · Autonomous Maintenance (Jishu Hozen)

Operators take ownership of basic care — cleaning, lubrication, tightening (CLT) and inspection — so they detect and arrest deterioration before it becomes failure.

Example: Operator cleans a conveyor daily, spots a hairline crack in a chain link, swaps it at the planned stop — no line-down event.

The order matters: the first four pillars are the operational core (you'll see them on every TPM site). The last four are the enabling pillars — they make the core sustainable and push prevention upstream into design (Early Equipment Management) and across the office (Office TPM). 5S underneath is non-negotiable: you cannot inspect a dirty machine, because grime hides the very leaks and cracks AM is meant to catch.

Page 4 · Pillar 1 in Depth

Autonomous Maintenance — The Seven Steps

Autonomous Maintenance (AM, Jishu Hozen) is the heart of TPM and the hardest to do well. It is rolled out in seven disciplined steps — each completed and audited before the next. The progression takes operators from “I just press the button” to “I understand and protect this machine.”

The seven-step AM staircase. Each step is audited (a “step certification”) before the team climbs to the next — capability and ownership grow together.
StepWhat the team doesWhat it builds
1 · Initial cleanDeep-clean & inspect; cleaning is inspection; tag every defect found.Exposes hidden defects; restores base condition.
2 · Sources & hard spotsEliminate sources of contamination & places hard to clean/lube/inspect.Makes the machine quick to keep clean.
3 · CLT standardsOperators write provisional Clean–Lubricate–Tighten standards with time targets.Daily care becomes repeatable & visual.
4 · General inspectionTraining on each subsystem (hydraulics, drives, electrics); inspect by checklist.Operator skill — they now understand mechanisms.
5 · Autonomous inspectionOperators run their own inspection standards reliably; overlap with PM removed.Ownership of condition monitoring.
6 · StandardisationStandardise the whole workplace — flows, tools, data, visual controls.Order & consistency across the area.
7 · Full self-managementTeam continuously improves, sets own targets, sustains the gains.A self-directed, improving cell.
🏷️ The red/blue tag system

During AM, every abnormality gets a physical tag. Blue (operator) tags = fixes the team can do; red (maintenance) tags = needs a specialist. Open-vs-closed tag counts become a live visual KPI of the machine's restoration — and a fair split of work between operators and maintainers. This is “everyone owns every machine,” made concrete.

Page 5 · Pillar 2 in Depth

Planned Maintenance — The Specialist's Half

If AM is the operator's half, Planned Maintenance (PM) is the maintainer's. With operators now catching the small stuff, the maintenance team is freed to do proactive, scheduled, skilled work — and to drive the asset up the strategy curve from reactive toward predictive.

Build the asset register

Criticality-rank every machine; failure here decides the strategy mix.

Eliminate forced deterioration

Fix the AM tags; restore design condition so PM intervals make sense.

Information & spares system

CMMS history, BOMs, spares min/max, PM job plans — the data backbone.

Move to condition-based

Add vibration/oil/thermal routes; replace calendar PM with PdM where it pays.

A mature PM system tracks a small set of leading and lagging indicators and feeds them straight back into strategy. The key relationship: as AM + PM mature, the ratio of planned-to-unplanned work flips. A reactive plant runs ~80% unplanned (“firefighting”); a TPM plant pushes planned work above 80–90%, which is cheaper, safer and predictable.

TPM flips the work mix from mostly-unplanned to mostly-planned. Planned work is roughly 3–5× cheaper per job than the same repair done as an emergency breakdown.
Page 6 · The Math

The Reliability Math — MTBF, MTTR & Availability

TPM is a culture, but it is measured with hard reliability engineering. Three metrics convert “fewer breakdowns” into numbers a manager can track — and they map straight onto the Availability factor of OEE.

MTBF = Total Run Time / Number of failures Mean Time Between Failures — reliability MTTR = Total Repair Time / Number of failures Mean Time To Repair — maintainability Inherent Availability A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) the bridge to OEE's “A”

Read the levers directly: AM and PdM raise MTBF (failures happen less often); better spares, job plans and training lower MTTR (when they do happen, you're back fast). Availability rises from both ends. This is why TPM attacks reliability and maintainability together — pushing only one leaves availability on the table.

↑

MTBF

Autonomous care + condition monitoring → fewer failures.

↓

MTTR

Spares ready, job plans written, operators trained → faster recovery.

A↑

Availability

Both levers push A = MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR) upward.

🧮 Worked example

A machine runs 4 000 h and suffers 8 breakdowns totalling 20 h of repair. MTBF = 4000/8 = 500 h; MTTR = 20/8 = 2.5 h; A = 500/(500+2.5) = 99.5%. Now halve the failures with AM (MTBF → 1000 h) and the same MTTR gives A = 1000/1002.5 = 99.75% — and, more importantly, half as many disruptive events, which is where the real Performance and Quality gains hide.

One caution: MTBF assumes a constant failure rate (the flat middle of the bathtub curve). During infant mortality or wear-out, the simple mean misleads — which is exactly why condition-based methods (Page 2) and Weibull analysis exist. TPM's AM step 1 (“initial clean exposes hidden defects”) is, in effect, an attack on the bathtub curve's early-failure region.

Page 7 · The OEE Link

How TPM Attacks the Six Big Losses

TPM and OEE are two halves of one idea: OEE measures the loss, TPM removes it. Every one of the six big losses has a TPM pillar or tool pointed straight at it. This table is the bridge between the two cards — and the single most exam-worthy connection in the whole manufacturing track.

OEE factorBig lossTPM weapon
Availability1 · BreakdownsAutonomous + Planned + Predictive maintenance (raise MTBF).
Availability2 · Setup & changeoverFocused Improvement → SMED (single-minute exchange of die).
Performance3 · Minor stops & idlingAM inspection + poka-yoke + line balancing.
Performance4 · Reduced speedRestore base condition (AM) so the machine runs at nameplate.
Quality5 · Startup rejectsQuality Maintenance — control machine conditions at start-up.
Quality6 · Production rejectsQuality Maintenance + SPC/RCA on the source condition.
The pillars are not abstract — each one feeds a specific OEE factor. Lift the pillars and the OEE number rises by arithmetic, not hope.
Page 8 · Rollout

Deploying TPM — Nakajima's 12 Steps & the Model Machine

TPM fails far more often from bad rollout than bad theory. JIPM defines a 12-step programme across four phases. You do not boil the ocean — you prove it on one model machine, then expand.

PhaseKey steps
Preparation1 Management declares TPM · 2 Educate & promote · 3 Form the TPM organisation · 4 Set policy & baseline OEE targets · 5 Draft the master plan.
Kick-off6 Formal launch (everyone, all levels).
Implementation7 Focused improvement on the model machine · 8 Autonomous maintenance (7 steps) · 9 Planned maintenance · 10 Operations/maintenance training · 11 Early equipment management.
Stabilise12 Perfect TPM, raise the bar, pursue JIPM award.
  • 1
    Pick a pilot, not the plant. One “model machine” — visible, painful, winnable — proves the method and trains the first team.
  • 2
    Measure OEE before you start. No baseline, no proof. The before/after OEE is your business case for expansion.
  • 3
    Small-group activity is the engine. Cross-functional teams (operator + maintainer + engineer) own each machine — that is where ownership becomes real.
  • 4
    Leadership must show up. TPM that's delegated to “the maintenance department” dies. It is a management system, declared from the top.
⏳ How long?

A realistic model-machine result takes 3–6 months; a full-area rollout 1–3 years; cultural maturity (self-managing cells, JIPM-award level) 3–5+ years. TPM is a marathon — which is exactly why the quick early win on the model machine is so important to keep momentum.

Page 9 · Pitfalls

Why TPM Programmes Fail

TPM's failure modes are remarkably consistent across industries — and almost all are organisational, not technical. Spot these early.

🏚️ “Maintenance owns TPM”

Treated as a maintenance project, not a company system. Operators never engage, AM never lands. Cure: top-led, operations-owned, everyone participates.

🧹 Skipping 5S / base condition

Jumping to fancy PdM on a filthy, deteriorated machine. You can't monitor what you can't see. Cure: restore base condition first (AM step 1).

📉 No OEE baseline

No before/after number → no proof, no funding, programme quietly dies. Cure: measure honestly from day one (A22).

⏱️ No protected AM time

“Do AM when you get a chance” = never, under production pressure. Cure: scheduled, ring-fenced daily AM time.

🎖️ Award-chasing over loss-killing

Doing TPM for the certificate, not the losses — binders thicken, OEE doesn't move. Cure: keep the Pareto of losses as the scoreboard.

🔁 No standardisation / sustain

Gains slide back once the kaizen event ends. Cure: visual standards, audits, step-certification, leader standard work.

🔧 The integrity test

A real TPM programme has three fingerprints: operators doing daily AM on a clean machine, a flipped planned/unplanned work ratio, and a rising OEE trend tied to a loss Pareto. Missing any one means you have a poster on the wall, not a system on the floor.

Quick Check 1

Q: Why does TPM insist operators — not just maintainers — do daily cleaning and inspection?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: The operator is at the machine every minute of every shift — the cheapest, earliest detector of the small signals (leaks, looseness, vibration, heat) that precede every breakdown. Cleaning is inspection: it forces hands and eyes over every surface, catching the defect at point P on the P-F curve, long before functional failure F.
Quick Check 2

Q: A machine runs 6 000 h with 12 breakdowns totalling 30 h of repair. Find MTBF, MTTR and inherent availability.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: MTBF = 6000/12 = 500 h. MTTR = 30/12 = 2.5 h. A = 500/(500+2.5) = 99.5%. To raise A, AM/PdM lifts MTBF (fewer events) while better spares/plans cut MTTR (faster recovery) — both push A up.
Quick Check 3

Q: Map each of the six big losses to the TPM weapon that removes it.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: Breakdowns → AM/PM/PdM. Changeover → SMED (Focused Improvement). Minor stops → AM inspection + poka-yoke. Reduced speed → restore base condition. Startup rejects → Quality Maintenance. Production rejects → Quality Maintenance + SPC/RCA. (Losses 1–2 feed Availability, 3–4 Performance, 5–6 Quality.)
Quick Check 4

Q: A plant skips 5S and the initial clean and goes straight to vibration-based PdM. Why does the programme stall?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: A dirty, deteriorated machine hides the very defects monitoring is meant to catch and generates so much “forced deterioration” noise that baselines are meaningless. TPM restores base condition first (5S + AM step 1) so signals are clean — only then does condition monitoring earn its keep.
