One Job, Five Architectures

Every powertrain answers the same question — how do I get stored energy to the tyre contact patch? — with a different energy path.

Definition

A powertrain is everything between the energy store and the driven wheels: the converter (engine or motor), the transmission, and the control system that decides how much torque to deliver, when, and from where.

An internal-combustion (ICE) powertrain stores energy as fuel (~12,000 Wh/kg) but converts it at only ~25–35 % tank-to-wheel efficiency, needs a multi-speed gearbox because the engine only makes usable torque in a narrow rev band, and cannot recover braking energy. A battery-electric (BEV) powertrain stores far less energy per kilogram (~150–260 Wh/kg at cell level) but converts it at ~77–90 % battery-to-wheel efficiency, makes full torque from 0 rpm — so one fixed gear ratio is enough — and pumps braking energy back into the pack. The engineering trade is therefore energy density vs conversion efficiency, and everything on the next nine pages flows from it.

Between the two extremes sits the hybrid spectrum: micro/mild hybrids (48 V belt starter-generator that assists and harvests), full hybrids (power-split e-CVT, engine and motor blend torque), plug-in hybrids (bigger pack, 50–100 km electric range), range-extended EVs (engine only generates electricity), and fuel-cell EVs (H₂ stack generates, a small buffer battery handles transients).

The electrification spectrum

Electrification is a spectrum, not a switch. The BEV path — battery → inverter → motor → single-speed gear → wheels — is the spine of this module.

Why the industry flipped

Efficiency

~3×

Battery-to-wheel ~77–90 % vs tank-to-wheel ~25–35 %. Physics, not fashion.

Parts count

−90 %

~20 moving powertrain parts vs ~2,000. No oil, clutch, exhaust, valvetrain.

Torque at 0 rpm

100 %

Full torque from standstill — no clutch slip, no turbo lag, one gear ratio.

The catch: energy storage. Petrol carries ~12,000 Wh/kg; a Li-ion pack delivers ~150–180 Wh/kg at pack level — roughly a 70× gap. EV engineering is the art of living well inside that gap: minimise the load (p.2), maximise conversion efficiency (p.3–4), and manage the battery like the precious, fragile asset it is (p.5–6, 8).

Road-Load Physics — What the Motor Must Beat

Four forces stand between the vehicle and constant speed. Add them up and you have sized the powertrain.

The tractive-effort equation

At any instant the motor must supply force equal to rolling resistance + aerodynamic drag + grade + inertia. Everything in vehicle design — mass, shape, tyres — is a term in this one equation.

F_tractive = C_rr·m·g + ½·ρ·C_d·A·v² + m·g·sin θ + (m + m_rot)·a P_wheel = F_tractive · v P_battery = P_wheel / η_drivetrain rolling: C_rr ≈ 0.007–0.012 (EV tyres low-RR) aero: ρ=1.2 kg/m³, C_d·A ≈ 0.5–0.8 m² grade: sin θ (θ = slope) inertia: m_rot ≈ +3–5 % effective mass

Worked example — 2,000 kg SUV, C_rr = 0.010, C_d·A = 0.58 m², flat road, steady 100 km/h (27.8 m/s): rolling = 0.010 × 2000 × 9.81 = 196 N; aero = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.58 × 27.8² = 269 N. Total 465 N → P_wheel = 465 × 27.8 ≈ 12.9 kW; at 85 % drivetrain efficiency ≈ 15.2 kW electric ≈ 152 Wh/km. Note aero already dominates at 100 km/h — and it grows with v², so consumption per km grows roughly with v². This is why motorway range is always worse than city range: in the city, speeds are low and regen recovers the inertia term.

Where the energy goes vs speed

Aerodynamic power grows with v³ (force ∝ v², power = F·v). Above ~75 km/h drag is the main enemy; below it, mass and tyres are.

Traction Motors — Torque From Standstill

The EV motor is a three-phase AC machine (→ A30) run backwards and forwards a thousand times a minute by its inverter.

The torque–speed envelope

Every traction motor has two regions: constant torque below base speed (current-limited) and constant power above it (voltage-limited, field weakening). The corner point is base speed.

Below base speed: T = T_max (current limit) P = T·ω rises linearly Above base speed: P = P_max (voltage limit) T = P_max / ω falls as 1/ω Field weakening: inject negative d-axis current → weakens magnet flux → back-EMF stays under DC-link voltage → motor keeps spinning faster (2–4× base speed)

This envelope is why an EV needs no gearbox: the motor itself behaves like an infinitely variable transmission. A typical traction PMSM makes constant ~300 Nm from 0 to ~5,000 rpm, then constant ~150 kW up to ~16,000 rpm. Compare an ICE: near-zero torque below 1,200 rpm, usable band maybe 2,000–6,000 rpm — hence five to ten gears.

One machine covers the whole car envelope: launch torque at 0 rpm, motorway power at 16,000 rpm. The inverter's d–q current control does the shaping.

Motor speeds keep climbing (20,000+ rpm) because power = torque × speed and torque costs copper and magnets, while speed only costs gear ratio (→ p.7).

Why torque is really current

Torque

T ∝ I_q

Quadrature current makes torque. The accelerator pedal is a current request.

Speed

V-limited

Back-EMF rises with rpm until it meets the DC-link voltage — that's base speed.

Flux

−I_d

Negative d-axis current bucks the magnet flux to buy more speed: field weakening.

The inverter runs field-oriented control (FOC): measure rotor angle (resolver), transform the three phase currents into the rotating d–q frame, close two PI loops (→ A10) on I_d and I_q at ~10 kHz, transform back, and generate PWM. The motor becomes, in software, a DC motor with two knobs — one for torque, one for flux.

The Inverter — and Getting Energy Back

Six switches stand between the pack and the motor (→ A29, A30). They work in both directions — that's regen.

Traction inverter anatomy

The inverter chops DC-link voltage into three PWM phase voltages tens of thousands of times per second. Torque direction and power direction are set purely by switching timing — the same hardware motors and regenerates.

Why SiC took over — and the 800 V logic

Traction inverters moved from Si IGBTs to SiC MOSFETs (→ A29 p.9): SiC switches faster with far lower switching energy, has no IGBT knee voltage at light load, and tolerates higher junction temperatures. Real-world payoff is ~3–8 % more range, mostly at part load where cars actually drive. Doubling the pack to 800 V halves current for the same power: I²R losses in cables and windings drop ~4×, cables get thinner and lighter, and 350 kW charging becomes possible without absurd connector currents. The price: every component must be rated 1,000 V+, and creepage/clearance distances grow.

P_loss(conduction) = I²·R_ds(on) P_loss(switching) = (E_on+E_off)·f_sw 800 V vs 400 V at same power → I halves → conduction loss ÷4 → thinner busbars
Regenerative braking — the free-energy loop

Lift off (or press brake): the VCU requests negative torque. The inverter shifts phase so the machine generates; kinetic energy ½mv² flows back through the DC link into the pack. Typical recovery is 60–70 % of braking energy — city driving regains 15–30 % of total consumption. Friction brakes remain for hard stops, low speed (<~8 km/h regen fades), a full pack (nowhere to put the charge — BMS limits regen when cold or full), and failure back-up. Brake blending software mixes regen and friction seamlessly on one pedal — the hardest calibration job in the chassis; one-pedal driving is simply an aggressive regen map.

The Battery — Cell, Module, Pack

The most expensive, heaviest, most safety-critical component in the car. Everything else is designed around it.

Inside a lithium-ion cell

A Li-ion cell is a rocking-chair: lithium ions shuttle between a graphite anode and a metal-oxide cathode through a liquid electrolyte, across a micro-porous separator. Discharge = ions to cathode, electrons through your motor. Charge = pushed back.

Cell → module → pack
Series adds voltage: 96s × 3.7 V ≈ 355 V nominal (a "400 V" pack) 198s × 3.7 V ≈ 733 V (an "800 V" pack) Parallel adds capacity: 4p × 50 Ah cells = 200 Ah Pack energy: E = V_nom · Ah = 355 V × 200 Ah ≈ 71 kWh C-rate: I = C × Ah → 2C on 200 Ah = 400 A (3.5 kW/cell-group heat at 22 mΩ)

Cells group into modules (with sense leads and cooling interfaces), modules into a pack with contactors, pre-charge resistor, fuse/pyro-fuse, current sensor, isolation monitor and the BMS. The structural trend is cell-to-pack (CTP) and cell-to-chassis: delete module housings, bond cells straight into a floor structure — 10–20 % more energy in the same volume, but repairability suffers. The pack floor also defines the car: low centre of gravity, stiff platform, and the "skateboard" architecture every dedicated EV platform uses.

BMS & Charging — Guarding the Asset

The battery management system is the pack's PLC, protection relay and accountant in one box.

What the BMS actually does
  • 1
    Measure everything. Every series group's voltage (±1 mV class), pack current (shunt/Hall), dozens of temperatures, and isolation resistance between HV bus and chassis.
  • 2
    Estimate SoC & SoH. State of charge = coulomb counting (∫I dt) continuously corrected against the open-circuit-voltage curve (plus Kalman filtering — flat OCV chemistries like LFP make this genuinely hard). State of health = capacity fade + resistance growth tracked over months.
  • 3
    Balance cells. The weakest series group caps the whole pack. Passive balancing bleeds high cells through resistors (~100 mA) near top of charge, keeping the string aligned.
  • 4
    Enforce limits. Charge/discharge current limits as a function of temperature and SoC (cold pack → tiny regen and slow charge; hot pack → derate). Below 0 °C charging graphite plates lithium metal — permanent damage — so the BMS simply refuses or heats first.
  • 5
    Protect and disconnect. Contactor control with pre-charge (never slam contactors into discharged DC-link caps), fuse coordination, crash-signal pyro-disconnect, and thermal-runaway detection (dV/dt + temperature + pressure vents).
Charging — AC vs DC

DC charging follows a charge curve, not a constant number: roughly constant current (near peak power) until ~50–60 % SoC, then tapering constant-voltage-ish region as cell voltage approaches 4.2 V, with the BMS shaving current further for temperature. That is why 10→80 % is quick and 80→100 % is slow — and why trip planners hop between 10 and 70 %. Preconditioning (heating the pack to ~30–40 °C on approach to a charger) can halve charge time in winter.

Drivetrain — One Gear, Multiplied

Motor torque is multiplied once, split by a differential, and delivered. The e-axle packages all of it in one casting.

Torque path & the single-speed reducer
T_wheel = T_motor · G · η_gear ω_motor = ω_wheel · G F_traction = T_wheel / r_wheel a = (F_traction − F_roadload) / m Example: 310 Nm × G 9.0 × 0.97 = 2,706 Nm at the axle r = 0.35 m → F = 7,730 N → 2,000 kg → a ≈ 3.8 m/s² from standstill (0.39 g)

A ratio around 8–10 : 1 (usually two helical stages) matches a 16,000 rpm motor to ~180 km/h. Why not two speeds? Because the motor's constant-power region already covers the envelope — a second gear buys a few percent efficiency at high speed for cost, mass and a shift event; only performance/towing outliers (Porsche Taycan rear axle) bother. The open differential splits torque equally while letting wheels run different speeds in corners; torque vectoring is done electrically — twin motors per axle or brake-based — rather than with clutch packs.

The e-axle (3-in-1) integrates motor + inverter + reducer into one housing: shorter busbars, shared cooling, one assembly to bolt in. AWD EVs simply add a second e-axle and split torque in software, front/rear, in milliseconds — traction control at inverter speed (~100× faster than an ICE throttle) is why EVs launch so well.

Gear design details — involute geometry, helical overlap, bearing loads — are exactly the A20 Gears and A19 Bearings material applied at 16,000 rpm input speed (NVH: gear whine replaces engine noise).

Thermal Management — The Hidden Subsystem

Every watt of loss on pages 3–7 becomes heat somewhere. Range in winter is won and lost here.

Three loops, one refrigerant heart

A modern EV runs three coupled circuits — battery loop (target 20–40 °C), powertrain loop (motor/inverter, tolerates 60–90 °C coolant), and cabin — tied together by a refrigerant circuit / heat pump and multi-way valves that re-plumb the car in software.

Why temperature is destiny for the pack

Too cold

< 0 °C

Li plating on charge; internal resistance up ~3×; regen and fast charge blocked until heated.

Sweet spot

20–40 °C

Full power, full regen, minimum ageing. The BMS fights to live here.

Too hot

> 45–60 °C

SEI growth accelerates ageing; sustained >60 °C risks venting; runaway onset ~150–270 °C.

Thermal runaway is the failure everyone designs against: one cell's exothermic decomposition heats its neighbour past onset, and the chain propagates. Defences stack: chemistry choice (LFP's higher onset and no oxygen release), cell spacing and aerogel/mica barriers, vent channels that route hot gas out of the pack, pressure/temperature sensors giving occupants a mandated 5-minute escape warning (GB 38031, UN GTR 20), and fast-charge derating long before limits. Failure analysis of a runaway is a proper RCA exercise (→ A25).

Cell heat: Q̇ = I²·R_int + I·T·(dU/dT) (ohmic + entropic) 2C fast-charge on 200 Ah string, R_int 22 mΩ → I²R ≈ 3.5 kW just in cells → chiller sized 6–10 kW so 350 kW charging doesn't cook the pack

Networks, Control & Functional Safety

A modern car is ~100 computers on wheels. The powertrain is orchestrated, arbitrated and guarded in software.

Who talks to whom

The VCU (vehicle control unit) reads the pedal, applies torque maps and traction logic, and sends torque requests to the inverter and current limits from the BMS — over CAN / CAN-FD (→ A04), with automotive Ethernet climbing aboard for sensors and OTA.

ISO 26262 — functional safety on wheels

Automotive functional safety mirrors the machinery world you know from A35, with its own vocabulary: hazards are rated by Severity × Exposure × Controllability into ASIL A–D (vs PL/SIL). Unintended acceleration and loss of braking are ASIL-D: dual-channel pedal sensors with plausibility checks, lockstep-core microcontrollers, independent torque-monitoring watchdogs that can open the contactors, and defined safe states (torque = 0, HV off). HV-specific safety adds isolation monitoring (≥100 Ω/V), pyro-fuses fired by the airbag ECU, and service disconnects. Cybersecurity (ISO/SAE 21434 + UN R155/R156) is now homologation-mandatory — an OTA-updatable powertrain is also an attack surface: signed firmware, secure boot, gateway firewalls between infotainment and the torque path.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

The whole powertrain on one page — then prove it stuck.

Eight ideas to keep
  • 1
    Everything is the road-load equation. C_rr·mg + ½ρC_dAv² + grade + inertia. Aero power grows with v³ — speed is the range killer.
  • 2
    EVs win on conversion, lose on storage. ~85 % battery-to-wheel vs ~30 % tank-to-wheel, against a 70× energy-density gap.
  • 3
    The motor is its own gearbox. Constant torque to base speed, constant power beyond via field weakening — one ratio suffices.
  • 4
    Torque is I_q. FOC turns an AC machine into two current knobs; the pedal is a current request executed at 10 kHz.
  • 5
    The inverter is bidirectional. Regen is the same six SiC switches with reversed power flow — 60–70 % of braking energy comes back.
  • 6
    The weakest cell is the pack. Series×parallel sets V×Ah; balancing, SoC estimation and limits are the BMS's whole life.
  • 7
    Temperature is destiny. 20–40 °C or the BMS derates you; below 0 °C charging plates lithium; runaway is designed against in layers.
  • 8
    Software arbitrates torque. VCU → CAN → inverter under ISO 26262 ASIL-D, with cybersecurity as a legal requirement.
Six classic failure modes
⚠️ Lithium plating

Fast-charging a cold pack deposits metallic Li on the anode: permanent capacity loss, dendrite risk. Symptom: sudden capacity drop after winter DC charging. Fix: preconditioning + BMS cold-charge limits.

⚠️ Cell imbalance

One weak series group hits the 4.2 V ceiling first → charging stops early; hits the floor first → range vanishes. Symptom: shrinking usable window. Fix: balancing healthy? Replace the outlier group, not the pack.

⚠️ Isolation fault

Coolant leak, chafed HV cable or moisture drops HV-to-chassis resistance; the isolation monitor flags it and the car may refuse to start or charge. Never a "clear and drive on" code — find the leak path.

⚠️ Inverter power-stage failure

Shoot-through, bond-wire fatigue or a failed DC-link cap (→ A29 failures). Symptom: sudden torque loss, isolation trip, or a DTC pointing at one phase. IGBT/SiC modules fail short — fuses and pyro must clear.

⚠️ Bearing currents & gear whine

Fast PWM edges drive common-mode current through motor bearings → fluting (→ A19); high-rpm helical stages expose any micro-geometry error as whine. Fixes: shaft grounding ring, insulated bearing, gear micro-geometry.

⚠️ Contactor / pre-charge damage

Closing main contactors into discharged DC-link caps welds them shut (inrush). Symptom: contactor won't open — a serious safety fault. The pre-charge resistor sequence exists precisely to prevent this.

🧠 Quick Check 1

Why does an EV need only one gear ratio while an ICE needs 5–10?

Tap to reveal The electric motor delivers full torque from 0 rpm (constant-torque region) and then constant power up to very high speed via field weakening — its usable band covers the whole vehicle envelope. An ICE only makes useful torque in a narrow rev band, so gears must keep it there.
🧠 Quick Check 2

A car doubles its speed from 60 to 120 km/h on a flat motorway. Roughly what happens to the aerodynamic power it must supply?

Tap to reveal Aero force ∝ v², power = force × v ∝ v³ → about 8× the aerodynamic power (and roughly 4× the aero energy per km). This is why motorway consumption is dramatically worse than city consumption.
🧠 Quick Check 3

Why does DC fast charging slow down dramatically above ~80 % SoC?

Tap to reveal Cell voltage approaches the 4.2 V ceiling, so the charger must taper current to avoid overvoltage (CC→CV transition); the BMS also limits current to prevent lithium plating. The last 20 % can take as long as the first 70 %.
🧠 Quick Check 4

Why must the BMS refuse (or pre-heat before) charging below 0 °C?

Tap to reveal Below ~0 °C, intercalation into graphite is too slow, so lithium deposits as metal on the anode surface (plating) instead. It permanently destroys capacity and can grow dendrites that pierce the separator → internal short → thermal-runaway risk.
🧠 Quick Check 5

Same power, but the OEM moves from a 400 V to an 800 V pack. What happens to conduction losses in the cables, and why does charging benefit?

Tap to reveal Current halves (P = V·I), so I²R conduction losses fall to one quarter for the same resistance — or cables can be much thinner. Charging at 350 kW becomes feasible at ~440 A instead of ~875 A, which connectors and cables can actually carry.
🧠 Quick Check 6

Regen suddenly feels weak on a frosty morning with a 95 % charged pack. Name the two reasons.

Tap to reveal (1) Cold pack: charge-current limit is tiny near/below 0 °C (plating risk), so the BMS caps regen. (2) Nearly full pack: there's nowhere to put the recovered energy near 100 % SoC. The car blends in friction braking to compensate.
