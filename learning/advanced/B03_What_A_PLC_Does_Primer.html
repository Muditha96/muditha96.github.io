Cold Open — One Bottle in Fifty, for Eleven Weeks

LINE 3, A DRINKING-WATER PLANT NEAR KELANIYA CORE

The line fills, caps and labels 120 bottles a minute. Somewhere after the capper, a photo-eye checks that a cap is present. If the cap is missing the bottle is blown off the conveyor into a reject bin. That is the entire job of the sensor: see the cap, or reject.

For eleven weeks the line has been rejecting good bottles. Not many — roughly one in fifty, which is 2.4 bottles a minute, which nobody notices on a shift report but everybody notices in the reject bin at the end of the day. The maintenance log has seven entries. The photo-eye has been cleaned four times, replaced twice, re-aligned constantly. A supplier engineer came, ran the line at half speed for an hour, saw zero false rejects, and wrote “no fault found — recommend operator training.”

You are asked to look at it because you are the mechanical engineer, the conveyor is mechanical, and everyone has run out of ideas. There is nothing wrong with the conveyor. There is nothing wrong with the sensor either. The fault is in a number nobody in the room knows: the scan time of the PLC is 34 milliseconds, and the cap passes the sensor in 33.3.

That sentence is the whole reason this primer exists. By page 4 it will be obvious to you. Right now it probably isn't — and that gap is exactly the gap a mechanical engineer has when handed an automated machine.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A sensor sees a target for 33.3 ms. The PLC looks at that sensor once every 34 ms — at one instant per cycle, not continuously. What fraction of targets does it miss?

About 2 % — and that is the reject rate on the log sheet. The PLC samples the input at one instant in each 34 ms cycle. A pulse of width W covers that instant unless the instant happens to fall in the 34 − 33.3 = 0.7 ms gap between pulses. Because the conveyor and the processor are not synchronised, the sampling instant lands anywhere in the cycle with equal probability, so
P(miss) = (T − W) / T = (34 − 33.3) / 34 = 0.0196 = 1.96 % ≈ 1 bottle in 51 P(catch) = W / T = 33.3 / 34 = 97.9 % valid only while W < T
And now the supplier engineer's “no fault found” makes sense too. At half line speed the cap takes twice as long to cross the beam — W = 66.7 ms, which is larger than the 34 ms scan, so every target is caught and the fault vanishes completely while he watches. An intermittent fault that disappears under observation is very often a sampling fault, not a component fault.
FOUR FAULTS, ONE MACHINE CORE

The missed cap is only the fault that got you called. While you are in that panel you will find three more, and each one teaches a different half of what a PLC is. They run through this primer as a thread; page 15 closes all four with numbers.

● FAULT P1 — the missed pulse

A 33.3 ms cap signal sampled by a 34 ms scan. Costs 2 % of good product. Taught on pages 4 and 10.

● FAULT P2 — the double coil

Two rungs, written eight months apart, both write the glue-applicator output. The later one silently wins. Page 10.

● FAULT P3 — the memory that survived

The sequence step number was declared retentive. After a power dip the machine resumes at step 6 with the arm physically at home. Page 11.

● FAULT P4 — the force nobody removed

A guard-door input has been forced true since commissioning. The door interlock has not worked for three months. Page 13.

Notice what the four have in common. Not one of them is a broken component. Every one is a misunderstanding of what the machine in the box is doing — and P4 is the kind that eventually removes somebody's hand.

What Transfers from Mechanical Engineering — and What Does Not

YOU HAVE ALREADY BUILT ONE OF THESE CORE

A cam shaft with six lobes and six limit switches is a programmable logic controller. It reads the world (the followers), it decides (the lobe profile), it acts (the switches), and it does this over and over in a fixed cycle. Every idea in this primer already exists in that shaft. What changes is only where the lobe profile is stored.

WHY THE PLC WAS INVENTED — AND IT WAS NOT COMPUTING CORE

In 1968 General Motors' Hydra-matic division wrote a specification for a replacement for relay panels. The problem was not that relays were unreliable; the problem was the annual model change. Re-wiring a body-shop relay panel took weeks, and the panel was the schedule. GM asked for a device that a plant electrician — not a programmer — could re-configure, that would survive a factory environment, and that could be maintained with the skills already on site. Bedford Associates' winning entry, the Modicon 084, is the ancestor of everything in this primer, and Dick Morley's team made one decision that still shapes the industry: the programming language would look exactly like the relay drawings the electricians already read.

That is why ladder logic exists, and why it looks archaic. It is not a bad programming language pretending to be modern. It is a deliberate compatibility layer with the existing workforce — and fifty-eight years later it is still the most widely deployed industrial language on earth, because the argument that created it never stopped being true.

The last row is the whole difficulty. A mechanical linkage responds to the world continuously: push the lever and the valve moves, with no interval in which the machine is unaware. A PLC is a sampled system. Between one photograph and the next it is blind, and it will happily act on a world that has already changed. Everything strange about PLCs — missed pulses, one-scan delays, double coils, outputs that lag by exactly two cycles — is a consequence of that single fact.

WHEN A PLC IS THE WRONG ANSWER STRETCH

Engineers who have just learned PLCs put PLCs on everything. The honest boundaries:

  • 1Below about six relays with no sequence, a contactor and two relays is cheaper, faster to fault-find, and needs no software licence. A single motor with a start, a stop and an overload does not need a processor.
  • 2Safety functions do not belong in a standard PLC. An emergency stop, a guard interlock or a two-hand control goes to a safety relay or a safety PLC with a rated architecture — see A52. A standard PLC has no diagnostic coverage and no rated failure rate. This is not a formality; it is why P4 on page 13 matters.
  • 3Microsecond control — a motor current loop, a switching converter — belongs in a drive or an embedded processor. A PLC scan of 5–50 ms is four orders of magnitude too slow (A36).
  • 4Continuous process plant with hundreds of loops is DCS territory, not PLC territory — the difference is engineering scale and integration rather than capability (A61).
  • 5A single-board computer is not a PLC. A Raspberry Pi can do wonderful things in a plant, including on this line — but not the interlocks, and not anything a person's safety depends on.

Inside the Box — Five Parts, and What Each One Fails At

THE ANATOMY CORE

Open any PLC panel in the world and you find the same five things on a DIN rail: a power supply, a CPU, input modules, output modules, and something that lets it talk. Everything else is a variation on packaging.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A modern PLC CPU is roughly as powerful as a mobile phone from 2009. Yet a plant will pay LKR 400,000 for one when a far faster single-board computer costs LKR 12,000. What is the money actually buying?

Determinism, longevity and accountability — none of which is speed. A PLC guarantees that the same program takes the same time every cycle, because there is no operating system deciding to do something else. It boots to control in seconds after a power cut with no file system to repair. Spare parts and firmware exist for fifteen to twenty-five years. It carries certification and a vendor who will answer for it. And it is diagnosable at 3 a.m. by an electrician with a meter, because every I/O point has an LED.

THE WATCHDOG, RUN/STOP, AND WHAT THE LEDS MEAN CORE

A hardware timer is reloaded at the end of every scan. If a scan ever takes longer than its limit — typically 50–300 ms, configurable — the timer expires, the CPU faults, and all outputs go to their configured safe state. That is the watchdog, and it is the reason an infinite loop in a PLC produces a stopped machine rather than a running one. Compare it with a mechanical over-speed trip: same philosophy, same century.

The Scan Cycle — the Load-Bearing Idea

FOUR STEPS, FOREVER CORE

A PLC in RUN mode does exactly four things, in the same order, without pause, until you stop it: read the inputs, solve the program, write the outputs, do the housekeeping. One trip round that loop is a scan. The time it takes is the scan time, and it is the most important number about any PLC installation — and the one number that is almost never written on the drawing.

THE PROCESS IMAGE — WHY THE PHOTOGRAPH EXISTS CORE

The obvious design would be to read each sensor at the moment the program needs it. Every PLC ever built deliberately does not do that. Instead the operating system copies all inputs into a block of memory called the process image input table, runs your entire program against that frozen copy, collects all results in the process image output table, and only then copies that block out to the terminals. Three reasons, and the first one is not about speed:

  • 1Consistency. A rung near the top and a rung near the bottom both testing the same sensor will always see the same value in one scan. Without a process image, a sensor that changed mid-scan could make a piece of logic contradict itself — the machine would both start and be interlocked, in the same cycle, and no amount of staring at the program would explain it.
  • 2Repeatability. Given the same photograph, the program produces the same answer every time. That is what makes a PLC testable, simulatable and auditable — and it is exactly what a general-purpose computer does not give you.
  • 3Speed. Reading a bit from RAM takes nanoseconds; fetching it across the backplane or a fieldbus takes microseconds to milliseconds. Doing the transfer once in a block rather than thousands of times individually is what makes a millisecond scan possible at all.

That last caption is Fault P2 in one line, and page 10 turns it into a real bug. It is also the escape hatch: most PLCs offer an immediate read or write instruction that bypasses the image and goes straight to the module. It is the right tool for a genuinely time-critical bit — and the wrong tool everywhere else, because it costs bus time and it breaks the consistency guarantee you just paid for.

THE PULSE-CAPTURE RULE, DERIVED PROPERLY CORE

This is the arithmetic behind the cold open, and it is worth being able to do on the back of a work order.

A pulse of width W is sampled once per scan T, at an instant with no fixed relationship to the pulse. If W ≥ T → every pulse is seen. Guaranteed capture. If W < T → seen with probability P = W / T, missed with probability (T − W) / T Line 3: W = 20 mm target / 600 mm·s⁻¹ = 33.3 ms T = 34 ms P(miss) = (34 − 33.3)/34 = 1.96 % → 2.4 bottles per minute at 120 bpm Add the input filter and it is worse: the filter needs the signal stable for its own delay before the bit ever reaches the image, so the usable width is W_eff = W − t_filter = 33.3 − 3 = 30.3 ms P(miss) = (34 − 30.3)/34 = 10.9 % — which is the number actually observed on some shifts Design rule: W ≥ 2T for any signal that must be seen and acted upon reliably. The factor of two buys you the sampling margin and the one-scan action delay together.

Notice the shape of this. It is the same statement as the sampling theorem you met in vibration work — you cannot reliably detect an event shorter than your sampling interval — only here the sampling interval is your own program's length, which means the fault was created by whoever wrote the program, not by the sensor and not by the conveyor.

A limit switch on a rotary indexer is closed for 18 ms per revolution. The PLC scan is 12 ms, the input filter is 4 ms and the program must both see it and energise a relay output. Does this work?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: It captures, but only just, and the action is late. Usable width W_eff = 18 − 4 = 14 ms > T = 12 ms, so every pulse is photographed — the margin is 2 ms, which is not a design margin, it is luck. Against the W ≥ 2T rule you would want 24 ms of usable width. And the response is 4 + 2×12 + 8 = 36 ms after the switch closes, by which time the indexer has moved on. Verdict: reduce the filter to 0.5 ms (giving 17.5 ms usable), or latch the input. If the output must be positioned accurately relative to the index, neither is enough — that is a motion-control job, not a PLC-rung job.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

An electrician replaces a failed proximity sensor with one from the stores. Same size, same 24 V, same M12 connector, correct wiring by colour. The input LED never lights. The sensor is not faulty and the wiring is not wrong. What happened?

Sinking versus sourcing — the single most common I/O mistake in the world. A PNP (sourcing) sensor switches +24 V onto its output wire; it needs an input card whose common is tied to 0 V, so current flows sensor → input → 0 V. An NPN (sinking) sensor switches its output wire to 0 V; it needs a card whose common is tied to +24 V. Put the wrong pair together and the circuit simply never completes — no damage, no light, no clue, and two hours lost.
PNP sensor (sourcing) +24V → [sensor] → signal wire → [input] → card common tied to 0 V ✓ NPN sensor (sinking) card common tied to +24 V → [input] → signal wire → [sensor] → 0 V ✓ PNP sensor + card common on +24 V → both ends try to source; no return path ✗ dead NPN sensor + card common on 0 V → both ends try to sink; no supply path ✗ dead
Regional habit makes this worse: PNP dominates in Europe and therefore in most machinery imported into Sri Lanka, while NPN is common on Japanese and older Asian equipment. A mixed plant has both, in identical-looking barrels, in the same drawer.

OUTPUTS — RELAY OR TRANSISTOR, AND THE DIODE YOU MUST NOT FORGET CORE

Relay output

2 A, 8 ms

Volt-free contact, switches AC or DC, tolerant of anything. Slow, and it wears out — a few hundred thousand operations at load. Use for contactors, lamps, anything switched occasionally.

Transistor output

0.5 A, <1 ms

DC only, polarity fixed, electrically silent, effectively infinite life. Use for solenoid valves, fast pulses, high-cycle machines. Overload it once and it is dead.

  • 1Every DC coil gets a flyback diode, fitted at the coil. An interrupted inductor generates V = −L di/dt — hundreds of volts across a transistor rated for 30. It is the most common way output cards are killed, and the diode costs less than a cup of tea.
  • 2Every AC coil gets an RC snubber or varistor, for the same reason and also to stop the arc radiating noise into the input cards next door.
  • 3Fuse each output group. One shorted valve should not take down a whole card, and the fuse tells you which group in five seconds.
  • 4Check the per-point and per-group current ratings separately. Sixteen points rated 0.5 A each rarely means 8 A per card; the group limit is often 2–4 A. Datasheet, not intuition.
  • 5Never switch a motor directly from an output. The output switches a contactor coil; the contactor switches the motor. Both because of current and because the contactor is where the overload protection lives (A51).

ABSOLUTE ADDRESSES VERSUS SYMBOLS — AND WHY THIS IS A SAFETY ISSUE CORE

You can write a rung as I0.3 AND NOT I0.5 → Q0.2. It works. It is also unreadable at 3 a.m., and the next engineer cannot tell whether I0.5 is a guard door or a jam sensor. Every modern platform lets you attach a symbol — a name — to each address, and then write:

Cap_Sensor AND NOT Reject_Bin_Full → Reject_Blast_Valve instead of I0.3 AND NOT I0.5 → Q0.2

The second version is a puzzle. The first is a sentence. Ladder as a Sentence — the Grammar in Ten Minutes

A RUNG IS AN IF-THEN, WRITTEN SIDEWAYS CORE

Ladder logic has exactly two kinds of element. On the left, contacts, which ask questions about bits. On the right, coils, which set bits. Power — imaginary power, in memory — flows from the left rail to the right. If it gets through, the coil is true. That is the entire language.

Read every rung that way, out loud, from the left: series is AND, parallel is OR, the coil is THEN. A rung you cannot say as one sentence is a rung that is doing too much and should be split.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

An emergency stop button is wired, correctly, as a normally-closed contact. In the ladder program, which instruction should test it — the one that looks normally-closed, or the one that looks normally-open?

EDGE DETECTION — THE INSTRUCTION THAT MAKES THE SCAN USEFUL CORE

Almost every “do this once” requirement — count a part, advance a step, log an event, print a label — needs a signal that is true for exactly one scan when something changes. That is a rising-edge detector, and its internals are two lines of logic worth understanding because you will meet them hand-written in old programs:

Pulse := Signal AND NOT Signal_Last ; (* true only on the scan where Signal became 1 *) Signal_Last := Signal ; (* remember for next scan — MUST be last *) In IEC languages this is the standard function block R_TRIG (rising) and F_TRIG (falling). Rockwell calls the ladder version ONS; Siemens draws it as —|P|— with its own memory bit. The rule that catches everyone: every edge instruction needs its own private memory bit. Re-using one memory bit for two edge detectors means the second one silently never fires.

And now a warning that connects straight back to page 4. An edge detector can only detect an edge that appears in the process image. If the pulse is shorter than the scan, the edge detector never sees it — not because edge detection is broken, but because there was no edge in the photograph to detect. Fault P1 is not a missing R_TRIG. No instruction can recover information that was never sampled.

THE HARD PART NOBODY MENTIONS: THE NOZZLE IS NOT AT THE SENSOR CORE

The cap sensor is at the inspection point. The air-blast nozzle is 900 mm downstream. The belt runs at 600 mm/s. So a bottle inspected now must be rejected 1.5 seconds later — and at 120 bottles a minute there are three other bottles between the sensor and the nozzle at all times.

Transport delay t_d = 900 mm / 600 mm·s⁻¹ = 1.5 s Bottle pitch t_p = 60 s / 120 = 0.5 s Bottles in transit n = t_d / t_p = 3 Naive design: TON of 1.5 s started by a bad cap, then fire the blast. Why it fails: the moment the line stops — jam, break, shift change — the timer keeps running while the bottles do not move. It blasts a bottle that is not there, and three good bottles later. A timer measures time. The problem is about position. Correct design: a shift register clocked once per bottle, not once per second. Each bottle's verdict moves one place along the register every time a bottle leaves the inspection point. When a verdict reaches position 3, that bottle is at the nozzle — whether that took 1.5 s or 4 hours.

This is the single most valuable idea on the page, and it generalises far beyond bottles. Anything that must happen to a moving object downstream of where it was measured is a tracking problem, not a timing problem. Reject stations, print-and-apply labellers, weigh-and-divert lines, oven-exit sorters — all the same shape. Clock the register from the object itself: a bottle-leaving edge, an index pulse, or an encoder if the spacing is not fixed.

The line is stopped for a fifteen-minute tea break with three bottles between sensor and nozzle, one of them capless. What happens on restart with the shift-register design, and what would have happened with the 1.5-second timer?

When the Scan Bites — the Six Faults Only Scanned Machines Have

ORDER OF EXECUTION IS PART OF THE LOGIC CORE

In a relay panel every rung is live simultaneously. In a PLC the rungs are solved one after another, top to bottom, and the order changes the answer. Two programs with identical rungs in different order are different programs — something that has no equivalent in mechanical engineering and which nobody warns beginners about.

A 40-step machine written descending on a 100 ms scan spends 4 seconds per cycle just walking through steps. On a line running 300 parts an hour that is a real capacity loss that no mechanical change will recover. Written ascending, the same machine flashes through its states so fast that an operator watching the HMI sees the sequence jump from 1 to 10 and reports that “the machine skips steps.” Both complaints are the same fact.

A colleague proposes fixing a missed-pulse problem by putting the sensor rung at the very top of the program, arguing it will then be read sooner. Is he right?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: No — and the reason is worth being precise about. His change is harmless and useless. This distinction — between when a value is sampled and when it is used — is the difference between guessing and engineering.

Memory After Power Off — and the Machine That Lunged

FAULT P3 CORE

A short power dip during a thunderstorm — four cycles, long enough to drop the PLC, short enough that the lights barely flickered. When power returned the case-packer's arm, physically parked at home, drove immediately to the position it would have occupied at step 6, into a stack of cartons that were no longer where it thought they were. Nobody was hurt. The reason was one checkbox.

RIDE-THROUGH, WARM AND COLD RESTART STRETCH

Two more distinctions that appear in vendor documentation and confuse people:

  • 1Hold-up time. A good 24 V industrial supply rides through a mains dip of roughly 10–20 ms — one cycle at 50 Hz is 20 ms — so brief dips never reach the CPU. Sizing the supply with headroom buys ride-through for free; running it at 95 % of rating throws that away. If dips are frequent, a 24 V DC UPS module is a few thousand rupees and eliminates a class of nuisance stops.
  • 2Warm restart resumes with retentive data intact; cold restart clears everything to initial values. Some CPUs choose automatically based on how long power was lost or whether the battery held. Know which one your machine does, because the safe design must be correct in both cases — which, if you follow the retain rules above, it automatically is.
  • 3Homing is not optional. On any machine with axes, the honest startup sequence is: power up → everything invalid → operator selects manual → home each axis → only then allow auto. It feels slow on a Monday morning. It is the difference between a machine that starts and a machine that lunges.

A pump station must not lose its run-hours counter, and must not restart automatically after a power cut — but it also must resume automatically after a controlled stop from SCADA. How do you satisfy all three?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Separate the three ideas into three variables with three different lifetimes. (1) Run_Hours is RETAIN — it describes the past, and updating it once a minute rather than every scan protects flash endurance. (2) Run_Command is non-retain and is explicitly cleared in the first scan, so a power cut can never restart the pump — IEC 60204-1 satisfied. (3) The SCADA Auto_Enable is RETAIN, because a controlled stop is a deliberate operator decision that should survive; on power-up the program then sees Auto_Enable true, but Restart_Required is also true from the first scan, so it waits for a local acknowledgement before starting. The pattern generalises: retain the intent, never the command, and always insert a human between a power cut and the first movement.

A TWELVE-POINT REVIEW CHECKLIST CORE

  • 3Every timer preset has a stated reason, and none is shorter than 10 × the scan time.

During commissioning, an engineer forces a guard-door input true so he can run the machine with the door open while adjusting a gripper. Three months later, what state is that force in?

Still active — which is exactly Fault P4. A force overrides the process image at the hardware boundary: the physical input can say whatever it likes and the program sees the forced value. It is independent of your laptop, it stays when you disconnect, and on many CPUs — including the S7-200 family, where forced values are stored with the program and system blocks in permanent memory and can even be copied from a memory cartridge — it survives a power cycle and a download.

The force defeated the program's interlock, not the safety function. FORCING — RULES THAT ARE NOT NEGOTIABLE CORE
  • 1A force is a temporary lie told to the program. It is legitimate for testing an output before field wiring exists, or proving a rung during commissioning. It is never a fix.
  • 2Write it down before you apply it — a force register at the panel with tag, value, reason, who, when, and when it will be removed. Paper on the panel door works; nothing works better.
  • 3Never force anything protective: guard switches, e-stop feedback, overloads, level trips, pressure trips. If the machine cannot be commissioned without defeating a guard, the correct answer is a documented hold-to-run mode with reduced speed, not a force.
  • 4The FORCE LED is a stop-work condition on any machine you are about to work on. Check it before you put a hand anywhere — the same reflex as proving a circuit dead before touching it.
  • 5Clear all forces before handover, verify the force list is empty, and record that verification. Add it to the acceptance checklist so somebody else has to look too.

There is a milder cousin worth knowing: many platforms distinguish a force from a temporary value modification, which is written once and then immediately overwritten by the program on the next scan. Modification is much safer and is often all you actually needed — but it is also useless for holding an input, which is precisely why people reach for forces instead.

FIVE LAYERS, AND THE ONE THAT MUST STAND ALONE CORE

A PLC is never alone. Below it are sensors and actuators; beside it is a safety system that must not depend on it; above it are the panel, the plant network and eventually a business system that wants to know how many bottles you made. Knowing which layer a problem lives in is half of solving it.

THE DIAGNOSTIC LADDER — PRINT THIS CORE

A general order of attack for “the machine is doing something strange.” It works because it moves from cheap and broad to expensive and narrow, and because it refuses to open the laptop until the free evidence is exhausted.

  • 1Look at the CPU LEDs. RUN? ERROR? FORCE? Five seconds, and it settles whether you are chasing logic or hardware — or a lie.
  • 2Read the diagnostic buffer. The CPU has already recorded what it objected to, with a timestamp. People skip this and then spend an hour discovering what it says.
  • 3Look at the I/O LEDs against reality. Field device on but LED dark → wiring, common or sensor type. LED lit but nothing moves → fuse, load or force. This splits the machine in half before you think.
  • 4Read the scan time — current, min and max. Anything above about a third of the watchdog, or a max far above the average, is a finding in itself.
  • 5Go online and find the false condition. The first false contact in the rung is the fault, or names the tag that leads to it.
  • 6Cross-reference that tag. Who writes it, and how many writers are there? This is where double coils, forgotten routines and mode conflicts confess.
  • 7Only now question the program's design — timing, sequence, tracking, retention. And when you change something, change one thing and write it in the change log.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — A SIGNAL A PLC SIMPLY CANNOT HAVE CORE

The labeller uses a registration mark to align print. The mark is 8 mm long and the web runs at 1.2 m/s. The PLC scan is 12 ms. Can the PLC read the mark? If not, what is the correct answer?

Pulse width W = 8 mm / 1200 mm·s⁻¹ = 6.67 ms Capture W < T, so P(miss) = (12 − 6.67)/12 = 44.4 % Nearly half the marks are invisible. And the ones that are seen arrive with up to 12 ms of quantisation error = 14 mm of web position. Required scan for the W ≥ 2T rule: T ≤ W/2 = 3.33 ms Is 3.33 ms achievable? The comms slice alone was 16 ms. Even a stripped program on a fast CPU rarely holds 3 ms with a network attached — and it would have to hold it in the worst scan, not the average. The requirement is outside what a scanned program can do. Correct answers, in order of preference 1. Use the drive's or the labeller's own registration input — hardware latch, microseconds 2. Use a high-speed / interrupt input on the PLC: the event is captured outside the scan 3. Use a pulse-catch / latched input module: the pulse is held until the next READ phase 4. Change the physics: a longer mark or a slower web — usually unacceptable commercially

Discussion. This is the most valuable judgement in the whole primer, and it is a judgement about where a function belongs, not about programming skill. A scanned controller is the right tool for machine logic and the wrong tool for events measured in single milliseconds. Recognising the boundary early — at specification, not at commissioning — is what separates an engineer from a person who writes rungs. The same reasoning sends current control to the drive, position loops to the servo (A09), and microsecond switching to the power stage (A29).

WORKED EXAMPLE 3 — HOW LONG SHOULD THE SHIFT REGISTER BE? CORE

The reject nozzle is 900 mm downstream of the cap sensor. The line runs 120 bpm at 600 mm/s. Management then asks for 150 bpm. Does the register length change? What about a product changeover to a different bottle pitch?

At 120 bpm bottle pitch p = v / rate = 600 mm·s⁻¹ / 2 s⁻¹ = 300 mm register length n = distance / pitch = 900 / 300 = 3 At 150 bpm (the belt is sped up to 750 mm/s to keep the same spacing) p = 750 / 2.5 = 300 mm n = 900 / 300 = 3 — unchanged Why: n = d / p = d / (v / r) = d · r / v Speeding the line up increases r and v together, so n is untouched. The register length is a geometric property — how many bottles fit between two points — not a speed property. A timer-based design would have needed re-tuning; the tracking design needs nothing. Now the changeover. A larger bottle at 500 mm pitch: n = 900 / 500 = 1.8 — not an integer, so no fixed-length register is correct. The verdict for a bottle would arrive either 0.2 of a pitch early or 0.8 late, and the blast would clip the wrong bottle. Three legitimate answers: a) make the pitch a mechanical constant (star wheel, screw, indexing) — best if possible b) track on an encoder: store the encoder count at inspection, fire when count + 900 mm is reached. Distance-based, works at any pitch, any speed, any stop c) move the nozzle so that d is an exact multiple of every pitch you will run — rarely possible

Discussion. Option (b) is how every serious reject, print-and-apply and divert system in the world works, and it is worth recognising the pattern: an encoder converts the problem from “when” to “where,” and “where” survives stops, speed changes and product changeovers. The moment a machine has more than one product, distance-based tracking stops being sophistication and becomes the minimum competent design.

WORKED EXAMPLE 4 — A SEQUENCE THAT COSTS 25 % OF THE LINE CORE

A 40-step case packer runs on a 100 ms task, with its step rungs written in descending order — step 40's rung first, step 1's rung last. The mechanical cycle time is 12 s. The customer complains of low throughput and blames the mechanics.

Descending order → exactly one step transition per scan (page 10) Scan penalty = steps × T = 40 × 100 ms = 4.0 s per cycle Actual cycle = 12.0 + 4.0 = 16.0 s Throughput = 3600/16.0 = 225 cases/h against 3600/12.0 = 300 cases/h Loss = 25 %, entirely in software Two independent fixes, and they compound Re-order the step rungs ascending → the whole sequence can advance in one scan Move the sequence to a 20 ms task → penalty 40 × 20 ms = 0.8 s even if left descending Either alone recovers most of it. Both together: penalty → ~0.02 s, cycle → 12.02 s, 299 cases/h. The check nobody does: measure the cycle with a stopwatch, then compute the sum of the mechanical motions. If the machine is slower than the sum of its parts, the difference is almost always scan-related, and no amount of pneumatics tuning will find it.

Discussion. Note who gets blamed. A 25 % throughput shortfall reads, to everyone in the plant, as a mechanical problem — slow cylinders, weak air, sticky guides — and weeks disappear into flow controls and regulators. The stopwatch-versus-sum-of-motions check costs ten minutes and points straight at the controller. Carry it: it is the mechanical engineer's version of reading the scan time.

PROBLEM SET — TEN PROBLEMS, ALL FROM THIS LINE CORE

Work each with a pencil before revealing. Line data: 120 bpm, 600 mm/s, scan 34 ms before the fix and 18 ms after, downtime LKR 22,000/hour, contribution LKR 12/bottle.

P1 — capture. A proximity switch on a rotating shaft is made for 25 ms per revolution. The scan is 20 ms and the input filter is 3 ms. What fraction of revolutions is missed, and does the answer satisfy good practice?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: W_eff = 25 − 3 = 22 ms > T = 20 ms, so none are missed — capture is guaranteed. But W/T = 1.10, far below the ≥ 2 target, so any future increase in scan time — one more HMI client, one more comms connection — breaks it silently. This is a latent fault, not a working design. Either latch the input or hold the scan under 11 ms with a documented watchdog margin.

P2 — the NC trap. A programmer replaces —| Guard_Closed |— with —|/| Guard_Closed |— “because the switch is normally closed.” Describe exactly what the machine now does.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: The logic is inverted: the machine will now run only when the guard is open, and will stop when it is closed. In practice the symptom is reported as “the machine will not start,” somebody props the guard open to test it, the machine starts, and the conclusion drawn is that the guard switch is faulty. The dangerous part is not the fault — it is the diagnosis it invites. The correct test is to watch the input bit in the monitor table while opening and closing the door, and confirm 1 = closed before touching any rung.

P3 — counter type. A production counter is declared INT and counts every bottle at 120 bpm. When does it fail, what does the HMI show, and why does the failure time move around?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: 32767 / 120 = 273.1 min = 4 h 33 min of running after the last reset. The next bottle makes it −32768, so the HMI shows a large negative total and every “count ≥ batch size” comparison becomes false, stalling the batch. It moves around because the clock starts at the last reset, which depends on breakdowns, changeovers and shift habits — making it look like a random daily fault. As DINT the same counter runs 34 years. Rule: every counter and totaliser is DINT, always, with no exceptions worth arguing about.

P4 — scaling. A 4–20 mA pressure transmitter spans 0–10 bar into a Siemens card (0–27648). Raw reads 20736. What is the pressure? Then explain why (raw * 1000) / 27648 in INT arithmetic gives nonsense.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: 20736 × 10 / 27648 = 7.50 bar. The naive integer expression fails because 20736 × 1000 = 20,736,000, far beyond the INT limit of 32,767 — the intermediate result overflows before the division ever happens, and the answer is garbage that changes sign unpredictably. Three cures: promote to DINT or REAL before multiplying; divide before multiplying where precision allows; or use the platform's SCALE/NORM function blocks, which is what they exist for. General habit: check the largest intermediate value in any integer expression, not just the result.

P5 — response budget. A safety-related-looking (but standard-PLC) interlock must stop a conveyor within 250 ms of a trip switch operating. Scan 34 ms, input filter 3 ms, relay output 8 ms, contactor 25 ms, mechanical braking 90 ms. Does it pass — and is the question even legitimate?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: 3 + 2×34 + 8 + 25 + 90 = 194 ms — it passes with 56 ms of margin, and would fail if the scan ever reached 62 ms. But the question is illegitimate as posed. If a stopping time is a safety requirement, it must not be met by a standard PLC scan at all; it belongs to a safety relay or safety controller with a rated response time and a rated failure rate, and the calculation belongs in the safety function's verification (A52, and A35 for the distance calculation that usually accompanies it). Answering the arithmetic without challenging the architecture is the mistake.

P6 — double coil, in reverse. An output's rung is visibly false online, yet the field device is energised and the output LED is lit. The force list is empty. Give three explanations.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: (1) A second writer later in the scan — another coil, a MOVE into the output byte, or a block that writes a whole word of outputs at once. Cross-reference finds it, and remember that a word-level write is easy to miss when you search only for the bit. (2) A jumped or disabled section left the bit at its last value — nothing is driving it false, so it simply stays. (3) Different task periods: the rung you are watching is in a 200 ms task while the real writer sits in a 10 ms task, so the screen is showing you an old, irrelevant truth. Note that all three are order or scope problems — none is a fault in the rung you are staring at, which is why staring harder never works.

P7 — retention. Classify each as retentive or not, with a reason: (a) total bottles produced, (b) current sequence step, (c) selected recipe number, (d) “axis homed” flag, (e) blade-change countdown, (f) the run command from SCADA.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: (a) Retain — describes the past; update once a minute rather than every scan to protect flash endurance. (b) Never — it is a command about the future, and after a power cut the machine's position is unknown. This is Fault P3. (c) Retain — an operator decision, and losing it causes a wrong-product start, which is its own hazard. (d) Never — position validity cannot survive a power cut; the axis must re-home and prove it. (e) Retain — consumable wear is physical and genuinely accumulated. (f) Never — retaining a run command is precisely the automatic restart that IEC 60204-1 prohibits. The one-line test: could restoring this value make the machine move before a human agreed? Then it must not be retentive.

P8 — economics. The false-reject rate is 1.96 %. Show the monthly cost at 20 productive hours/day and 26 days/month, and compare it with two hours of a controls engineer at LKR 9,000/hour.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: 120 × 0.0196 = 2.35 bottles/min → ×60×20 = 2,822/day → ×26 = 73,382/month → × LKR 12 = LKR 880,600/month. Two hours of engineering costs LKR 18,000, a return of roughly 49:1 in the first month and far more thereafter. The point of the problem is not the ratio — it is that nobody computed it for eleven weeks. A 2 % loss is invisible on a shift report and enormous on a P&L, and the person best placed to notice is whoever can multiply a reject rate by a contribution margin.

P9 — task design. You must add vibration monitoring that reads eight analogue channels, computes RMS over 100 samples and writes to the historian. Where does it go, and what must you avoid?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: Not in the main task. Put it in a slow cyclic task (200 ms–1 s) or, better, off the PLC entirely on an edge device (A36, A42) — a PLC scan is the wrong sampling instrument for vibration anyway, since anything above about 10 Hz is already aliased at typical scan rates. Avoid: a 100-iteration loop in the every-scan task (this is exactly Worked Example 1's mistake); writing to persistent memory or a file every scan; and any blocking communications call. If you must keep it in the PLC, spread the work — process ten samples per scan rather than a hundred — so the worst-case scan does not spike into the watchdog.

P10 — synthesis. You are handed a new machine: 60 I/O, a 12-step sequence, one reject station 1.2 m downstream, a guard door, and an HMI. Write the specification for the controller and the program structure, in the order you would actually do it.

Tap to reveal ↓ A: 1. Safety first, and separately. Risk assessment, then the guard and e-stop on a hard-wired safety relay or safety controller with the required performance level. It reports its status to the PLC and takes nothing from it. Nothing else is designed until this is settled. 2. I/O schedule and symbol list from the electrical drawings, with healthy states stated explicitly for every protective device. 3. Timing requirements: list every signal with its pulse width, apply W ≥ 2T, and identify anything that must leave the scan (high-speed counters, registration, interrupts). That fixes your scan-time budget and therefore your CPU class — do it before you buy. 4. Tracking: the reject station is 1.2 m downstream, so specify the pitch and decide shift register versus encoder now; ask whether more than one product will run, because the answer is always yes eventually. 5. Program structure as page 12: safety/mode, input scaling, manual, sequence (one CASE, one writer of the step number, a timeout with a diagnostic string on every step), outputs block as the sole writer of every coil, alarms, HMI. 6. Startup block: clear commands, invalidate positions, Restart_Required. 7. Retention policy written down, tested by pulling the plug during a cycle and confirming that nothing moves on restoration. 8. Handover: backups in three places, force list empty and verified, scan time recorded with watchdog margin, change log at the panel, and the twelve-point checklist signed. The order matters more than any individual item — every one of the four Line 3 faults came from a step that was skipped, not from a step done badly.
KEY DERIVATIONS — THREE RESULTS WORTH BEING ABLE TO REBUILD STRETCH
D1 — the capture probability, and where W ≥ 2T comes from The scan samples an input at one instant per period T. The pulse arrives with no fixed phase relationship to the scan, so the sampling instant φ is uniformly distributed on [0, T). A pulse of width W starting at phase φ is captured ⇔ the sampling instant falls inside it. Measured over one period, the favourable set has measure min(W, T). P(capture) = min(W, T) / T → P(miss) = max(0, T − W) / T Guaranteed capture therefore needs only W ≥ T. So why does the working rule say 2T? Because capture is not the requirement — acting is. After the pulse is photographed, the program must solve and the WRITE phase must occur, which costs up to one more scan, and the worst case adds the input filter: T_response(worst) = t_filter + 2T + t_output Requiring that the response completes while the event is still physically relevant — the bottle still in front of the nozzle, the mark still under the head — gives W ≥ 2T as the smallest ratio that leaves the system any margin at all. It is an engineering rule with the same character as a factor of safety: not a theorem, but the number that stops you living on the edge of a cliff you cannot see. D2 — why the shift-register length does not depend on line speed pitch p = v / r (v = belt speed, r = parts per unit time) length n = d / p = d · r / v (d = sensor-to-nozzle distance) For a line whose spacing is fixed by the machine (star wheel, screw, indexer), increasing throughput means increasing v and r together with r/v constant, so: ∂n/∂v |_(r/v const) = 0 → n is invariant under speed change n changes only if d changes (move the nozzle) or if p changes (different product). This is why tracking designs survive speed trials and timer designs do not — and why the changeover, not the speed-up, is what breaks them. When p is not constant, n is not an integer and no fixed register is correct; the problem must be re-expressed in distance, which is exactly what an encoder provides: fire when (count_now − count_at_inspection) × mm/count ≥ d. D3 — the sequence-order penalty, and the two ways to attack it Let a sequence have S steps, running in a task of period T. Ascending rung order: a step's transition is evaluated after the rung that set it, so several transitions can occur in one scan → penalty → ~T total. Descending rung order: a step's transition rung is evaluated before the rung that would advance it again → exactly one transition per scan. t_penalty(descending) = S × T Throughput loss L = S·T / (t_mech + S·T) Case packer: S = 40, T = 100 ms, t_mech = 12 s → L = 4/16 = 25 % Two independent levers, and they multiply: reduce S·T by re-ordering (S·T → ~T, a factor of 40 here) or by moving to a faster task (T = 100 → 20 ms, a factor of 5). The lesson is not that descending order is wrong — it is sometimes deliberately chosen so that every state is observable on the HMI — but that the choice has a price, and the price is computable.
SEVEN TRAPS THAT CATCH EVERYONE ONCE CORE
  • 1Instruction type versus device type. A healthy NC field device reads as 1, so it is tested with the “normally open” instruction. Invert this and the machine runs with the guard open.
  • 2Two coils, one address. The last write in the scan wins and the earlier rung is silently discarded. Cross-reference before you theorise.
  • 3Timers are tested once per scan. A preset of 50 ms on a 34 ms scan elapses between 50 and 84 ms. Never ask for a preset under 10 × the scan.
  • 4Levels are not edges. IF sensor THEN count := count + 1 counts once per scan, not once per part — and the error scales with line speed.
  • 5INT is 32,767. Counters and totalisers are DINT; check the largest intermediate value in every integer expression, not just the result.
  • 6Forces survive everything. Going offline, power cycling, often downloads and memory-card transfers. The FORCE LED is a stop-work condition.
  • 7Retained step numbers restart machinery. Retain what describes the past; never what commands the future. IEC 60204-1 is not optional about this.
