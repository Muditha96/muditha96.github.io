Seeing Heat Instead of Light

A thermal camera does not measure temperature. It measures infrared radiation — and everything in this module flows from that distinction.

Definition

Infrared thermography is the non-contact measurement and mapping of surface temperature by detecting the thermal radiation every object above absolute zero emits, converting radiance into a calibrated temperature image called a thermogram.

Every surface warmer than 0 K radiates electromagnetic energy. At everyday temperatures (−20 °C to a few hundred °C) that radiation peaks in the long-wave infrared, around 8–14 µm — a wavelength our eyes cannot see but a microbolometer detector can. The camera converts the radiance arriving at each pixel into an apparent temperature, then paints the result with a false-colour palette.

Why does industry care? Because almost every failure mode announces itself thermally first: a loose lug heats from I²R, a failing bearing heats from friction, a blocked cooling channel heats from lost convection, a failed steam trap changes the temperature signature of the line. Thermography lets you find all of this from a distance, energized, at line speed — the ultimate non-intrusive condition-monitoring tool (the P in the P-F curve of A23 TPM).

Where infrared sits — the electromagnetic spectrum

Thermal cameras work in atmospheric "windows" where air is transparent to IR: 3–5 µm (MWIR) and 8–14 µm (LWIR).

The Physics: Planck, Wien & Stefan-Boltzmann

Three equations from 1900 explain everything your camera does.

Blackbody radiation — the three laws

A blackbody is a perfect emitter (ε = 1). Planck's law gives its full emission spectrum, Wien's law locates the peak, and Stefan-Boltzmann integrates the total power.

Planck: M(λ,T) = (2πhc²/λ⁵) · 1/(e^(hc/λkT) − 1) [W·m⁻²·µm⁻¹] Wien: λ_max = 2898 / T → 300 K ⇒ 9.66 µm · 1000 K ⇒ 2.9 µm Stefan-Boltzmann: M = σ·T⁴, σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴ (grey body: M = ε·σ·T⁴)

Two consequences run the whole trade. First, T⁴ is brutal: doubling absolute temperature multiplies radiated power sixteen-fold — which is why hot faults "glow" so obviously against background. Second, Wien's shift explains camera bands: room-temperature plant peaks near 10 µm (buy LWIR), while furnaces and molten metal shift toward 1–3 µm (short-wave furnace cameras see through flames).

Blackbody curves — hotter is higher AND bluer

Planck curves never cross: a hotter body out-radiates a colder one at every wavelength, and its peak slides to shorter λ (Wien).

Emissivity — Why the Camera Lies

The single largest error source in thermography. Master this page and you are ahead of half the people holding cameras.

The measurement equation

The camera receives three radiation streams, not one: emitted by the target, reflected from the surroundings, and transmitted through the atmosphere.

W_received = ε·τ·W(T_obj) + (1−ε)·τ·W(T_refl) + (1−τ)·W(T_atm) emitted (signal) reflected (noise) atmosphere (small at <10 m)

Kirchhoff's law says for an opaque surface ε + ρ = 1: what a surface doesn't emit, it reflects. A polished aluminium busbar with ε ≈ 0.05 is a 95 % mirror in the infrared — the "temperature" you read off it is mostly a reflection of the room, the sky, or your own body heat. To solve the equation for the true object temperature, you must enter ε and the reflected apparent temperature T_refl into the camera. Garbage in, garbage out.

Typical emissivity values (8–14 µm, normal viewing)

Field fixes for low-ε surfaces: electrical tape (ε ≈ 0.95), flat paint patch, a drilled cavity (acts like a blackbody, ε → 0.98), or measure an adjacent oxidised/painted part of the same joint. For reflected temperature: crumpled-then-flattened aluminium foil at the target plane acts as a diffuse IR mirror — read its apparent temperature and enter that as T_refl.

Inside the Camera

Uncooled microbolometers own the plant floor; cooled photon detectors own the lab. Know which physics you are buying.

Microbolometer — the industrial workhorse

Each pixel of an uncooled microbolometer is a microscopic resistor on a MEMS bridge (vanadium oxide or amorphous silicon). Absorbed IR warms the bridge a few millikelvin; its resistance change is read out as signal.

Uncooled vs cooled — selection table

NETD (noise-equivalent temperature difference) is the smallest ΔT the detector can resolve above its own noise — the "sensitivity" spec. For finding a 20 K electrical fault, 50 mK is plenty. For medical or building science where 0.5 K matters, buy better NETD. Key spec set: resolution × NETD × temperature range × lens FOV — in that order of scrutiny.

Resolution: IFOV & the Spot-Size Rule

The most common quantitative error after emissivity: measuring a target smaller than the camera can actually resolve.

IFOV — instantaneous field of view

Each pixel sees a cone of angle IFOV = FOV / n_pixels. At distance d, that cone covers a spot S = IFOV × d. If your target is smaller than the measurement spot, the pixel averages target with background — and under-reads hot spots.

IFOV (mrad) ≈ FOV(°) × 17.45 / n_pixels_horizontal Spot size S = IFOV × d (geometric — detection limit) Measurement spot ≈ 3 × IFOV × d (MFOV: need ~3×3 px on target for a true reading)

Example: a 320×240 camera with a 24° lens → IFOV = 24×17.45/320 ≈ 1.3 mrad. At 10 m the geometric spot is 13 mm, but a trustworthy temperature needs the target to fill ≈ 39 mm. A 10 mm cable lug at 10 m will look warm but read tens of degrees low. Fix: get closer, use a telephoto lens, or buy more pixels. Distance-to-spot thinking is mandatory for outdoor substation work.

Focus, range & palette — the other three image killers

🎯 Focus

An out-of-focus thermogram cannot be fixed later — blur spreads radiance across pixels and lowers every hot-spot reading. Focus is a radiometric parameter, not cosmetics.

📏 Range

Saturated pixels clip at range top: a 400 °C fault on a 150 °C range reads "150 °C". Pick the range before judging severity.

🎨 Palette & span

Iron/rainbow/grey change perception, not data. Manually narrow the span around the target to reveal subtle ΔT — auto-span hides faults in busy scenes.

Electrical Inspection — The Bread & Butter

Loose connections don't fail quietly. They cook — following I²R — and thermography catches them months before the flashover.

The physics of a bad joint

A degraded connection (loose, corroded, work-hardened) raises contact resistance R. Power dissipated is P = I²R — heating scales with the square of load current.

P_joint = I² · R_contact → doubling load ⇒ 4× the heat ΔT_normalized ≈ ΔT_measured × (I_rated / I_measured)² (normalize findings to full load!)

The load-normalization line is the professional's edge: a joint showing a mild +8 °C at 30 % load projects to roughly +89 °C at full load (×(100/30)² ≈ ×11). Always record load current with every electrical finding, and never survey below ~40 % load if you can help it. Compare like phases under the same load — a healthy three-phase set should be thermally symmetric; one warm phase is either load imbalance (check clamp meter) or a genuine defect.

Safety — thermography does not repeal arc-flash rules
  • 1Opening energized panels is the hazard, not the camera. Follow NFPA 70E / EN 50110 arc-flash procedures, PPE categories and approach boundaries.
  • 2IR windows (crystal or polymer viewports) let you survey closed panels. Note: window transmission τ < 1 — enter it in the camera or apply the correction factor, or every reading is low.
  • 3Never survey alone in HV areas; the thermographer's eyes are on the screen, not the surroundings — bring a qualified escort.

Buildings, Electronics & R&D

The same physics, gentler temperatures — where NETD and technique matter more than range.

Building envelope

Building thermography hunts ΔT of 1–5 °C: insulation voids, thermal bridges, air leakage and moisture. It needs an inside–outside temperature difference of at least ~10 °C and no direct sun on the façade.

🧱 Thermal bridges

Studs, lintels, slab edges conduct heat past insulation — regular cold stripes inside, warm stripes outside. Geometry repeats; defects don't.

💨 Air leakage

With a blower door depressurizing the building, infiltrating cold air paints feathery streaks at frames, penetrations and sockets — unmistakable versus conduction.

💧 Moisture

Wet material has huge thermal capacity and evaporative cooling — flat roofs surveyed at dusk show wet insulation as warm islands (heat stored from the day).

📋 Standard

ISO 6781 / EN 13187 govern qualitative envelope thermography — conditions, viewing, and reporting requirements.

Beware solar loading: a façade in morning sun stores hours of radiant energy and will mask (or fake) every defect. Survey pre-dawn, after sustained overcast, or from the inside.

Electronics & development benches

PCB debugging: power a board and the thermogram instantly maps the current — a shorted decoupling cap or reversed diode lights up seconds after power-on, before it dies. Close-up lenses resolve 25–100 µm per pixel for component-level work. Remember shiny solder and bare copper are low-ε: a thin matt lacquer or dye evens the surface for quantitative work.

R&D uses: motor and drive thermal validation against insulation class limits (Class B 130 °C / F 155 °C / H 180 °C — tie-in A08 VFD derating), battery pack gradient mapping (→ A39: cell ΔT target <5 °C), weld nugget cooling analysis, injection-mould cooling balance, and active thermography (pulsed/lock-in): flash-heat a composite and watch the cooling transient — subsurface voids and delaminations change the local cooling curve. That is NDT with a camera.

Survey Practice, Standards & Certification

Cameras don't produce findings — disciplined thermographers do.

The route discipline
  • 1Baseline first. Shoot every asset healthy, at known load, from a marked position and angle. All future severity is relative to this.
  • 2Fix the variables. Record load %, ambient temp, wind, and distance with every image. ΔT without load context is noise. Wind above ~5 m/s halves outdoor hot-spot ΔT.
  • 3ε and T_refl before reading. Set both per target (page 3). For comparative phase-to-phase work, identical wrong ε still ranks correctly — but absolute values need the full correction.
  • 4Verify spot size (page 5) — get closer rather than zooming the display; display zoom adds nothing radiometrically.
  • 5Report for action: IR + visual image pair, location, load, ΔT, severity class, recommended action & deadline, and the repair-verification re-scan. A finding without a re-scan after repair is an open loop.

Certification levels mirror vibration analysis (same ISO 18436 family): Cat I collects on defined routes, Cat II sets criteria and diagnoses, Cat III designs the programme. For a plant PdM team, one Cat II leading several Cat I surveyors is the standard structure.

Fixed & automated thermography

The route camera is going permanent: fixed radiometric cameras now watch critical switchgear, conveyor bearings, storage silos and battery rooms 24/7, streaming per-ROI temperatures over Modbus TCP, REST or MQTT into the historian (→ A06 OPC-UA, A33 IIoT stack). Alarm logic = absolute limit + rate-of-rise + comparison between ROIs. Early-fire detection (waste bunkers, coal, biomass) is the same camera with different alarm mathematics. This turns thermography from a monthly snapshot into a continuous condition signal — the logical end state for critical assets.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

The whole module in one page — then prove it.

The eight ideas that matter
  • 1The camera measures radiation, not temperature — you supply ε, T_refl and distance to make it temperature.
  • 2Stefan-Boltzmann T⁴ makes hot faults loud; Wien puts room-temperature plant in LWIR 8–14 µm.
  • 3ε + ρ = 1: shiny metal is an IR mirror. Tape it, paint it, or measure the oxidised part.
  • 4Microbolometer for the plant, cooled MWIR for speed & science. NETD is the sensitivity spec.
  • 5Spot-size rule: need ~3×3 pixels on the target or the reading is fiction.
  • 6Electrical severity = ΔT at known load, normalized by (I_rated/I_meas)² — phase-to-phase and vs-ambient.
  • 7Mechanical/process work is pattern vs baseline — thermography locates, vibration & oil analysis confirm.
  • 8Programme > camera: routes, baselines, load records, re-scans — ISO 18434 / 18436 structure.
Six classic thermography failures
🪞 The reflection ghost

A "hot spot" that moves when you move = your own body or a lamp reflected off low-ε metal. Change angle before believing any shiny-surface finding.

🕶️ The low-ε under-read

Shiny busbar at 120 °C reads 40 °C with ε=0.95 set. The fault is invisible until the flashover. Tape/paint targets or use cavity readings.

📏 Too far away

Target smaller than the measurement spot averages with background — a 10 mm lug at 10 m on 320 px reads tens of degrees low. Distance-to-spot first.

🌞 Solar & wind lies

Morning sun stores heat that fakes faults; wind >5 m/s strips ΔT and hides real ones. Note both; survey at night/calm when severity matters.

📉 Light-load all-clear

Panel surveyed at 20 % load shows nothing; joint fails at full summer load. Normalize by (I_r/I_m)² or re-survey above 40 % load.

🔒 Saturated range

Pixels clipped at range maximum flatten every fault to the same value. If the hottest pixel equals range top, re-shoot on a higher range.

🧠 Quick Check 1

A polished aluminium bar sits at exactly 100 °C in a 25 °C room. The camera is set to ε = 0.95. Will it read high or low, and why?

Tap to reveal Low — drastically. With ε ≈ 0.05 the bar emits almost nothing; 95 % of received radiation is the 25 °C room reflected. Telling the camera ε = 0.95 makes it interpret that weak signal as the object's emission, so it reports close to room temperature. Kirchhoff: ε + ρ = 1.
🧠 Quick Check 2

Why do plant thermal cameras use the 8–14 µm band rather than, say, 6–7 µm?

Tap to reveal Two reasons: Wien's law puts the emission peak of ~300 K surfaces near 9.7 µm, and the atmosphere is transparent in the 8–14 µm window (water vapour absorbs heavily around 6–7 µm). Signal is maximal exactly where air doesn't eat it.
🧠 Quick Check 3

A breaker lug shows ΔT = +6 °C at 25 % load. Roughly what ΔT does that predict at full load, and what severity class is it really?

Tap to reveal P = I²R ⇒ scale by (100/25)² = 16× → ≈ +96 °C at rated load. What looked like a "possible deficiency" is a major deficiency. This is why load current must be recorded with every electrical finding.
🧠 Quick Check 4

Your 320-px camera has a 24° lens (IFOV ≈ 1.3 mrad). What is the largest distance for a valid reading on a 13 mm bolt head?

Tap to reveal Measurement spot ≈ 3 × IFOV × d ≤ 13 mm ⇒ d ≤ 0.013 / (3 × 0.0013) ≈ 3.3 m. Beyond that the pixel averages bolt with background and under-reads. Detection is possible farther out — measurement is not.
🧠 Quick Check 5

Both sides of a steam trap image at nearly the same hot temperature. Diagnosis?

Tap to reveal Failed open — blowing steam straight through. A healthy trap shows hot inlet and clearly cooler outlet (condensate). Hot–hot = live steam passing (energy loss); cold–cold = failed shut or flooded. Confirm with ultrasound before replacement.
🧠 Quick Check 6

Why is an out-of-focus thermogram a radiometric error, not just an ugly picture?

Tap to reveal Defocus spreads each target's radiance over neighbouring pixels, so hot-spot pixels mix with cooler background — every small hot target reads low, and no software can restore the lost radiance afterwards. Focus, then freeze, then save.

Worked Examples — Full Solutions

Exam-grade numericals, solved step by step. Cover the solution, attempt it, then compare line by line.

WE-1 · Correcting a low-emissivity reading

Problem. A camera set to ε = 0.95 reads an apparent 38.0 °C on an oxidised copper busbar whose true emissivity is ε = 0.60. Reflected apparent temperature T_refl = 22 °C, τ ≈ 1. Find the true surface temperature.

Step 1 — camera's belief → received radiance (blackbody-equivalent, σT⁴ basis): W_rec = ε_set·W(311.15) + (1−ε_set)·W(295.15) = 0.95·σ·311.15⁴ + 0.05·σ·295.15⁴ = σ·(0.95·9.374e9 + 0.05·7.589e9) = σ·9.285e9 Step 2 — invert with the TRUE ε: W_rec = ε_true·W(T_obj) + (1−ε_true)·W(295.15) σ·9.285e9 = 0.60·σ·T_obj⁴ + 0.40·σ·7.589e9 T_obj⁴ = (9.285e9 − 3.036e9)/0.60 = 1.0416e10 T_obj = 319.5 K ≈ 46.4 °C

Insight: the camera under-read by ~8.4 °C. At higher target temperatures the same ε mistake produces far larger errors (T⁴ leverage). Always re-solve — never "add a fudge factor".

WE-2 · Camera selection for a substation survey

Problem. You must measure 25 mm clamp connections from 25 m (minimum safe approach). Camera A: 640×480, 12° lens. Camera B: 320×240, 24° lens. Which (if either) gives a valid measurement?

Camera A: IFOV = 12°×17.45/640 = 0.327 mrad spot @25 m = 0.327×25 = 8.2 mm → measurement spot ≈ 3×8.2 = 24.5 mm ≤ 25 mm ✔ Camera B: IFOV = 24×17.45/320 = 1.31 mrad spot @25 m = 32.7 mm → measurement spot ≈ 98 mm ✘ (4× too large) Camera B's max valid distance: d = 25 mm/(3×1.31 mrad) ≈ 6.4 m — inside the safety boundary → unusable.

Answer: only Camera A. Note the engineering pattern: the safety boundary fixes d, the target fixes S, so resolution × lens is the only free variable. This is how you write a camera procurement spec.

WE-3 · Load-normalized severity classification

Problem. Route survey, MCC feeder: B-phase lug reads 41 °C, A and C read 34 °C, ambient 28 °C. Clamp meter: 120 A on a 400 A frame (30 % load). Classify now and at full load; state the action.

Measured: ΔT_phase = 41−34 = 7 °C · ΔT_amb = 13 °C Table (page 6): 4–15 °C phase → "probable deficiency" — repair at next outage. Normalize to rated load: ΔT_full ≈ 7 × (400/120)² = 7 × 11.1 = 78 °C 78 °C ≫ 15 °C phase-criterion → MAJOR deficiency at working load.

Action: treat as major — schedule urgent repair, do not wait for the annual outage; re-scan after remediation. The severity table is only meaningful after normalization: this single habit separates Category II analysis from snapshot tourism.

WE-4 · Band selection by Wien & signal ratio

Problem. A kiln shell runs ≈ 350 °C. (a) Where does its emission peak? (b) How much more power does a 350 °C hot patch radiate than the 60 °C healthy shell around it (ε equal)? (c) LWIR or MWIR camera?

(a) λ_max = 2898/623.15 = 4.65 µm → peak sits in the MWIR window (b) M₁/M₂ = (623.15/333.15)⁴ = 1.8705⁴ ≈ 12.2× (c) Either band detects easily (12× contrast). MWIR captures the peak and suits high-T ranges; LWIR still works and is cheaper — check the camera's calibrated range covers 350 °C+ and fit a high-T filter if specified.

Insight: band choice is rarely about "can I see it" — it is about calibrated range, atmospheric path, and cost. Reserve cooled MWIR for speed (kHz), long range, or gas imaging.

Problem Set — Test Yourself

Ten problems, roughly in exam order of difficulty. Work on paper first; tap to check. Target: 8/10 before moving on.

P1 · Warm-up

Convert: a surface at 85 °C — what is T⁴ in the Stefan-Boltzmann law? (Spot the trap.)

Tap to check T must be kelvin: (85+273.15)⁴ = 358.15⁴ ≈ 1.645×10¹⁰ K⁴. Using 85⁴ is the single most common exam error in radiation problems. M = εσT⁴ = ε × 5.67×10⁻⁸ × 1.645×10¹⁰ ≈ ε × 933 W/m².
P2 · Wien

A camera optimised for 3.9 µm peak emission is best matched to targets around what temperature?

Tap to check T = 2898/λ_max = 2898/3.9 ≈ 743 K ≈ 470 °C — furnace/kiln territory, hence MWIR high-temperature cameras.
P3 · Kirchhoff

An opaque surface reflects 70 % of incident LWIR. Its emissivity is? And what fraction of the camera signal from it is scene reflection?

Tap to check ε = 1 − ρ = 0.30. Received signal = 0.30·W(T_obj) + 0.70·W(T_refl): 70 % of what the camera sees is the surroundings, weighted by their radiance — measure T_refl or the reading is fiction.
P4 · Radiated power

A joint rises from 40 °C to 80 °C (ε = 0.9). By what factor does its radiated power increase?

Tap to check (353.15/313.15)⁴ = 1.1277⁴ ≈ 1.62×. Only +62 % for +40 °C — near room temperature the T⁴ curve is locally shallow; the camera's NETD (mK-scale) is what makes such faults trivial to see, not raw contrast.
P5 · IFOV

640×480 camera, 45° wide lens, target = 10 mm cable lug. Maximum distance for a valid measurement?

Tap to check IFOV = 45×17.45/640 = 1.227 mrad. d_max = 0.010/(3×0.001227) ≈ 2.7 m. Wide lenses are for context images — switch to tele for measurement.
P6 · Load normalization

ΔT = 4 °C at 50 % load. Projected ΔT at rated current, and severity class before vs after normalization (phase-comparison criteria)?

Tap to check ×(100/50)² = 4× → 16 °C. Raw: 4 °C = "probable deficiency" (low end). Normalized: 16 °C > 15 °C → major deficiency. Classify on normalized values; record load every time.
P7 · Measurement equation

Camera set ε = 0.85 reads 100 °C apparent. True ε = 0.85 but you forgot T_refl and left it at 20 °C default; actual surroundings are a 300 °C furnace wall behind you. Is the true target hotter or cooler than 100 °C?

Tap to check Cooler. The real reflected component (from a 300 °C wall, W huge) is far larger than the camera assumed, so more of the received signal is reflection and less is emission — the camera over-attributed radiance to the target. Reflected-temperature errors flip sign with hot surroundings: under-read in cold rooms, over-read near furnaces.
P8 · NETD vs accuracy

A camera lists NETD 40 mK and accuracy ±2 °C. You measure two adjacent lugs as 61.3 °C and 63.8 °C. Is the 2.5 °C ΔT trustworthy? Is "63.8 °C" trustworthy?

Tap to check The ΔT is trustworthy (same camera, same scene, noise floor 0.04 °C ≪ 2.5 °C). The absolute 63.8 °C is only ±2 °C (calibration uncertainty). This is why comparative thermography is robust: systematic errors cancel in ΔT.
P9 · Pattern diagnosis

A 3-phase cable run is uniformly warm along its whole length on one phase only — no point concentration. Bad joint or load imbalance? What single instrument settles it?

Tap to check Load imbalance — I²R along distributed conductor resistance heats the whole run evenly; a joint fault concentrates at a point and fades along the conductor. A clamp ammeter on each phase settles it in seconds. Camera locates, meter confirms.
P10 · Design problem

Specify a fixed monitoring setup for six busbar joints spread over 1.2 m width, mounted 2 m from the camera position, smallest joint 20 mm. Required: resolution & lens FOV. (Assume 4:3 sensor options 320/640 wide.)

Tap to check FOV must cover 1.2 m @ 2 m → half-angle atan(0.6/2)=16.7° → ≥ 33.4° horizontal (use 45° class). Validity: need 3×IFOV×2 m ≤ 20 mm → IFOV ≤ 3.33 mrad. 45° lens: 320 px → 2.45 mrad ✔ (margin 1.4×); 640 px → 1.23 mrad ✔✔. Choose 640×480 + 45° for margin and per-joint ROI trending. Add τ correction if behind an IR window.

Key Derivations, Symbols & Exam Traps

The theory an MSc examiner expects you to produce from scratch — and the mistakes that cost marks and findings.

Derivation 1 · Wien from Planck (the expected sketch)
M(λ,T) = C₁/[λ⁵(e^{C₂/λT} − 1)], C₁=2πhc², C₂=hc/k Set ∂M/∂λ = 0. With x = C₂/λT the condition reduces to: x·eˣ/(eˣ−1) = 5 ⇒ x ≈ 4.9651 (transcendental, solved numerically) λ_max·T = C₂/4.9651 = (1.4388×10⁻² m·K)/4.9651 = 2.898×10⁻³ m·K ∎ State: differentiate, substitute, transcendental root, constant. That earns the marks.
Derivation 2 · Stefan-Boltzmann from Planck
M(T) = ∫₀^∞ M(λ,T) dλ — substitute x = C₂/λT: M(T) = (C₁T⁴/C₂⁴)·∫₀^∞ x³/(eˣ−1) dx and ∫₀^∞ x³/(eˣ−1) dx = π⁴/15 ⇒ M = σT⁴ with σ = 2π⁵k⁴/(15h³c²) = 5.670×10⁻⁸ W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴ ∎ The π⁴/15 integral (Riemann ζ(4)·Γ(4)) may be quoted, not derived.
Derivation 3 · The measurement equation from energy balance
Opaque surface: α + ρ = 1; Kirchhoff (thermal equilibrium): α = ε ⇒ ρ = 1−ε Radiance toward camera = emitted + reflected ambient: L_surface = ε·L_bb(T_obj) + (1−ε)·L_bb(T_refl) Path through atmosphere (transmittance τ, emitting at T_atm): L_cam = τ·ε·L_bb(T_obj) + τ·(1−ε)·L_bb(T_refl) + (1−τ)·L_bb(T_atm) ∎ Three unknowns you must supply: ε, T_refl, τ(d, humidity). The camera solves for T_obj.

The seven exam & field traps
  • 1Celsius in T⁴. Radiation laws take kelvin. 85⁴ vs 358.15⁴ is a ×300 000 error.
  • 2NETD ≠ accuracy. 40 mK sensitivity coexists with ±2 °C absolute accuracy. ΔT comparisons are precise; absolute values are not.
  • 3ε is not a material constant. It varies with surface finish, oxide, angle (drops steeply past ~60° viewing) and wavelength band. Quote it with conditions.
  • 4Display zoom ≠ optics. Digital zoom adds no pixels on target; only distance or lens changes IFOV.
  • 5Auto-span hides faults. A 300 °C flare in frame compresses a 15 °C fault into one colour step. Lock and narrow the span manually.
  • 6Glass is opaque in LWIR (and transmits in SWIR). You cannot shoot through a window with a plant camera — that "hot machine behind glass" is your own reflection.
  • 7IR windows have τ < 1 (crystal ~0.9, polymer varies with age). Skip the correction and every panel reading is systematically low.

Hands-on ladder — 8 exercises, cheapest camera is fine
  • 1The ε experiment. Mug of hot water; put electrical tape on one side. Read tape vs bare ceramic vs a strip of foil. Explain all three numbers with the measurement equation.
  • 2Measure T_refl properly. Crumple-flatten foil, place at target plane, ε=1, read apparent temperature. Enter it; watch a shiny-target reading move.
  • 3Spot-size proof. Read a 10 mm hot screw head from 0.5 m, then 3 m, then 8 m. Plot indicated T vs distance — you will SEE the averaging error grow.
  • 4Palette & span drill. Same scene: auto-span vs manual 5 °C span around the target. Count the faults visible in each.
  • 5Motor baseline. Thermogram a healthy motor at known load: frame, DE/NDE bearings, coupling. File it as your first baseline record (position, angle, load noted).
  • 6Panel survey (qualified escort, PPE). Survey one MCC section: phase-to-phase comparisons, record load %, classify with the page-6 table, write a one-page report with IR+visual pairs.
  • 7Building drill. On a cold morning, image a wall inside: find studs (regular), find a defect (irregular). Repeat with sun on the façade — document how solar loading destroys the survey.
  • 8Trend one asset for a month. Weekly image, same setup, log max ROI temperature vs load. This tiny dataset is a complete miniature PdM programme (→ A23).
