A Computer That Disappears Into the Machine

An embedded system is a computer designed around ONE job — the machine defines the computer, not the other way round.

Definition

An embedded system is a combination of processor, memory and I/O dedicated to a specific function inside a larger mechanical or electrical system — usually with real-time constraints, a fixed resource budget, and a lifetime measured in decades, not upgrade cycles.

Your laptop runs any program; the controller inside a VFD runs one program, forever, and must never miss a deadline. That single difference drives everything in this module: why microcontrollers have no operating system, why "average speed" is meaningless and worst-case latency is everything, why an SD card is the weakest part on a Raspberry Pi, and why safety functions stay in certified hardware (→ A35) no matter how smart the Pi is.

The compute spectrum — one axis, three species

One axis, three species: as compute power grows, timing determinism shrinks. Industrial design = picking the right point, or combining two (Pi + Pico is a classic pair).

Why the Raspberry Pi matters industrially

Volume

60 M+

boards sold — a consumer-priced computer with industrial-scale supply and documentation.

Compute Module

CM4/CM5

solder-down variants made for OEM carrier boards — the version real products embed.

Lifecycle pledge

≥2036

Raspberry Pi commits long production lifetimes (Pi 5 family ≥ Jan 2036) — essential for machine builders.

Industrialised derivatives close the gap to the control cabinet: Revolution Pi (DIN-rail, 24 V, CE/UL, CODESYS soft-PLC), NetPi, Strato Pi and dozens of CM carrier boards with isolated I/O, RS-485, CAN and dual Ethernet. The Pi itself is the compute engine; industrial hardening lives on the carrier.

Inside the SoC — What Your €80 Buys

A modern board computer is a System-on-Chip (SoC) plus memory plus an I/O companion — know the blocks and you know the limits.

Anatomy of a Raspberry Pi 5

Pi 5 splits the classic SoC: BCM2712 computes, the in-house RP1 chip handles all low-speed I/O over PCIe. Note what's missing — a built-in ADC.

The Pi 5's RP1 is itself a lesson: even a GHz-class Linux computer delegates hard-real-time pin wiggling to a dedicated silicon block — the same layering you should use at system level (Pi for brains, MCU for microseconds).

Memory hierarchy — why "GHz" lies
register ≈ 0.4 ns │ L1 cache ≈ 1–2 ns │ L2/L3 ≈ 5–15 ns DRAM ≈ 80–120 ns │ SD card page ≈ 0.2–2 ms │ network ≈ 0.1–100 ms one cache miss ≈ 200 instructions lost — one SD access ≈ 2,000,000

Real-time code lives in cache and SRAM. Anything that may touch flash, SD or swap can stall for milliseconds — which is why control loops never allocate memory, log to disk, or call the network in the loop path.

Real-Time: The Worst Case Is the Only Case

"Fast" is not "real-time". Real-time means a mathematically bounded response, every single time.

Hard, firm, soft

Hard RT

never

A missed deadline = system failure. Airbags, motor commutation, E-stop chains. MCU or safety PLC territory.

Firm RT

worthless

Late result is useless but not dangerous — a vision trigger that fires after the part passed (→A16).

Soft RT

degraded

Late result loses value gradually — HMI updates, trend logging, dashboards. Plain Linux is fine.

Classify every function before choosing hardware. A machine typically contains all three — and only the hard slice needs deterministic silicon.

Latency & jitter — three software stacks

The x-axis is what matters: not where the peak sits, but where the tail ENDS. Since kernel 6.12, PREEMPT_RT is part of mainline Linux — a real option on the Pi.

Scheduling math — Rate-Monotonic (RMS)

Give every periodic task a priority by rate (shortest period = highest priority). Liu & Layland (1973) proved a sufficient schedulability bound for n tasks:

U = Σ (Cᵢ/Tᵢ) ≤ n·(2^(1/n) − 1) n=1→1.000 n=2→0.828 n=3→0.780 n→∞→ln 2 ≈ 0.693 Cᵢ = worst-case execution time, Tᵢ = period. Below the bound: ALWAYS schedulable.

Keep total utilisation under ~69 % and any number of RMS tasks provably meet deadlines. (Between the bound and 100 % they may still pass — check with exact response-time analysis. EDF schedules to 100 % but is harder to certify.)

GPIO: Where 3.3 V Meets the 24 V World

Most dead Pis were killed at the pins. The plant speaks 24 V and amps; the SoC speaks 3.3 V and milliamps.

The electrical facts

Logic level

3.3 V

NOT 5 V tolerant. 5 V on a GPIO = dead RP1 port, sometimes a dead board.

Drive current

~8 mA

per pin (configurable a few mA more) — enough for an LED or a gate, never for a relay coil.

Total budget

~50 mA

across the whole header. Power actuators from their own supply, switch them with transistors.

Inputs float unless tied: enable internal pull-ups/pull-downs (~50 kΩ) or fit external ones. Mechanical contacts bounce for 1–10 ms — debounce in hardware (RC + Schmitt) or software (ignore edges for ~20 ms).

Industrial input & output — the two canonical circuits

Left: 24 V sensor read through an optocoupler — isolation is the industrial default. Right: GPIO drives a logic-level MOSFET that switches the 24 V coil; the flyback diode absorbs the inductive kick (L·di/dt).

Interfacing rules of thumb
  • 1Never wire plant voltage to the header. Everything above 3.3 V goes through a divider (signals), optocoupler (isolation) or level shifter (buses).
  • 2Switch loads, don't power them. GPIO → MOSFET/relay driver → load. The Pi's 5 V rail is not a field supply.
  • 3One star ground, shields landed once. Ground loops between plant 0 V and Pi GND inject volts of noise — isolate or single-point bond (same discipline as A11 sensors).
  • 4Inductive = flyback. Every coil (relay, solenoid, valve) gets a freewheel diode or TVS, right at the coil.

Before fieldbuses (→A01–A05) there are the centimetre-scale buses that connect chips to chips.

Key distinction: UART/I²C/SPI are logic-level, on-board buses — centimetres, quiet environment. The moment a cable leaves the enclosure, you want differential signalling (RS-485, CAN →A04) or isolation.

On the wire — I²C transaction vs SPI exchange

I²C trades speed for pin count and multi-drop addressing; SPI trades pins for raw speed and simplicity. Choose I²C for many slow sensors, SPI for one fast ADC or display.

Bus debugging discipline

First tool: a logic analyser (€15 8-channel is fine). I²C failures are 90 % electrical — missing pull-ups (wrong value: 4.7 kΩ @100 kHz, 2.2 kΩ @400 kHz), bus capacitance >400 pF from too-long wires, or address clashes. SPI failures are 90 % configuration — wrong CPOL/CPHA mode, CS toggling mid-frame, or exceeding the slave's clock spec. UART failures: baud mismatch and swapped TX/RX. Check voltage levels before logic: a 5 V I²C sensor needs a bidirectional level shifter, not luck.

Industrial I/O: Analog In, Power Out, Fieldbus Around

The Pi has no ADC and weak pins — industrial practice is external converters, HATs and DIN-rail carriers.

Reading 4–20 mA (→A11) on a board with no ADC

The plant's favourite analog signal becomes Pi-readable in two steps: a precision shunt converts current to voltage (250 Ω → 1–5 V, or 160 Ω → 0.64–3.2 V to stay inside 3.3 V), then an external ADC digitises it. The resolution math:

PWM out — from LED dimming to servo angles
duty D = t_on / T_period V_avg = D · V_supply hobby servo: T = 20 ms, pulse 1.0–2.0 ms ⇒ −90°…+90° hardware PWM (RP1: 4 ch) = glitch-free · software PWM = jitter ⇒ servo buzz

The same PWM principle scales up to VFDs (→A08) and MOSFET drives (→A29): vary the on-time ratio, filter with inductance, get an average. On the Pi always claim a hardware PWM channel for anything motion-related; bit-banged PWM under Linux jitters by milliseconds.

Fieldbus & soft-PLC on a Pi

Modbus RTU / TCP (→A01)

RS-485 HAT + pymodbus, or a USB dongle. The lingua franca for meters, VFDs and legacy gear.

CAN (→A04)

MCP2515 (SPI) or CM carrier with native CAN-FD. Mind 120 Ω termination at both ends.

OPC UA / MQTT (→A06, A33)

The Pi's home turf: full open62541 / paho stacks, gateway role between fieldbus and IT.

CODESYS soft-PLC

IEC 61131-3 runtime on Raspberry Pi / RevPi — ladder & ST programs, EtherCAT master (→A03) with RT-patched kernel.

Revolution Pi deserves its own mention: CM4/CM5-based, DIN-rail, 24 V supply, isolated I/O modules, CE/UL — a Raspberry Pi that a plant electrician will actually accept in the cabinet.

Reliability Engineering: Making a Pi Survive 5 Years Unattended

The compute is easy; staying alive through power cuts, heat, and flash wear is the real engineering.

Threat #1 — SD card wear & corruption

SD cards are consumer NAND with tiny wear-levelling controllers. Continuous logging plus sudden power loss corrupts filesystems — the #1 field failure of Pi deployments. Defences, in escalating order:

  • 1Reduce writes: mount logs to tmpfs/zram, disable swap, batch data before writing.
  • 2Read-only root with overlayfs — the OS partition can never corrupt; state lives on a separate partition or remote store.
  • 3Better media: industrial SD (SLC/pSLC, powerloss-protect), or eMMC on Compute Modules, or NVMe on Pi 5.
  • 4A/B partitions: updates write to the inactive slot; a failed update rolls back automatically (the RAUC/Mender pattern).
Threat #2 — power & heat

Power rules: a Pi 5 wants a 5 V / 5 A supply; undervoltage (< 4.63 V) triggers throttling and SD corruption risk. In a cabinet, feed it from the 24 V rail through a quality 24→5 V DC-DC (industrial buck, not a phone charger), add a supercap/UPS HAT so the box can flush buffers and shut down cleanly on power loss.

Threat #3 — software hangs: the watchdog chain
BCM watchdog (hardware, ~15 s max) ← systemd watchdog ← app heartbeat app stops petting → systemd restarts service → still dead → HW reboot design rule: every layer assumes the layer above WILL fail

Enable the SoC's hardware watchdog (dtparam=watchdog=on, systemd RuntimeWatchdogSec=), have services expose liveness (sd_notify), and log the reason for every reset. Combine with brown-out detection and you cover the three classic field deaths: hang, power dip, and kernel panic. MTBF thinking from A23 applies: the Pi is one block in a series reliability chain — PSU and SD usually dominate, not the SoC.

The Pi as Edge Gateway & AI Node

Where the Raspberry Pi genuinely beats a PLC: protocol translation, local analytics, vision, and machine learning at the edge.

The canonical edge-gateway architecture (→A33)

The gateway pattern: dumb, deterministic devices below; smart, best-effort analytics above; the Pi translating between the two worlds — south fieldbus, north MQTT/OPC UA.

Edge toolbox

Node-RED

Flow-based wiring of Modbus → dashboard → MQTT in minutes. Superb for prototypes and honest small deployments; version-control the flows JSON.

Containers

Docker/Podman isolate apps and pin dependencies; compose one stack (broker + gateway + DB + dashboard) reproducibly per machine.

Local time-series DB

InfluxDB/TimescaleDB at the edge: pre-aggregate to 1 s/1 min before shipping — 100× less uplink traffic.

TinyML / NPU

Pi 5 + AI HAT (Hailo-8L, 13 TOPS): real-time defect detection or anomaly scoring on-device — no cloud round-trip.

The performance rule from A33 applies verbatim: close loops at the edge (ms), train and compare in the cloud (minutes). Vibration FFT for predictive maintenance (→A23) is the textbook Pi workload: sample at 10 kHz on an MCU or ADC, window and FFT on the Pi, publish spectral features upward.

Security minimum (non-negotiable on a networked Pi)

Change default credentials, key-based SSH only, firewall (nftables) allowing exactly the needed ports, TLS on MQTT/OPC UA, signed OS updates, and put OT devices behind the Pi in their own network segment — the Purdue-zone discipline from A33. A forgotten Pi with default passwords is the classic pivot point into a plant network.

The honest decision rules
  • 1Safety function? Never a Pi. No SBC is SIL/PL certified. E-stop, light curtains, speed monitoring go to certified relays/PLCs (→A35). The Pi may observe, never protect.
  • 2Hard real-time < 100 µs? Use an MCU (or the PLC you already have). Pi + PREEMPT_RT is honest to ~100 µs-class loops; below that, silicon determinism wins.
  • 3Product for sale? Use a Compute Module, not the consumer board — pinned BOM, longevity pledge, EMC test the carrier once.
  • 4One-off cabinet install? Buy industrialised (RevPi, Strato Pi): DIN rail, 24 V, isolation and certifications are cheaper bought than built.
  • 5Just a dashboard or logger? Plain Pi + read-only root + UPS HAT is perfectly professional — most "industrial Pi failures" are unmanaged consumer setups.
Total cost of ownership — the number that decides
TCO = hardware + engineering hours + downtime risk + maintenance €80 board + 100 h custom integration ≫ €800 RevPi + 10 h hardware is the CHEAPEST line — optimise the other three

This is the same lesson OEE (→A22) teaches about machines: purchase price is a poor proxy for cost. The Pi wins TCO when its ecosystem (Linux tooling, containers, Python, community) slashes engineering hours — not because the board is cheap.

Recap, Failure Modes & Self-Test

The complete mental model on one page — then prove you own it.

The 8 load-bearing ideas
  • 1Embedded = dedicated + constrained + long-lived. The machine defines the computer.
  • 2MCU / MPU / IPC axis: determinism falls as compute rises — combine layers (Pi brains + MCU microseconds).
  • 3Real-time = bounded worst case. RMS bound U ≤ n(2^(1/n)−1) → keep utilisation under ~69 %.
  • 43.3 V, ~8 mA, not 5 V tolerant: isolate inputs, transistor-drive outputs, flyback every coil.
  • 5UART/I²C/SPI on the board; differential (RS-485/CAN) off the board.
  • 6No ADC on the Pi: shunt + external ADC for 4–20 mA; hardware PWM for motion.
  • 7Reliability = writes, watts, watchdogs: RO-root, clean 5 V/5 A power + UPS, hardware watchdog chain.
  • 8Gateway is the killer app; safety is the forbidden app.
Six classic failure modes
⚠️ SD card corruption

Continuous logging + power cuts kill consumer cards in months. Fix: RO overlayfs, tmpfs logs, industrial SD/eMMC/NVMe, clean shutdown via UPS HAT.

⚠️ 5 V into a 3.3 V pin

One mis-wired sensor kills the GPIO block. Fix: level shifters, optocouplers, and label the header — the plant assumes everything tolerates 24 V.

⚠️ Phone-charger power

Undervoltage → random throttling, USB dropouts, SD corruption. Fix: proper 5 V/5 A supply or 24→5 V industrial buck; monitor vcgencmd get_throttled.

⚠️ Ground loop noise

Plant 0 V tied to Pi GND at two points → volts of offset, flaky I²C, dead UARTs in the worst case. Fix: single-point grounding, isolation on every field signal.

⚠️ Missing flyback diode

Relay or valve coil switched without a freewheel path → L·di/dt spike destroys the driver and couples resets into the SoC. Fix: diode/TVS at the coil, snubbers on AC.

⚠️ Sequencing "soft" timing on stock Linux

Bit-banged pulses jitter by ms whenever the kernel gets busy — servos buzz, protocols drop. Fix: hardware peripherals (PWM/SPI), PREEMPT_RT, or delegate to an MCU.

🧠 Quick Check 1

Three RMS tasks use 25 %, 25 % and 25 % of the CPU. Provably schedulable?

Tap to revealU = 0.75 < n(2^(1/n)−1) for n=3 → 0.780. Yes — 0.75 ≤ 0.780, so the Liu–Layland sufficient bound guarantees all deadlines. (At 0.79 it would need exact response-time analysis — possibly still OK, but no longer guaranteed by the bound.)
🧠 Quick Check 2

Why does a Raspberry Pi need an external chip to read a 4–20 mA pressure transmitter?

Tap to revealTwo reasons: the Pi has no on-board ADC (the RP1 does digital I/O only), and GPIO is a 3.3 V logic input, not a current input. Practice: 250 Ω (or 160 Ω) precision shunt converts 4–20 mA to a voltage, then an SPI/I²C ADC (MCP3008/ADS1115) digitises it. Bonus: <4 mA reading = broken wire detection.
🧠 Quick Check 3

A relay coil is switched directly from a GPIO pin and the Pi randomly reboots when the relay releases. Diagnose.

Tap to revealTwo faults: the coil current exceeds the ~8 mA pin budget (use a MOSFET/driver), and there is no flyback diode — on release, L·di/dt generates a high-voltage spike that couples into the supply and resets/kills the SoC. Fit a freewheel diode across the coil and switch it through a logic-level MOSFET.
🧠 Quick Check 4

When is Linux + PREEMPT_RT an acceptable real-time platform, and when is it not?

Tap to revealAcceptable for ~100 µs-class worst-case latencies — soft-PLC scan loops, gateway timestamping, coarse motion supervision — since PREEMPT_RT (mainline since kernel 6.12) bounds the scheduling tail. NOT acceptable for sub-10 µs hard deadlines (commutation, fast encoders — use an MCU/FPGA) and NEVER for safety functions, which need certified hardware regardless of latency (→A35).
🧠 Quick Check 5

Your I²C sensor works on the bench but drops out on the machine with 2 m of cable. Why, and what's the industrial fix?

Tap to revealI²C is a board-level, single-ended, high-impedance bus — 2 m of cable adds capacitance (>400 pF limit) and picks up plant noise, so edges slump and ACKs fail. Fixes: shorten the run, drop to 100 kHz with stronger pull-ups, use a differential I²C extender — or properly: move the sensor to a fieldbus-grade link (RS-485, IO-Link →A05, CAN →A04) designed for cables.
🧠 Quick Check 6

Management wants the Pi that runs the dashboard to also monitor the light curtain "since it's already there". Your answer?

Tap to revealNo. A safety function must meet a required Performance Level under ISO 13849 with certified, redundant, diagnosed hardware — no SBC qualifies, whatever its uptime (→A35). The light curtain wires to a safety relay/PLC that removes power directly. The Pi may passively log the curtain status for OEE/analytics, but must never be in the safety chain.
