The Line That Printed 1.2 mm Late, Every Time, Perfectly Repeatably

A SHRINK-SLEEVE LABELLING LINE, EKALA — 68 SLAVES, 250 µs, AND A KNIFE THAT CUTS IN THE WRONG PLACE

A beverage plant in Ekala commissioned a shrink-sleeve applicator with a flying shear: film runs at 400 m/min, a rotary knife cuts a sleeve every 200 mm, and a registration mark tells the machine where the cut must land. Sixty-eight EtherCAT slaves — eight servo drives, a print-mark sensor, fifty-nine I/O and measurement terminals — on one line, 250 µs cycle. The specification allowed ±0.5 mm of registration error. The machine delivered 1.2 mm, consistently, in one direction, and roughly 4 % of sleeves went to the reject bin.

The integrator's response was the correct one for a fieldbus problem and completely useless here: he measured the bus. Every slave was in OP. The working counter was correct on every cycle. There were no CRC errors on any port. The bus load was 27.8 %. In pure network terms this line was in perfect health — and it was, because nothing that was wrong with this machine was visible in the network statistics.

Fault 1 — the outputs were applied when the frame arrived, not when the clock said so. An EtherCAT frame carrying 68 × 12 bytes of process data is not 68 frames. It is one:

process data = 68 slaves × 12 B = 816 B one LRW datagram = 10 B header + 816 B + 2 B WKC = 828 B EtherCAT payload = 2 B EtherCAT header + 828 B = 830 B on the wire = 8 preamble/SFD + 14 MAC/EtherType + 830 + 4 FCS + 12 IFG = 868 bytes = 6,944 bits t_frame = 6,944 / 100 Mbit/s = 69.44 µs → 69.44 / 250 = 27.8 % bus load for comparison, one frame per device (the PROFINET RT model → A02): 68 × 6.72 µs = 456.96 µs — impossible in a 250 µs cycle EtherCAT is 6.58× cheaper on the wire here, and that is why it was chosen

So far, so good — and this is exactly why EtherCAT was specified. But the terminals had been left in SyncManager-synchronous mode: each terminal applies its outputs at the instant the frame reaches it. That instant is decided by the master's software, and the master was a soft-PLC on a stock Linux kernel with no real-time patch. Its measured cycle jitter was ±180 µs.

web speed = 400 m/min = 6.667 m/s registration budget = ±0.5 mm → ±0.5 mm / 6.667 m/s = ±75 µs of timing budget master jitter ±180 µs → 180e-6 × 6.667 = ±1.20 mm ← the observed error frame travel skew, first slave to last (a CONSTANT, not jitter): 68 × 0.6 µs + 0.5 µs = 41.3 µs → 0.275 mm — and this one is compensable

Read those two lines again, because they are the whole of page 9. A constant delay of 41 µs is not an error at all; you subtract it once, in software, and it disappears forever. Jitter of 180 µs cannot be subtracted, because you do not know which way it went this cycle. EtherCAT ships with the cure — distributed clocks, which let every slave apply its outputs on a hardware event synchronised to well under a microsecond — and on this machine it was switched off.

Fault 2 — the spare part went into the wrong slot, and nothing complained. Three months earlier a technician replaced a failed 8-channel digital output terminal. He pulled the broken one from position 27, found the identical spare, and — because the terminals look identical and the rail is crowded — clipped it in at position 12, moving the terminal that had been at 12 down one place. Both were the same part number, same vendor, same revision.

EtherCAT identity, by default, is POSITION IN THE WIRING, not a name. (contrast: PROFINET identity is a NAME and travels with the device → A02) master checks per position: vendor ID + product code + revision position 12 expects EL2008, finds EL2008 → PASS position 27 expects EL2008, finds EL2008 → PASS result: the machine reaches OP with a clean bill of health, and the glue-applicator bits now drive the reject-gate solenoids

There is a cure for this too, and it is one rotary switch per terminal: the station alias, a second address stored in the slave, which the master can be told to verify. It costs nothing, it was not used, and the fault presented for weeks as “intermittent random behaviour on the reject station”.

Fault 3 — somebody put a switch in the middle of the segment. When the vision team needed a camera near the shear, the nearest network socket was an EtherCAT slave's spare port, so an 8-port managed switch went in between slave 34 and slave 35 — camera on one port, a laptop on another, and the EtherCAT line carried straight through. It appeared to work — the line ran, and the handful of “cyclic frame missed” entries that began appearing in the master's log were written off as a software quirk.

an EtherCAT slave forwards a frame after a few bits (cut-through), ~0.6 µs a standard switch stores the whole frame, then forwards it: added delay = 2 × 69.44 µs = 138.88 µs (out and back) turnaround = 152.04 → 290.92 µs against a 250 µs cycle → frames missed and when the camera has a full-size frame already in transmission: blocking = (1522 + 20) × 8 / 100 = 123.36 µs per direction worst case = 152.04 + 138.88 + 246.72 = 537.64 µs = 2.15 cycles and, silently: the switch is not an EtherCAT Slave Controller, so it does not timestamp anything — every distributed-clock delay measurement past it is wrong

Three faults, three models. A time model the commissioning engineer did not know he had chosen; an identity model that is positional and was never pinned down; and a topology model that quietly assumes every device in the segment is an EtherCAT Slave Controller. Those three — time, identity, topology — are what this module is about, and the line is repaired with recomputed numbers on page 14.

What it cost. 2,000 sleeves/min × 4 % reject × 480 min × LKR 4 = LKR 153,600 per shift, for roughly forty shifts before anyone connected the reject rate to the network configuration — about LKR 6.1 million, none of which appeared as downtime, and all of which was fixed by three configuration changes and one rotary switch per terminal.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

The bus load was 27.8 %, no frames were lost, and the working counter was correct on every single cycle. So where did 1.2 mm of registration error come from?

B. The network delivered the data flawlessly — and then every terminal applied it at the moment the frame happened to arrive, which is the moment the master happened to send, which wandered by ±180 µs. A is the tempting answer and it is real, but it is a constant 41 µs (0.275 mm) that you compensate once; it cannot produce a wandering error. C confuses load with latency — two different budgets, both computed on page 10. D would cut 69.44 µs to 6.94 µs and change nothing at all about a software jitter of 180 µs: you cannot buy your way out of a timing problem with bandwidth. If you picked A, you have just met the single most useful distinction in real-time engineering — delay is compensable, jitter is not — at the moment it is most likely to stick.

THE DEFINITION, AND THE ONE IDEA UNDER IT CORE

EtherCAT (Ethernet for Control Automation Technology) is a fieldbus that uses standard Ethernet cabling, standard Ethernet framing and a standard network card at the master, and then breaks with Ethernet in exactly one place: a slave does not receive a frame, act on it and send a new one. It modifies the frame while the frame is still passing through it.

Everything else follows from that sentence. If a slave can extract its outputs and insert its inputs on the fly, then one frame can serve every slave on the network, and the cost of adding a device is not another frame — it is a few more bytes in the frame you were sending anyway, plus a few hundred nanoseconds of forwarding delay. That is why a 68-slave machine costs 69 µs of wire time instead of 457 µs.

Origin and status. Developed by Beckhoff Automation and published in 2003; handed to the EtherCAT Technology Group (ETG), now the largest fieldbus organisation in the world by membership. Standardised as IEC 61158 Type 12 and IEC 61784-2 CPF 12, plus ISO 15745-4 and SEMI E54.20 for semiconductor equipment. The specification is open; the ETG publishes it to members without royalty, and ESC silicon is available from several vendors. This matters commercially: the master is free software on hardware you already own.

The master is software. A standard NIC, a driver that bypasses the operating system's network stack, and a stack that builds frames. No special card, no scheduling switch, no licence per node in most stacks. This is EtherCAT's quiet economic superpower — and its quiet weakness, because the quality of the master's real-time behaviour is entirely your problem (page 9).
The slave is hardware. Every slave contains an EtherCAT Slave Controller (ESC) — an ASIC (ET1100, ET1200), an FPGA IP core, or a block inside a drive's SoC. The ESC does the on-the-fly processing in silicon. You cannot make a slave out of a PC and a standard NIC, and that asymmetry is the whole design.

The logical ring. Ethernet is full duplex, so every cable is two independent one-way paths. EtherCAT uses one direction to go out and the other to come back. Each ESC has two, three or four ports; if a port has no link, the ESC closes it and folds the frame internally to the next port. A daisy chain is therefore, electrically and logically, a ring that happens to be drawn as a line — and that is why a single frame can leave the master and come back to the master having visited everything.

FIVE THINGS ETHERCAT DELIBERATELY IS NOT CORE

Every honest protocol description is a list of what was traded away. EtherCAT traded a great deal, on purpose, and every item below has bitten a real machine.

1 — It is not switchable Ethernet. You cannot put a standard switch, a media converter that buffers, or a router inside a segment. Every device in the path must be an ESC. The camera in the cold open was not a configuration mistake; it was a category mistake.
2 — It is not multi-master. One master owns one segment, absolutely. There is no arbitration, no peer-to-peer, no second controller reading the same I/O. (Compare A04 CAN, where every node may transmit, and A06 OPC UA, where anything may talk to anything.)
3 — It is not named. Identity is by position in the wiring unless you deliberately configure a station alias. A device that is moved is a different device (page 6).
4 — It is not secure. There is no authentication, no encryption and no access control inside a segment. Anyone who can reach a spare RJ45 port can write any output. Security is entirely physical and architectural — see A54.
5 — It is not routable. Cyclic EtherCAT lives at layer 2 with EtherType 0x88A4 and never leaves the segment. (EtherCAT-over-UDP exists for slow, remote, non-cyclic access; it is not how machines are built.)
and 6 — the master is not portable. Moving from one master stack to another (TwinCAT, acontis, IgH EtherCAT Master, CODESYS, KPA) means re-doing the configuration, because the ENI/ESI handling, the DC setup and the diagnostics differ. The slaves are interoperable; master projects are not.

Processing On The Fly — and the Law It Gives You

The processing unit does not copy the frame into memory and study it. It matches addresses and swaps bytes as they stream past, in silicon, at line rate.

THE FRAME IS LONGER THAN YOUR MACHINE CORE

Here is the fact that makes on-the-fly processing not merely clever but obvious, and almost nobody computes it.

at 100 Mbit/s, one bit lasts 10 ns signal velocity in Cat5e ≈ 0.6c ≈ 2 × 108 m/s → 5 ns per metre so one bit occupies 10 ns / 5 ns/m = 2 metres of cable the smallest EtherCAT frame = 84 bytes = 672 bits → 1.34 km long the cold open's frame = 868 bytes = 6,944 bits → 13.9 km long the machine it runs → about 100 m

The frame is not a parcel that travels from slave to slave. It is a ribbon 14 km long being dragged through a 100 m machine: while its tail is still leaving the master, its head has already turned round at the last slave and is on its way back. Every slave on the segment is inside the same frame at the same time. Once you see it that way, storing the whole frame before forwarding it — what an ordinary switch does — looks like what it is: an enormous and unnecessary act of waiting.

So the ESC does not wait. It begins retransmitting on the outgoing port a fixed few hundred nanoseconds after the bits start arriving — enough to have read the datagram header and decided whether this datagram concerns it. Everything after that is done to a stream: outputs are read out of the passing bytes, inputs are written into the passing bytes, the working counter at the end of the datagram is incremented, and the frame's FCS is recalculated on the fly because its contents have changed.

E-bus (LVDS)
The backplane between terminals on a DIN rail. No PHY, no magnetics, no cable. Forwarding delay ~0.3 µs per device, and the cheapest possible I/O point.
100BASE-TX
Ordinary Cat5e between boxes. The PHY costs latency both ways. Forwarding delay ~0.6–1.2 µs per device. Use the figure in the device's ESI, not a guess.
100BASE-FX / fibre
Longer runs and galvanic isolation, similar forwarding delay plus the fibre's own propagation (~5 ns/m). Buys distance and EMC, not speed.
THE CYCLE LAW CORE

Two different budgets come out of the paragraph above, and confusing them is the single most common EtherCAT design error. Bus load is how much of the cycle the wire is busy. Turnaround is how long the master waits for its frame to come home. They are not the same number and they fail in different ways.

D = total process data (bytes) N = number of slaves t_f = forwarding delay of one ESC (µs) L = total cable length (m) B = link rate (100 or 1000 Mbit/s) T = cycle time (µs) frames needed n = ceil(D / 1486) ← 1486 B is one datagram's ceiling wire bytes (frame k) W_k = 38 + max(2 + 12 + D_k, 46) frame time t_frame = Σ_k W_k × 8 / B BUS LOAD = t_frame / T ← can you afford the wire? TURNAROUND = t_frame + 2·N·t_f + 2·L·5 ns/m ← will the frame get home in time? FEASIBILITY TURNAROUND < T, and in practice < 0.8 T

Read the turnaround term carefully: 2·N·t_f. Each ESC is traversed twice — once outbound, once on the return leg through the folded ring. This is the term that eventually stops you, and it is the one term that a faster link rate does not touch. Silicon forwarding delay is silicon forwarding delay whether the bits arrive at 100 Mbit/s or 1 Gbit/s.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A machine with 16 bytes of I/O per slave must run at a 100 µs cycle. At 100 Mbit/s it tops out at 38 slaves. Move it to gigabit — ten times the wire speed. How many slaves now?

C — 73 slaves, a factor of 1.9 for ten times the bandwidth. At 38 slaves the budget is 52.8 µs of frame time against 45.6 µs of forwarding delay — roughly half and half. Gigabit divides the first term by ten and leaves the second untouched, so the ceiling moves to where forwarding delay alone eats the cycle: 73 × 2 × 0.6 µs = 87.6 µs, and there is barely room for the frame. D is nearly right in spirit and wrong in arithmetic. This is why EtherCAT G pairs gigabit with branch controllers that run several segments in parallel: the answer to a chain that is too long is never a faster chain, it is fewer devices in series.

WHAT THE LAW DOES NOT INCLUDE STRETCH

The planner gives the network's contribution. Four more terms live outside it, and each has stopped a real machine:

Master stack time. Building the frame, copying the process image, running the PLC task. On a soft-PLC this is often larger than the whole network term — and it is where the jitter of page 9 comes from.
Slave application time (PDI). The ESC hands data to the slave's microcontroller across the Process Data Interface. A drive's control loop may only read it every second cycle. The network being ready does not mean the device is.
Mailbox and acyclic traffic. Parameter reads, diagnostics, file transfers and EoE tunnelling share the cycle. Leave headroom: a bus load above ~60 % makes acyclic work slow and lumpy.
Frame splitting. Past 1486 bytes of data you need a second frame, and past roughly 1486 × n you need n. The planner shows the jump; the trap is a machine that grows by one terminal and silently crosses the boundary.

The Frame, Byte by Byte — and the Only Acknowledgement You Get

THE ENVELOPE CORE

An EtherCAT frame is an Ethernet frame with EtherType 0x88A4. That is the whole encapsulation: no IP, no UDP, no TCP, no VLAN required, nothing above layer 2. It is also the filter to type into Wireshark, and it is worth committing to memory alongside PROFINET's 0x8892.

Destination MAC. Usually a broadcast or a fabricated address; nobody switches on it, because there is nothing in the segment that switches. Some masters put a recognisable pattern here to spot their own frames returning.
The returning frame. The master's frame comes back to the master, contents modified. The master compares what it sent with what it received. The frame is then discarded — the C (circulating) bit exists so that a frame which somehow re-enters the ring is destroyed rather than circulating forever.

EtherCAT over UDP (UDP port 0x88A4) exists for the case where a master must reach a segment through a routed network. It works, it is in the standard, and it is used for remote diagnostics and slow configuration. It is not how a machine's cyclic data is carried: you have re-introduced everything EtherCAT removed.

THE DATAGRAM CORE

Inside the payload is a 2-byte EtherCAT header (an 11-bit length and a 4-bit type; type 1 is the ordinary command frame) followed by one or more datagrams. A datagram is the unit of work: a command, an address, some data, and a counter.

THE WORKING COUNTER — THE ONE NUMBER THAT MATTERS CORE

EtherCAT has no acknowledgements, no replies and no per-slave status message in the cyclic path. It has a 16-bit counter at the end of every datagram, and that counter is the entire feedback channel.

Each slave that successfully performs the requested operation increments it as the frame passes:

read command (APRD, FPRD, BRD, LRD) → +1 per slave that read successfully write command (APWR, FPWR, BWR, LWR) → +1 per slave that wrote successfully read-write (LRW, APRW, FPRW, BRW) → +1 for the read +2 for the write = +3 so for one LRW datagram covering the machine: WKC_expected = 1×(slaves contributing INPUTS only) + 2×(slaves consuming OUTPUTS only) + 3×(slaves doing BOTH in this logical range)

What this buys you. One integer compared per cycle tells you whether every device that should have participated did. The master computes the expected value from the configuration, and any mismatch is an immediate, cycle-accurate alarm. It is elegant, it is nearly free, and it is checked in hardware.

What it does not buy you. Three things, and every one of them has been mistaken for a network fault by somebody with a laptop:

It is not data integrity. Corruption is caught by the Ethernet FCS (and counted per port, page 12). WKC answers a different question: did the right number of slaves take part?
It does not identify who. A deficit of 3 means one both-direction slave dropped — or one input slave and one output slave. The counter is a sum, and sums lose information.
It says nothing about the physical world. A blown output driver, a broken sensor, a seized valve: the ESC still processes the datagram perfectly and increments the counter. WKC is a network-participation check, not a health check.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

On a running machine, channel 3 of an output terminal has failed — the driver transistor is dead and the valve never opens. What does the working counter do?

C — nothing. The ESC was asked to copy bytes from a passing frame into its output memory, and it did so flawlessly. What happens after that — whether a transistor conducts, whether a valve moves, whether the machine does anything at all — is entirely outside the working counter's universe. This is the most commonly misunderstood thing about EtherCAT diagnostics: a green bus is a statement about the bus. Channel-level faults must come from the device's own diagnostic objects (CoE, page 7) or from the process. If you take one habit from this page, take this one: never answer “the network is fine” to a question about the machine.

One datagram reads a value from one slave and, from that point on in its journey, carries that value as the payload it writes into everyone else. The master never sees the value in time to act on it; the frame itself is the courier. That is how a hundred clocks are corrected against a reference in one pass.

Two four-channel terminals thirty metres apart share one byte of the process image. No padding, no wasted frame, no per-device datagram.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A machine has 40 input terminals, each with 4 digital channels. How many bytes does the input process image occupy?

B — 20 bytes. 40 × 4 = 160 bits = 20 bytes exactly, because the FMMU maps with bit granularity: a terminal's four bits can start at bit 5 of byte 7 and finish at bit 0 of byte 8, and the hardware does not care. That is not a detail — it is the difference between a 20-byte frame and a 40-byte frame, and on a machine with hundreds of digital points it decides whether you fit under the 1486-byte ceiling. Most fieldbuses pad to byte or word boundaries; EtherCAT does not, and the FMMU is why.
THE FMMU CORE

The Fieldbus Memory Management Unit lives in every ESC — typically three to eight of them per slave. Each FMMU entry holds: a logical start address and start bit, a length in bits, a physical start address and start bit, and a direction. When a logical datagram sweeps past, the ESC checks whether the datagram's logical range overlaps any of its FMMU windows, and if so it reads or writes exactly those bits, in place, at line rate.

Why it changes the economics. Because the slave claims its own slice, the master does not have to address anyone. One LRW datagram spanning the whole image serves every slave in the machine, and adding a device adds bytes, not messages. Everything on page 3 flows from this.
What it costs you. The mapping is decided at configuration time and downloaded during start-up. Change the hardware and the map changes — which is exactly why moving a terminal (cold open, fault 2) silently re-points somebody's bits at somebody else's transistors.
THE SYNCMANAGER — CONSISTENCY WITHOUT LOCKS CORE

The FMMU decides where bytes go. The SyncManager decides when it is safe for the two sides — the network and the slave's own microcontroller — to touch them. There are two modes, and the difference between them is one of the most quietly elegant pieces of design in industrial networking.

Buffered mode (3 buffers) — process data. Three copies of the same memory area with hardware-swapped pointers. The writer always has a free buffer; the reader always gets the most recent complete one. Nobody ever waits, nobody ever sees a half-written image, and no data is ever “locked”. Old data is simply overwritten — correct for cyclic values, where the newest is the only one that matters.
Mailbox mode (1 buffer, handshake) — acyclic data. A single buffer that must be read before it may be written again. Nothing is lost, everything is delivered in order, and the price is that the sender may have to wait. Correct for parameters, diagnostics and file transfer, where losing a message is not acceptable.

Learn the pairing, because it is a general principle. Cyclic data wants freshness and tolerates loss — use buffered. Acyclic data wants completeness and tolerates delay — use handshaken. The same split appears in A06 OPC UA as subscriptions versus service calls, and in A04 CAN as PDOs versus SDOs. Getting it backwards — queueing sensor readings, or dropping parameter writes — is a design error that survives testing and fails in production.

SyncManager assignment, conventional layout: SM0 mailbox out (master → slave) handshaken SM1 mailbox in (slave → master) handshaken SM2 process data OUT (outputs) buffered, 3× SM3 process data IN (inputs) buffered, 3×

The SyncManager watchdog. Attached to SM2 is a timer, typically defaulting to 100 ms. If the outputs are not written within that time, the ESC drives the outputs to their configured safe state without asking the slave's application, the master, or anybody else. It is a hardware backstop and it is the reason a cut cable does not leave a valve energised. Its value is a real engineering decision — long enough to survive a redundancy switchover, short enough that the machine stops before it hurts something — and it is set on page 11.

Identity — Why the Right Spare Part in the Wrong Slot Is Invisible

THE THREE FILES, AND WHICH ONE IS WHICH CORE

Three descriptions govern every EtherCAT machine, and confusing them wastes more commissioning hours than any other single misunderstanding.

ESI — EtherCAT Slave Information
An XML file per device type, supplied by the manufacturer. Describes what the device is and can do: identity, PDOs, SyncManager and FMMU layout, mailbox protocols, distributed-clock options. The counterpart of PROFINET's GSDML and CANopen's EDS.
SII / EEPROM — in the slave
The device's own on-board copy of its identity and its power-on configuration, read by the master over the ESC. If the EEPROM is blank or corrupt the device is a brick with a link light. It can be rewritten — which is both a repair tool and a way to destroy a device.
ENI — EtherCAT Network Information
The compiled network configuration: the whole start-up script, the process image layout, the FMMU/SM downloads, the DC settings. Generated from the ESI files plus your project. This is what the master actually executes.

The practical consequence: a device works because the ESI matched, the ENI was generated from it, and the physical machine still looks like the ENI. Break any one of those three and the symptom is the same — a slave that will not leave PRE-OP — while the cause is completely different in each case.

WHAT “IDENTITY” ACTUALLY MEANS HERE CORE

A slave's identity is the triple in CoE object 0x1018, mirrored in the EEPROM:

0x1018:01 Vendor ID who made it 0x1018:02 Product Code what it is 0x1018:03 Revision Number which generation of it 0x1018:04 Serial Number which individual ← almost never checked register 0x0010 Configured Station Address assigned by the master at start-up register 0x0012 Station Alias set by YOU — switches or EEPROM

By default the master walks the segment with auto-increment commands, so position in the wiring is the identity, and the identity triple is only used to confirm that the device found at position k is the type the project expected at position k. Two identical terminals swapped therefore pass every check that is switched on by default. This is not a bug and it is not hidden; it is a deliberate trade for a bus that must enumerate two hundred devices in milliseconds without anybody configuring anything.

The cure is the station alias. Set a non-zero alias on each device — two rotary switches on most Beckhoff terminals and couplers, or a value written into the EEPROM for devices without switches — and enable the master's identification option. The master then addresses and verifies devices by alias rather than by position, and a moved device is instantly a mismatch. It costs a few minutes per machine at build time and it converts an entire class of silent faults into loud ones.

HOT CONNECT — PARTS OF THE MACHINE THAT COME AND GO STRETCH

Some machines legitimately change shape while running: tool changers, exchangeable heads, trolleys that dock, a test station that is wheeled away. EtherCAT handles this with Hot Connect groups — a set of slaves declared in the project as optional, allowed to disappear and reappear without stopping the rest of the segment.

What it requires. Every device in a hot-connect group must have a station alias, because position addressing cannot survive a group vanishing. The group must attach at a defined port, and the master needs a moment (typically one to a few hundred milliseconds) to re-enumerate when it appears.
What it does not do. It does not make a group's absence harmless to your program — the process data for a missing group is simply not updated, and it is your logic that must notice. Read the group's state, not its data. A stale value looks exactly like a real one.

The failure mode to design against: a machine where the tool changer is absent, the PLC keeps using the last-known tool data, and the operator is told everything is normal. If a group can be absent, the program must have an explicit “this group is present and in OP” interlock, and the safety function must not depend on data from a group that can walk away.

THE MAILBOX PROTOCOLS CORE

Alongside the cyclic process data, EtherCAT carries an acyclic channel — the mailbox — and several protocols ride inside it. They share the same wire and the same cycle, which is why bus-load headroom matters.

CoE — CANopen over EtherCAT. By far the most common. The CANopen object dictionary (indices and sub-indices), SDO read/write for parameters, and PDO mapping objects 0x1600+ / 0x1A00+ with assignment via 0x1C12 / 0x1C13. Drives usually follow the CiA 402 profile — see A09.
SoE — Servo drive profile over EtherCAT. The SERCOS parameter model (IDNs) instead of CANopen objects, standardised in IEC 61800-7. Used by drive vendors with a SERCOS heritage. Same wire, different vocabulary.
FoE — File over EtherCAT. A deliberately tiny file transfer, used for firmware updates in BOOT state. No file system, no directory — a name and a stream.
EoE — Ethernet over EtherCAT. Tunnels ordinary Ethernet frames through the mailbox so that a web server inside a device, or a laptop on a gateway terminal, can be reached without breaking the segment. This is the correct answer to the cold open's switch. It is also slow and it consumes your acyclic budget: use it for configuration, never for video.
AoE — ADS over EtherCAT. Beckhoff's routed messaging, used to reach a device behind a gateway or a second segment.
VoE — vendor specific. Exactly what it says. Documented by the vendor or not at all.

PDO mapping versus PDO assignment is the distinction that trips people. The mapping objects (0x1600, 0x1A00…) say which parameters make up a PDO and in what order. The assignment objects (0x1C12 for outputs, 0x1C13 for inputs) say which of those PDOs are actually active in this configuration. A device with a rich object dictionary often supports many mappings and activates three — and a “the value is always zero” complaint is very often a parameter that was mapped but never assigned.

Distributed Clocks I — How a Hundred Clocks Measure Their Own Distance

THE PROBLEM, STATED PROPERLY CORE

Sixty-eight devices must act at the same instant. Each is a different distance from the master, and each has its own crystal oscillator running at its own slightly wrong frequency. Three separate problems hide inside that sentence:

1 — Distance. How far, in nanoseconds, is each slave from the reference? Constant, but unknown until measured.
2 — Offset. Each local clock started counting whenever the device powered up. They agree on the rate of time but not on what time it is.
3 — Drift. A 25 ppm crystal gains or loses 25 µs every second. Left alone, two clocks separate by 90 ms per hour. Solving 1 and 2 once is not enough; the correction must run forever.

And note who keeps the time. In EtherCAT the reference clock is not the master — it is a slave, normally the first DC-capable device in the segment. Every other slave is disciplined to it, and the master, if it wants to stay in step, disciplines itself. This inversion is deliberate: the slaves are hardware with dedicated counters, the master is software on an operating system. The least trustworthy clock in the system does not get to be the reference.

STEP 1 — THE MEASUREMENT, AND WHY IT IS BEAUTIFUL CORE

The master sends one broadcast write to register 0x0900. As that frame passes, every ESC latches its local time at each port into registers 0x0900, 0x0904, 0x0908, 0x090C — the receive time at ports 0 to 3. Because the segment is a folded ring, the same frame crosses each slave twice: outbound at port 0, inbound at port 1. The master then reads the latched values at leisure.

Now the trick. Define, for slave i:

RTᵢ = (time latched at port 1) − (time latched at port 0) both numbers come from the SAME slave's SAME counter, taken a few microseconds apart, so: the slave's unknown offset → cancels the slave's frequency error over 5 µs → utterly negligible you can measure the ring before anything is synchronised

That is the whole idea, and it is why EtherCAT's synchronisation converges in milliseconds where a protocol built on exchanging timestamps between different clocks — IEEE 1588 / gPTP, see A37 — needs an iterative servo just to get the delay measurement trustworthy. EtherCAT does not measure the delay between two clocks. It measures a loop inside one clock.

STEP 2 — SOLVING FOR THE LINKS CORE

RTᵢ contains everything downstream of slave i. Subtract the next slave's round trip and what is left is the link between them, twice, plus the forwarding delays that lie between the two measurement points:

RTᵢ = f + 2dᵢ + f + RTᵢ₊₁ (intermediate slaves) RTₙ₋₁ = f + 2dₙ₋₁ + RTₙ (the tail: its return traversal is already inside RTₙ) RTₙ = f (last slave, port closed, folds internally) so dᵢ = ( RTᵢ − RTᵢ₊₁ − δᵢ ) / 2 with δᵢ = 2f, or f for the last link and the system delay of slave i (its distance from the reference) is Dᵢ = Σk<i ( f + dᵤ ) a check worth remembering: RT₁ / 2 = Dₙ half the master's round trip is the last slave's system delay — when the forward and reverse paths are symmetric, and only then

The master writes each slave's system time offset (0x0920) and system time delay (0x0928) so that every slave, adding its own numbers, arrives at the same idea of “now” — a 64-bit nanosecond count with an epoch of 1 January 2000.

STEP 3 — HOLDING IT THERE FOREVER CORE

Measurement is a one-off; drift is permanent. Every cycle (or every few cycles) the master sends an ARMW or FRMW datagram: it reads the system time out of the reference slave and, from that point onward in its journey, writes that value into every slave it passes. Each ESC compares the arriving reference time (adjusted by its own known system delay) with its own counter and nudges its local clock rate — a hardware time-control loop, not a jump.

Why nudge instead of set? Because a clock that jumps backwards breaks everything built on it — a SYNC0 pulse could be skipped or doubled. The ESC therefore speeds or slows its counter until it agrees. Monotonic time is worth defending.
What you should see. Masters expose a per-slave system time difference. In a healthy machine it settles to tens of nanoseconds and stays there. A value that wanders by microseconds, or grows steadily, is the alarm — and it is visible long before anything trips.

Typical achieved accuracy: well under 100 ns across a segment, often a few tens of nanoseconds, and essentially independent of how many slaves there are — because each one's distance is measured individually rather than accumulated. That number is what page 9 spends.

FOUR WAYS THE MEASUREMENT LIES STRETCH
⚠ Asymmetric paths

Cause: the algorithm halves the round trip, which assumes out and back cost the same. Two fibres of different length in one duplex pair, or a media converter that buffers in one direction, breaks that. Effect: a constant offset of half the asymmetry — invisible in every statistic, visible only as a machine that is always slightly wrong in one place. Same trap as gPTP in A37.

⚠ A non-ESC in the path

Cause: a standard switch, a hub, a buffering converter. Effect: it does not timestamp, and it adds a variable delay, so every measurement past it is wrong and unstable. This is the silent half of the cold open's third fault.

⚠ Topology changed, delays not re-measured

Cause: a hot-connect group appeared, a redundancy switchover reversed a path, someone moved a drop line. Effect: stale system delays — the machine synchronises confidently to the wrong numbers. Re-measure after any topology change; good masters do it automatically, and you should verify that yours does.

⚠ The wrong reference clock

Cause: the first DC-capable slave is a simple terminal in a cabinet that gets switched off with the lighting circuit, or sits behind a hot-connect boundary. Effect: the whole machine's time base disappears when that device does. Choose the reference deliberately: something central, permanently powered, and not optional.

SYNC0, SYNC1 AND SHIFT TIME CORE

A DC-capable ESC can generate one or two hardware pulses per cycle from its synchronised clock.

SYNC0 — the main event, at the cycle period. Typically triggers apply outputs and latch inputs in the slave's application. Its instant is identical, to within the DC accuracy, on every slave in the machine, whether it is the first terminal or the sixty-eighth.
SYNC1 — an optional second pulse at a fixed offset from SYNC0, or at a multiple of the period. Used when inputs must be sampled at a different instant from when outputs are applied, and for devices that need a two-phase sequence (sample, then convert, then apply).

Shift time is the deliberate offset between the nominal cycle start and the SYNC0 pulse. It exists for one reason: the data must already be there when the pulse fires. The condition to satisfy is:

t_arrival(worst case) + t_copy(PDI, into the application) < t_SYNC0 where t_arrival includes: master send jitter + frame time + this slave's system delay Dᵢ so the shift is chosen as: shift = worst-case arrival + margin and the consequence of getting it wrong is not a small error: frame late → the slave applies the PREVIOUS cycle's data, or raises AL status 0x001A and drops to SAFE-OP

This is the honest limit of distributed clocks, and it is worth stating plainly: DC does not make a jittery master acceptable. It converts a silent position error into a loud, detectable, one-cycle-late fault. That is an enormous improvement — a machine that stops and tells you is infinitely better than a machine that quietly makes 4 % scrap — but it is not a licence to run a real-time network from a general-purpose operating system.

Master shift. Good masters go one step further and discipline their own transmit instant to the reference clock, so the frames land in the same place in the window every cycle. This is usually a single option in the master's DC settings, it costs nothing, and on a soft-PLC it is the difference between a comfortable margin and an occasional missed window.

Needs DC. Coordinated motion and electronic gearing — a virtual master axis is worthless if the followers act at different instants. Flying shears, print registration, cam profiles. Does not need DC. Conveyors, level and temperature loops, lamps, most pneumatics (→ A12), interlocks, anything whose mechanical time constant is tens of milliseconds. A quietly powerful feature to know about: DC-capable inputs can timestamp an edge in hardware — the ESC latches the distributed-clock time at which a signal changed. A print mark sensor with a timestamped input measures registration to nanoseconds regardless of the cycle time, because the measurement no longer happens at the cycle. If you ever need to measure something faster than your cycle, this is the mechanism, and it is usually already in the terminal you own.

SIZING THE PROCESS IMAGE CORE

Three limits shape the image, and they bite in this order:

1486 bytes per datagram. Cross it and the master needs a second datagram — and probably a second frame. Costs 12 bytes of header and a step in the frame time. Machines that grow one terminal at a time cross this boundary without anybody noticing.
~1500 bytes per frame. Several datagrams can share a frame (an LRD for inputs, an LWR for outputs, an ARMW for the clock), each costing 12 bytes. Masters pack automatically; you should still know how many frames per cycle your machine sends.
The FMMU count per slave. Typically three to eight. A device with many separately mapped areas can run out, which appears as a configuration error rather than a size error — and confuses everybody.

Oversampling is the sleeper. An oversampling terminal delivers many samples per cycle — ten, a hundred, a thousand — each with its own timestamp derived from the distributed clock. It is the correct way to capture a waveform at 100 kHz on a 1 kHz bus, and it multiplies that terminal's process data by the oversampling factor. One 100× oversampled analogue input can be larger than fifty digital terminals. Budget it explicitly.

ETHERCAT G AND THE BRANCH CONTROLLER RESEARCH

Gigabit EtherCAT (EtherCAT G, and 10 Gbit G10) exists, and page 3 showed why it is not the obvious win it sounds like: the frame time falls by ten, the chain does not move at all. The genuinely interesting part of EtherCAT G is not the wire rate but the branch controller, which runs several segments in parallel from one gigabit trunk, so their forwarding delays no longer add up in series.

When it is the right answer. Vision, oversampling and high-channel-count measurement — applications where the process data is genuinely large. Here the frame time really is the limit, and gigabit really does divide it by ten.
Honest status. Largely a single-vendor ecosystem, priced accordingly, and rare outside high-end machine building. In Sri Lanka you are unlikely to meet it before 2030. Know that it exists, know that parallel segments is the structural fix for a long chain, and reach for a second master interface or a second segment long before you reach for gigabit.

Topology, Redundancy, and the Setting That Throws It All Away

THE SHAPES, AND WHAT EACH COSTS CORE
Line. The default and the cheapest. Every device has two ports; the last open port folds the frame back. Cost: the chain delay of page 3, and a break anywhere isolates everything downstream.
Tree, via junctions and drop lines. A device with three or four ports (a junction such as an EK1122, or a coupler with spare ports) starts a branch. Cost: the frame enters and leaves the branch, so each branch device's forwarding delay is paid twice and the branch's cable length twice.
Star. A junction with several branches. Physically tidy, logically identical — it is still one ring, walked in a fixed order. This surprises people who expect a star to be parallel; it is not, and it does not reduce the chain.
Ring. The line's last port connected back to a second master port. Costs one cable and a master that supports cable redundancy. Buys the best redundancy in this library.

The port order inside an ESC is 0 → 3 → 1 → 2. That is why the logical order of slaves in a tree is not the order you drew on the layout, and why the number printed next to a device in the master's list can look wrong. Position addressing follows the frame, and the frame follows that order — another reason to configure aliases (page 6) rather than reasoning about positions.

CABLE REDUNDANCY — WHERE ETHERCAT WINS OUTRIGHT CORE

Close the line into a ring and give the master a second port. Now the master sends frames both ways. A break anywhere — a cut cable, a pulled connector, a device that loses power — leaves every remaining slave reachable from one side or the other, and the recovery happens within a single cycle because there is no protocol to run: both paths were already live.

EtherCAT cable redundancy → recovery < 1 cycle (250 µs on our line) PROFINET MRP, standard → recovery ≈ 200 ms (→ A02) 200 ms / 250 µs = 800× on a web at 6.667 m/s: 1 cycle = 1.7 mm of film 200 ms = 1.33 m of film

That is not a marginal difference; it is the difference between a redundancy scheme that hides a fault from the process and one that merely shortens the stoppage. It is EtherCAT's strongest single claim, and it is worth knowing precisely so that you can make it precisely.

What it requires, honestly:

A master that supports it, with two interfaces on the same stack. Not every master does, and it is sometimes a licensed option. Check before you quote it.
Distributed clocks need care. When the ring breaks, propagation paths reverse; the master must handle the delay values for both directions. Good masters do this automatically — verify yours, because a switchover that silently corrupts DC is worse than no redundancy.
It protects the path, not the device. A failed slave still loses its own I/O; the rest of the machine survives. And a cabinet that loses power loses every device in it, ring or no ring.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A plant has repeated nuisance stops from a marginal connector. An engineer raises the SyncManager watchdog from 100 ms to 400 ms and the stops disappear. What has actually been achieved?

B. The watchdog is not a filter and it does not repair anything. It is the answer to one question: how long may this machine keep acting on data that has stopped arriving? Raising it to 400 ms means a conveyor still running, a heater still on, a valve still open for four tenths of a second after the network died — at 6.667 m/s that is 2.7 metres of product. The stops disappeared because the evidence disappeared. Safety over EtherCAT — SIL 3 Across a Network That Trusts Nothing

WHAT FSoE IS CORE

Safety over EtherCAT (FSoE) is a safety protocol that rides inside ordinary EtherCAT process data. It is standardised in IEC 61784-3-12, certified for use up to SIL 3 (IEC 61508) and PL e (ISO 13849), and specified by the ETG. To the network it is a few unremarkable bytes in the cyclic image; to the two endpoints it is a rigorously checked channel.

The governing idea is the black channel, the same principle PROFIsafe uses (→ A02) and the reason both protocols can be certified independently of the network under them: the transport is assumed to be capable of every failure, and none of those failures is allowed to matter. The consequence is practical and enormous — no switch, no terminal, no cable and no standard PLC in the path needs any safety approval, because nothing in the path is trusted.

The container. A small structure — of the order of six bytes for a single byte of safe data — carrying a command, the safe data, a CRC, and a connection ID unique to that safety relationship. A sequence is folded into the CRC calculation rather than sent as a separate field, so a stale or repeated frame simply fails the check.
The endpoints. An FSoE Master (normally the safety controller) opens a connection to each FSoE Slave (a safe input terminal, a safe output, a drive's STO). Each connection has its own ID and its own watchdog. A connection that goes quiet does not raise a warning — it goes to the safe state.
THE EIGHT FAILURES, AND WHAT CATCHES EACH CORE

IEC 61784-3 enumerates the ways a message can fail. Every safety protocol must show a countermeasure for each; this is the table an assessor will ask you about.

The one that catches people: the connection ID is part of the safety configuration, not part of the network configuration. Duplicate a project onto a second identical machine without re-assigning the safety addresses and you have built two machines that will accept each other's safety messages if they ever share a network. Every safety-configuration tool computes a checksum over the configuration for exactly this reason, and that checksum belongs in the machine file.

⚡ PREDICT FIRST

A single EtherCAT frame is destroyed by a burst of interference. The machine is running an FSoE connection to a drive's Safe Torque Off. What happens?

B. A safety protocol that stopped the machine on every single lost telegram would be intolerable in a factory and would be defeated within a week by people raising every timer they could find — which is a real and well-documented safety failure mode. So the FSoE watchdog is set to span several cycles: transient loss is absorbed, persistent loss trips. C is wrong in the part that matters — the event is counted, and those counts are your early warning. D describes something that does happen (the working counter will be wrong for that cycle) but the standard side reacting is not what protects anybody. The watchdog is the safety function; everything else is bookkeeping.
RESPONSE TIME — THE NUMBER THAT LEAVES THE NETWORK CORE

The safety function's total response time is what the risk assessment actually consumes, and the network is only one term in it:

total response = sensor detection + FSoE transport (input side, incl. watchdog allowance) + safety logic execution + FSoE transport (output side, incl. watchdog allowance) + actuator reaction (contactor drop-out, STO, brake) and that total goes straight into ISO 13855: S = K × T + C (→ A35, A52)

Two consequences follow, and both are worth saying out loud in a design review. First, a shorter cycle time buys guard distance: every millisecond removed from the response time is 1.6 mm of light-curtain distance at the standard approach speed of 1600 mm/s. Second, the watchdog is not free — a generous FSoE watchdog that makes commissioning quiet also lengthens the response time and pushes the guard further away. The worked arithmetic for that trade lives in A02's PROFIsafe example and in A35; the structure is identical, only the protocol's name changes.

What FSoE gives you. Safety devices anywhere in the segment, on the same cable as everything else, with no separate safety wiring and no safety-rated network hardware. Safe outputs, safe analogue values, and drive safety functions (STO, SS1, SLS → A52) reached over the same bus.
What it does not give you. Security. FSoE proves that a message is intact and current; it does not prove that it came from someone entitled to send it, and it is not designed to. Anyone with the safety configuration and physical access is inside. Functional safety and security are different disciplines — see A54.

SECURITY — SAYING THE HONEST THING CORE

EtherCAT has no authentication, no encryption and no access control. This is not an oversight to be fixed in a later revision; it is a consequence of processing frames in silicon at line rate, and it will not change. Anybody who can reach a port inside the segment can write any output on the machine, at any time, with free software.

Treat a spare RJ45 like a live terminal. Unused ports inside a cabinet are an access point. Lock the cabinet; use port blockers where the cabinet cannot be locked; never bring a segment outside the machine's enclosure.
Defend the master, not the bus. The master PC is the only thing an attacker can reach remotely. It should not be on the office network, should not browse the internet, should not share a NIC between EtherCAT and IT traffic, and should be patched deliberately rather than automatically.
Architecture, not protocol. Segmentation, a firewall at the cell boundary, and an OPC UA gateway (→ A06) for anything that must reach the plant network. The full programme is A54 and IEC 62443.

Said plainly to a manager: “the machine network cannot be secured by itself and was never meant to be; what we secure is the door to it”. That sentence is accurate, it is defensible in an audit, and it moves the conversation to the thing that can actually be done.

Recap, Failure Modes and the Quick Check

EIGHT IDEAS WORTH KEEPING CORE
1 — The frame is modified in flight. One frame serves the whole machine, so the cost of the network grows with bytes, not with devices. Everything else about EtherCAT is a consequence.
2 — There are two budgets, not one. Bus load is bytes per cycle; turnaround is the chain. A machine can be at 20 % load and still impossible. Compute both, always.
3 — 32 and 1486. The first 32 bytes of process data are free; the datagram ceiling is 1486 bytes. Between them they explain almost every step in a frame-time curve.
4 — The working counter is a participation check. It proves that the expected slaves processed the datagram. It says nothing about data validity and nothing whatever about the machine.
5 — Identity is position, unless you make it otherwise. One alias per slot converts a whole class of silent maintenance faults into loud ones, for free.
6 — The state names the layer. Stuck in INIT is hardware; PRE-OP is the mailbox and the ESI; SAFE-OP is the process-data mapping; falling out of OP is timing. Ask for the state and the AL status code before anything else.
7 — Distributed clocks measure a loop inside one clock. That is why no prior synchronisation is needed, why convergence is fast, and why accuracy does not decay with the number of slaves.
8 — Delay is compensable; jitter is not. And the watchdog and the recovery time are two different clocks that must be compared explicitly, every time.
SIX FAILURE MODES YOU WILL ACTUALLY MEET CORE
⚠ Repeatably wrong, never faulty

Signature: a consistent positional error, in one direction, on a machine whose network statistics are perfect. Cause: SM-synchronous mode inheriting the master's jitter. Fix: DC-synchronous mode, then a punctual master. Tell: the error scales exactly with line speed.

⚠ Green bus, wrong loads

Signature: outputs act on the wrong equipment after a maintenance visit; no alarm anywhere. Cause: identical devices swapped between positions; identity check passes at both. Fix: station aliases and identification. Tell: it started after a spare part was fitted, not after a fault.

⚠ The helpful switch

Signature: cycle overruns and unstable DC after somebody added “a bit of flexibility”. Cause: a store-and-forward device inside the segment. Fix: remove it; use EoE or a separate interface. Tell: the turnaround jumped by roughly two frame times.

⚠ One device drops from OP

Signature: a single drive falls to SAFE-OP with 0x001A while forty terminals stay happy. Cause: the frame misses the SYNC0 window — master jitter or a shift time set by guesswork. Fix: compute the shift, then fix the master. Tell: port error counters are flat, so the cable is innocent.

⚠ The marginal cable

Signature: occasional working-counter errors, a perfect link LED, no pattern anybody can see. Cause: a connector or a cable degrading. Fix: walk the RX error counters and replace the link that feeds the first rising port. Tell: forwarded-error counters rising downstream of exactly one port.

⚠ Redundancy that never helps

Signature: a ring is installed and every fault still stops the line — or the stops vanished after somebody raised a timer. Cause: watchdog and recovery time never compared. Fix: set the watchdog from the process consequence and prove the recovery time by pulling a cable during a witnessed test. Tell: nobody can tell you the recovery time in milliseconds.

QUICK CHECK — SIX QUESTIONS

1. Ninety slaves, 20 bytes each, 120 m of cable, 0.6 µs forwarding delay, proposed cycle 200 µs. Possible?

tap to revealNo, on both budgets. 1,800 B of data exceeds the 1,486 B datagram ceiling, so it is two frames: 123.04 + 29.28 = 152.32 µs of frame time, which is already 76 % of a 200 µs cycle. Add the chain — 2 × 90 × 0.6 = 108 µs — and 1.2 µs of propagation, and the turnaround is 261.5 µs, i.e. 131 % of the cycle. At 250 µs it is still 105 %. The first cycle that works is 500 µs (52 % turnaround, 30 % load). The honest answer to the customer is “200 µs is arithmetically impossible at any price; 500 µs works comfortably, and if you truly need 200 the answer is two segments, not a faster wire”.

2. An LRW datagram passes a servo drive that both consumes outputs and supplies inputs in the mapped range. By how much does that drive increment the working counter, and why that number?

tap to revealBy 3. A read-write command increments by 1 for a successful read and by 2 for a successful write, and the drive does both. The asymmetric weighting is deliberate: it lets the master distinguish a slave that read but failed to write from one that did neither, which a simple +1 per slave could never do. It is also why a deficit of 3 is ambiguous — one drive, or one input terminal plus one output terminal.

3. After a firmware update, one slave will not leave PRE-OP and reports AL status 0x0016. What is the fault, and what is not the fault?

tap to reveal0x0016 is “invalid mailbox configuration”, raised on the INIT→PRE-OP transition — the mailbox SyncManager setup the master offered does not match what this device now expects. The new firmware almost certainly carries a new revision number, and the project still holds the old ESI. Update the ESI, re-generate the ENI, and check the revision-check policy. What it is not: a cable, a connector, a power supply or a “faulty device”. The device reached INIT, which means the ESC, the link and the EEPROM are all working. Reaching INIT is itself a diagnosis.

4. A labeller runs at 250 m/min with a ±0.2 mm registration tolerance. The master's jitter is ±40 µs and the terminals are SM-synchronous. Does it pass?

tap to reveal250 m/min = 4.167 m/s, so the timing budget is 0.2 mm / 4.167 m/s = 48 µs. A ±40 µs jitter gives ±0.167 mm — it passes, using 83 % of the entire budget on one term. Which means there is nothing left for sensor delay, mechanical backlash, film stretch or a bad day for the operating system. The correct answer in a design review is “arithmetically yes, engineering-wise no”: put the axes on DC, spend 0.1 % of the budget instead of 83 %, and keep the rest for the physical world.

5. The RX error counter on slave 9's incoming port is climbing. Slaves 10 to 20 show climbing forwarded error counters. Slaves 1 to 8 are clean. Where is the fault, and what are the four candidates in order?

tap to revealThe fault is in the link feeding slave 9's incoming port — that is, between slave 8 and slave 9. Slave 9 is where the damage is first detected; everything after it merely forwards a frame already marked bad, which is exactly what the forwarded counters are for. Candidates, in order of probability: (1) the cable or its two connectors, (2) a marginal transmitter in slave 8's outgoing port, (3) a marginal receiver in slave 9's incoming port, (4) an external interference source coupling into that specific run — a VFD motor cable sharing a tray is the classic (→ A08). Note that you have localised it to one link out of twenty without unplugging anything.

6. Give three independent reasons why a standard managed switch cannot sit inside an EtherCAT segment.

tap to reveal(1) It stores and forwards. An ESC re-transmits after a few hundred nanoseconds; a switch waits for the entire frame, adding a full frame time in each direction — 138.9 µs for the cold open's frame — plus queueing delay whenever it has other traffic. (2) It is not an ESC, so it does not process datagrams on the fly and it does not timestamp ports: every distributed-clock delay measurement beyond it is wrong, and unstably wrong. (3) It does not fold the ring. EtherCAT's topology depends on ESCs auto-closing unconnected ports so the frame returns; a switch has no such behaviour and no concept of the logical ring. Any one of the three is fatal on its own. The correct way to attach foreign devices is a separate interface, or EoE through a gateway terminal.

WE1 — SIZING A THREE-STATION LINE CORE

Problem. A converting line in Homagama has three stations on one EtherCAT segment: Station A, 24 slaves × 6 B; Station B, 40 slaves × 20 B; Station C, 12 slaves × 32 B plus 2 drives × 24 B. Total cable 180 m, forwarding delay 0.6 µs. The OEM proposes a 250 µs cycle. Assess it, then assess it again after the customer adds a 200-byte inspection head.

slaves N = 24 + 40 + 12 + 2 = 78 data D = 144 + 800 + 384 + 48 = 1,376 B → 1,376 < 1,486, so ONE datagram payload = 2 + 12 + 1,376 = 1,390 B wire = 38 + 1,390 = 1,428 B t_frame = 1,428 × 8 / 100 = 114.24 µs chain = 2 × 78 × 0.6 = 93.6 µs prop = 2 × 180 × 0.005 = 1.8 µs -------- turnaround = 209.64 µs at 250 µs: turnaround = 83.9 % bus load = 45.7 % at 500 µs: turnaround = 41.9 % bus load = 22.8 %

Verdict on the proposal. 250 µs is possible and it is not comfortable. Eighty-four per cent turnaround leaves 40 µs for everything unmodelled — master stack time, a device with a slower ESC than assumed, one drop line nobody drew. Forty-six per cent bus load will make diagnostics sluggish and firmware updates painful. The professional answer is not “no”: it is “split the tasks” — put the two drives and their measurement in a 250 µs task and everything else in a 2 ms task, which is a configuration decision, not a purchase.

Now add the inspection head. D becomes 1,576 B, which crosses 1,486:

frame 1: 1,486 B data → wire 1,538 B → 123.04 µs frame 2: 90 B data → wire 142 B → 11.36 µs total 134.40 µs turnaround = 134.40 + 93.6 + 1.8 = 229.8 µs = 91.9 % of 250 µs

The lesson worth carrying. Two hundred bytes — one device — moved the machine from 84 % to 92 % of its cycle, because it crossed a boundary rather than because it was large. Record the remaining datagram headroom in the handover file: here it was 110 B, i.e. less than one inspection head. The customer who added it had no way of knowing, and nobody had told them.

WE3 — CHOOSING THE SYNC0 SHIFT, FOR THREE DIFFERENT MASTERS STRETCH

Problem. The repaired line: 68 slaves, 250 µs cycle, frame time 69.44 µs, system delay of the last slave 41.3 µs, PDI copy allowance 10 µs. Compute the required SYNC0 shift for three masters and say which are viable.

worst-case arrival = jitter + t_frame + D_last + t_PDI (a) soft-PLC, stock kernel, jitter ±180 µs = 180.0 + 69.44 + 41.3 + 10 = 300.7 µs > the 250 µs CYCLE → there is NO shift value that fits. Not tight — impossible (b) real-time kernel / dedicated NIC, jitter ±20 µs = 20.0 + 69.44 + 41.3 + 10 = 140.7 µs → shift 180 µs (72 % of cycle), margin 39.3 µs VIABLE (c) hardware master / master-shift enabled, jitter ±2 µs = 2.0 + 69.44 + 41.3 + 10 = 122.7 µs → shift 150 µs (60 % of cycle), margin 27.3 µs, and the cycle could safely be halved to 125 µs if the process wanted it

Read case (a) carefully, because it is the most useful result on this page. With a stock kernel the machine could not run DC at 250 µs at all — not badly, not marginally: there is no shift value that satisfies the inequality. Which means the cold-open machine, had somebody simply ticked “DC”, would have thrown 0x001A immediately and the integrator would very likely have concluded that “DC does not work on this machine” and switched it off again. That is how a correct feature acquires a bad reputation. The right conclusion from (a) is not “avoid DC” but “this master cannot serve this cycle, and now we have a number that proves it”.

Note also that (c) buys nothing today. The margin barely improves, because the frame time and the chain — not the jitter — now dominate. Spending on a hardware master here is only worthwhile if you intend to shorten the cycle, which is the honest thing to tell the person holding the budget.

WE4 — REDUNDANCY FOR TWO MACHINES, AND WHY THEY GET DIFFERENT ANSWERS CORE

Problem. Same plant, same budget meeting, two machines. Machine A: a bottle conveyor and case packer, 0.5 m/s, 4 ms cycle, 40 slaves. Machine B: the shrink-sleeve line, 6.667 m/s, 250 µs cycle, 68 slaves. Both are offered EtherCAT cable redundancy at the cost of one cable and a master licence. Recommend.

consequence of a single cable break, per machine: MACHINE A no redundancy : line stops, restart ~5 min, product intact EtherCAT ring : 4 ms → 2 mm of conveyor. Invisible MACHINE B no redundancy : line stops mid-web, ~180 m of film to re-thread, 25 min of downtime, scrap on both sides of the splice EtherCAT ring : 250 µs → 1.7 mm of film. Invisible (an MRP-class 200 ms scheme → 1.33 m — a splice, not a save)

Recommendation: fit it on B, decline it on A. Not because the technology differs — it is identical — but because the consequence differs by three orders of magnitude. Machine A's failure is a five-minute restart of an intact product flow. Machine B's failure destroys the web, and re-threading a shrink-sleeve applicator is a skilled twenty-five-minute job.

The three honesties that belong in the same recommendation. First, the ring protects the path, not the device: a drive that fails still stops the axis it controls. Second, it must be tested by pulling a cable during a witnessed acceptance test, because an untested redundancy scheme is an assumption, and the commonest defect is a ring that was never actually closed. Third, verify that the master re-handles the distributed-clock delays on switchover — on machine B a switchover that quietly corrupted DC would trade a stoppage for a scrap run, which is not an improvement.

WE5 — ERROR-COUNTER FORENSICS, AND WHY THE AVERAGE IS THE WRONG STATISTIC STRETCH

Problem. On the repaired line (250 µs cycle, drive watchdog 10 ms) the port error counter at slave 31 is accumulating about 40 bad frames per hour. The maintenance manager asks whether this will stop the machine. Answer with numbers.

frames per second = 1 / 250 µs = 4,000 frames per hour = 4,000 × 3,600 = 14.4 million 40 bad frames / hour → per-frame rate = 40 / 14.4e6 = 2.8 × 10⁻⁶ → one bad frame every 90 seconds drive watchdog 10 ms = 40 cycles one isolated bad frame = 1 lost cycle of 40 → absorbed, invisible probability of 40 CONSECUTIVE bad frames, if they were independent: (2.8e-6)⁴⁰ ≈ 0 — it will never happen by chance

So the answer appears to be “no” — and the answer is wrong, because the independence assumption is false. Electrical interference in a factory is not a Poisson process; it is bursts. A contactor closing, a VFD's output stage switching an unshielded motor cable in the same tray (→ A08), a welder striking — each produces a disturbance lasting from a few hundred microseconds to several milliseconds, during which every frame is corrupted.

the question is not “how many errors per hour?” the question is “how long is the longest burst?” burst 1 ms = 4 cycles → absorbed by a 10 ms watchdog burst 5 ms = 20 cycles → absorbed, but half the watchdog is gone burst 12 ms = 48 cycles → watchdog expires, drives to safe state, line stops 40 errors/hour in bursts of 40 = one stoppage per hour 40 errors/hour singly = never a stoppage — identical counters, completely different machines

What to do with that. The counter tells you a link is degrading and must be repaired — that part is not in doubt, and the repair is cheap. What the counter cannot tell you is whether the machine will stop, because that depends on the burst structure, which the counters do not record. So: fix the link on its merits, and separately ask what changed near that link — a new drive, a re-routed motor cable, a cable tray that now carries both. The counter localises the victim; the plant layout identifies the aggressor.

P9 — a three-line plant (10 marks). A Sri Lankan packaging plant is installing three machines, each with 60–90 EtherCAT slaves, plus a plant SCADA, an ERP interface and a requirement for remote support by the European OEM. Propose the network architecture and justify each boundary.

3 — OPC UA northbound from each master to the SCADA (→ A14) and onward to ERP. Vendor-neutral, named data, security built in — everything EtherCAT deliberately is not, at the layer where it is needed.
4 — Remote support terminates in a DMZ, never on a master. A VPN straight to a machine master is a route to unauthenticated writes on every output in that machine. Jump host, session recording, and access enabled on request rather than standing (→ A54, IEC 62443).
5 — One plant-wide standard for aliases, labelling, ESI storage and baseline counters, plus a shared spares pool. Three machines from one OEM should not need three conventions; and a common alias scheme is what makes a spare from line 1 safe to fit on line 3.

Refusals (5 marks — any three, 1 mark each, plus 2 for pairing every refusal with a cost):
(1) The switch inside the segment. Refuse: it adds 138.9 µs quiet and up to 246.7 µs more when the cameras transmit, and it silently invalidates every distributed-clock measurement. Alternative: cameras on a separate interface on the vision PC — one network card.
(2) SM-synchronous on a 4 m/s web. Refuse: at 4 m/s, every 100 µs of master jitter is 0.4 mm; the mode makes registration accuracy a property of the OEM's operating system. Alternative: DC-synchronous for the axes and the print-mark input — configuration only, plus a real-time master if the shift-time arithmetic (WE3) demands it.
(3) 100 ms watchdogs at 4 m/s. Refuse: 400 mm of blind travel with live outputs after communication stops. Alternative: watchdogs set per group from the consequence — free.
(4) No aliases. Refuse: every future spare-part swap is a silent hazard. Alternative: aliases and identification — one afternoon at build time.
(5) Standing VPN to the master. Refuse: unauthenticated write access to every output on the machine, permanently available. Alternative: jump host in a DMZ with access granted on request — and it costs the OEM nothing but a phone call.

Full marks are not available for a refusal without an alternative. “No” is a position; “no, and here is what it costs instead” is engineering.

Derivations — Where the Formulas Come From

D1 — SUMMATION FRAMING, AND EXACTLY HOW MUCH IT WINS BY STRETCH

Let N be the number of slaves and d the process data per slave in bytes. Ethernet imposes a minimum payload of 46 B and a fixed overhead on the wire of 8 (preamble and SFD) + 14 (addresses and EtherType) + 4 (FCS) + 12 (inter-frame gap) = 38 bytes.

ETHERCAT (one summing datagram): payload P = 2 + 12 + N·d (EtherCAT header + datagram header + WKC) wire W = 38 + max(P, 46) time T_ecat = W × 8 / B PER-DEVICE FRAMING (one frame per slave): wire per device w = 38 + max(d, 46) time T_dev = N × w × 8 / B

Two immediate consequences. First, the plateau: P ≤ 46 whenever N·d ≤ 32, so a frame carrying one byte and a frame carrying thirty-two bytes take exactly the same 6.72 µs at 100 Mbit/s. Second, the ceiling: the Ethernet payload cannot exceed 1500 B, so N·d ≤ 1500 − 2 − 12 = 1486, beyond which a second datagram (and usually a second frame) is required.

Now the ratio, in the region where the payload exceeds the minimum:

T_dev / T_ecat = N(38 + max(d,46)) / (38 + 2 + 12 + N·d) = N(38 + max(d,46)) / (52 + N·d) as N → ∞: T_dev / T_ecat → (38 + max(d, 46)) / d d = 12 B → 84/12 = 7.0× (at N = 68 the exact value is 6.58×) d = 46 B → 84/46 = 1.83× d = 100 B → 138/100 = 1.38× d = 400 B → 438/400 = 1.10×

This closed form is the honest statement of EtherCAT's advantage, and it is not the one the marketing makes. The advantage is not a property of EtherCAT; it is the ratio of frame overhead to useful data per device. A machine of two hundred one-byte terminals is where summation is transformative. A machine of twelve devices each carrying 400 bytes gains 10 % and would be perfectly well served by any Ethernet fieldbus. Small devices, in quantity, is the shape of problem EtherCAT was built for — and knowing that lets you say honestly when it is not the right answer.

The cost side. Against O(1) frames, EtherCAT pays O(N) in latency: the turnaround contains 2·N·t_f, one traversal outbound and one on the return. A per-device architecture on a switched line pays its own O(N) — store-and-forward at every hop, which is a far larger constant (→ A02). Both scale linearly in the chain; EtherCAT's constant is roughly an order of magnitude smaller. The structural escape from O(N) in either architecture is identical and has nothing to do with the protocol: put fewer devices in series.

D2 — THE DISTRIBUTED-CLOCK MEASUREMENT, IN FULL STRETCH

Model a chain of N slaves. Let fᵢ be slave i's port-to-port forwarding delay and dᵢ the one-way propagation delay of the link between slave i and slave i+1. The master broadcasts a write to register 0x0900; each ESC latches its local time at each port as the frame passes.

Define RTᵢ = (time latched at slave i's port 1) − (time latched at its port 0). Both timestamps come from the same counter, so slave i's unknown offset cancels exactly and its frequency error acts only over a few microseconds — a part in 10⁵ of a quantity already smaller than a microsecond. The measurement is therefore valid before any synchronisation exists.

Trace the frame from slave i's port 0: forward through slave i fᵢ travel the link dᵢ everything beyond slave i+1 RTᵢ₊₁ return through slave i+1 fᵢ₊₁ travel the link back dᵢ ---------------- RTᵢ = fᵢ + 2dᵢ + fᵢ₊₁ + RTᵢ₊₁ with the boundary condition at the tail, whose port is closed: RTₙ = fₙ (the internal fold; there is nothing beyond it) and therefore, for the tail link, fₙ is already inside RTₙ and must not be counted twice: RTₙ₋₁ = fₙ₋₁ + 2dₙ₋₁ + RTₙ

Solving downward gives the link delays directly:

dᵢ = ( RTᵢ − RTᵢ₊₁ − δᵢ ) / 2, δᵢ = fᵢ + fᵢ₊₁ (intermediate) δᵢ = fᵢ (tail link) and the system delay of slave i, its distance from the reference: Dᵢ = Σk < i ( fᵤ + dᵤ ) Identity (uniform f, symmetric links): RT₁ = 2·Σ(fᵤ + dᵤ) over the whole chain = 2·Dₙ → Dₙ = RT₁ / 2 half the master's round trip IS the last slave's system delay

The one assumption, and its exact error term. The division by two asserts that the forward and reverse paths of a link cost the same. Suppose they differ by an asymmetry a = dᵢfwd − dᵢrev. Then the algorithm returns the mean, and every slave beyond that link carries a constant offset of a/2 in its idea of the reference time.

a fibre pair with a 20 m length difference: a = 20 m × 5 ns/m = 100 ns → offset = 50 ns, permanent, invisible it does not drift, it does not alarm, it does not appear in any counter. it appears only as a machine that is always slightly wrong in one place. (the same trap, in the same form, applies to gPTP → A37)

Why this design converges so much faster than exchanging timestamps between clocks. IEEE 1588's four-timestamp exchange subtracts numbers taken by two different clocks, so the offset appears in the delay estimate and must be untangled iteratively by a servo. EtherCAT's round trip subtracts two numbers from one clock, so the offset is algebraically absent from the very first measurement. That is the whole reason a 200-slave EtherCAT segment can be synchronised within milliseconds of power-up.

D3 — WHY DELAY IS FREE AND JITTER IS NOT CORE

Let a machine element move at speed v, and let an actuation intended for time t₀ actually occur at t₀ + ε. The position error is

x_err = v · ε Decompose ε into a deterministic part D (always the same) and a random part J (different every cycle): ε = D + J CASE 1 — compensating D. Issue the command at (t₀ − D). the actuation lands at t₀ − D + D + J = t₀ + J D has been removed exactly, and it cost one subtraction, once. CASE 2 — compensating J. The same trick requires issuing the command at (t₀ − J), i.e. it requires knowing J BEFORE it happens. If J were knowable in advance it would be part of D by definition. Hence: J cannot be compensated. It can only be reduced, or made irrelevant. And errors combine as variances, so with several independent contributions: σ_x = v · sqrt( σ₁² + σ₂² + ... ) → the LARGEST term dominates; halving anything else is nearly wasted effort

Distributed clocks are the “make it irrelevant” branch. They do not reduce the master's jitter at all. They change which event actuates: instead of the arrival of a jittery frame, actuation is triggered by a local hardware pulse whose own jitter σ_DC is under 100 ns. The master's jitter is removed from the accuracy equation entirely — and reappears, transformed, as an availability constraint:

accuracy (with DC): x_err = v · σ_DC — independent of master jitter availability (with DC): J_max + t_frame + Dᵢ + t_PDI < shift so DC converts a CONTINUOUS error into a DISCRETE one: before — every cycle is slightly wrong, silently after — almost every cycle is exactly right, and the rare late one announces itself as AL status 0x001A trading a small permanent error for a rare detectable fault is almost always the right trade in a machine that makes product.

This is a general principle worth taking away from EtherCAT entirely: when you cannot remove a disturbance, look for a way to make the system insensitive to it, and prefer a failure mode that is loud and rare over one that is quiet and constant. The same reasoning governs anti-windup in PID control and diverse redundancy in functional safety.

D4 — THE WORKING COUNTER'S ALGEBRA, AND ITS IRREDUCIBLE AMBIGUITY STRETCH

For one datagram, let slave i have weight aᵢ ∈ {1, 2, 3} (read only, write only, both) and participation xᵢ ∈ {0, 1}. The working counter is a linear form:

WKC = Σᵢ aᵢ xᵢ WKC_expected = Σᵢ aᵢ deficit Δ = WKC_expected − WKC = Σᵢ aᵢ(1 − xᵢ) = Σi ∈ M aᵢ where M is the set of non-participating slaves. The diagnostic question is: given Δ, can M be recovered? Equivalently: is the map M ↦ Σi∈M aᵢ injective on subsets?

It is injective if and only if every subset of the weight multiset has a distinct sum. That condition requires the weights to grow at least as fast as powers of two (1, 2, 4, 8, …) — a Sidon-like condition on subset sums. EtherCAT's weights are confined to {1, 2, 3}, so the condition fails as soon as the machine contains, for example, two input-class devices and one output-class device: {1,1} and {2} both sum to 2. In any real machine the failure is total.

therefore, rigorously: the working counter can NEVER identify which slave is missing in a segment of more than about three devices. what it CAN do, and does perfectly: Δ = 0 → every expected slave participated, this cycle Δ > 0 → at least one did not, detected within ONE cycle Δ parity/size → weak constraints (e.g. Δ = 1 requires at least one weight-1 device to be absent) and the case people forget: WKC > expected → a slave is participating that the project does not know about — an extra device, or a mapping that overlaps two devices. Never dismiss a HIGH count.

The engineering reading. The working counter is not a weak diagnostic that could be improved; it is an optimal one for what it costs. Two bytes and one hardware increment per slave give a cycle-accurate, machine-wide participation check. Identity is deliberately left to a different mechanism (per-slave state and AL status), which is read acyclically because it is needed rarely. That separation — a cheap check every cycle, an expensive check on demand — is a design pattern worth stealing, and it appears again in every well-built monitoring system.

The trapWhy it is wrong
“The bus load is low, so the cycle is fine.”Load and turnaround are different budgets. A machine can sit at 20 % load with a turnaround exceeding the cycle, because turnaround is dominated by the chain
