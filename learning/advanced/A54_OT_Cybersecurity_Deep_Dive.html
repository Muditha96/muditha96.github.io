Why OT Is Not IT

The single idea that makes industrial security a different discipline: the consequence of failure is physical, and the system must never stop.

Operational Technology (OT) is the hardware and software that monitors and controls physical processes — PLCs, DCS, SCADA, drives, safety systems, instruments. Information Technology (IT) moves and stores data. Both need security, but their priorities are inverted, and copying an IT playbook onto a plant is how good intentions cause outages.

The Inverted Priority Triad

IT security ranks the classic C-I-A triad as Confidentiality → Integrity → Availability. Protect the data first. OT flips it to A-I-C: Availability → Integrity → Confidentiality. A stopped reactor, a tripped furnace, or a spurious valve command can injure people and destroy equipment. A leaked setpoint rarely can.

IT world

Reboot to patch. 3–5 year hardware life. Confidentiality is king. Downtime = inconvenience. Encrypt everything.

OT world

Cannot reboot a running unit. 15–25 year asset life. Availability is king. Downtime = lost production, safety risk. Determinism > encryption.

Why the IT Playbook Breaks on the Plant
IT reflexWhat it does in OT
Auto-push OS patchesReboots a controller mid-batch; unvalidated patch may break real-time drivers

"Just re-image the box"The box runs a discontinued Windows build with a costly unsupported driver stack

OT security is not "IT security, applied harder." It is a co-discipline with automation engineering — which is exactly why ISA (the automation professional body) authored the standard, and IEC adopted it.

A Short History of Why We Care
  • Stuxnet (2010) — the wake-up. Malware crossed an air gap on USB, targeted specific Siemens S7 PLCs driving uranium centrifuges, and sabotaged them while feeding normal readings to operators. It proved a cyber attack can produce a physical, targeted, deniable effect.
  • Ukraine grid (2015 & 2016) — remote attackers opened breakers and blacked out ~230,000 people; the 2016 CRASHOVERRIDE/Industroyer malware spoke grid protocols natively.
  • TRITON/TRISIS (2017) — malware targeted a Safety Instrumented System (Triconex), the last line of defence (see A52). Attacking the SIS means attacking the thing that prevents a disaster.
  • Colonial Pipeline (2021) — ransomware hit the IT billing side, but the operator shut the OT pipeline pre-emptively because IT and OT were not cleanly separated. Fuel shortages across the US east coast.

Lesson thread: the impact is physical, the safety layer is a target, and poor IT/OT separation turns an office breach into a plant shutdown.

Quick Check

An IT team proposes deploying their standard endpoint agent, which quarantines any process it doesn't recognise, onto the DCS operator stations. Why is this dangerous, and what is the OT-correct alternative?

Tap to reveal ↓A: An aggressive agent may quarantine or block a legitimate control process, freezing the operator's window into a live process — an availability and safety hazard. The proprietary control binaries are also unknown to a generic reputation engine, so false positives are likely. OT-correct alternatives: application allow-listing (only known-good executables run, no cloud reputation calls), tuned to the vendor's validated software set, in monitor mode first; endpoint protection that the automation vendor has tested and blesses; and any agent running with tested CPU limits so it can't starve the scan cycle. Security controls in OT must be validated against the control application before deployment.

The ISA/IEC 62443 Family

One numbering scheme, four groups, three roles. Learn the map and every document falls into place.

ISA/IEC 62443 is a series, not a single document. Its power is that it assigns responsibility to three distinct roles and gives each the parts that apply to them. The parts are grouped General → Policies & Procedures → System → Component.

The Three Roles

Asset Owner

Operates the plant. Owns the risk, the security programme, and the operating environment. (The 2-x parts.)

Integrator / Service Provider

Designs & commissions the automation solution and maintains it. (The 2-4 & 3-x parts.)

Product Supplier

Builds the PLC, drive, HMI or software product. (The 4-x parts.)

Security is a shared responsibility: a supplier can build a capable product (SL-C), an integrator must configure it to actually meet a target (SL-T), and the owner must operate it to keep it there (SL-A). No single party can deliver a secure plant alone.

The Two You Will Live In

62443-3-2 — Risk Assessment

How you divide the system into zones and conduits and derive a target Security Level for each. This is the engineering method (Page 6).

The catalogue of System Requirements (SRs) grouped under 7 Foundational Requirements, each with SL 1–4 enhancement levels (Page 7).

Certification & the Regulatory Pull (2025–2026)

62443 is increasingly the de facto answer to new law. The EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) imposes security requirements on products with digital elements, and the EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 (see A35) folds cybersecurity into machine safety. In the US the NIST framework and sector rules (e.g. TSA pipeline directives after Colonial) point the same way. Certification schemes — ISASecure (CSA, SDLA, SSA) and IECEE — let a supplier or integrator demonstrate conformance. The direction of travel: 62443 conformance is moving from "good practice" toward "market-access requirement."

Quick Check

A vendor's brochure says their PLC is "62443 certified to SL 3." A colleague concludes the plant is therefore SL 3. Where is the error?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The PLC being certified means its component capability (SL-C, per 62443-4-2) can reach SL 3 if configured and integrated correctly. The plant's achieved level (SL-A) depends on how the integrator wired the zones and conduits (62443-3-3) and how the owner operates the programme (62443-2-1). A SL-3-capable component in a flat network with default passwords achieves nothing like SL 3. Capability ≠ target ≠ achieved.

The Purdue Model & the IIoT Break

The reference architecture that structured OT security for 30 years — and why Industry 4.0 is quietly dismantling it.

The Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture (PERA) layers a plant into levels by function and timescale. It gives everyone a shared mental model of "where a device sits" and, crucially, where the boundaries that need protecting are.

The Purdue Levels
LevelNameWhat lives hereTimescale
L4/5Enterprise / BusinessERP, email, internet, corporate ITDays–months
L3.5DMZHistorian replica, patch server, remote-access jump host, data diode—
L3Site OperationsSCADA servers, historians, MES, engineering workstationsSeconds–minutes
L2Area SupervisoryHMIs, local control room, batch serversSub-second
L1Basic ControlPLCs, DCS controllers, RTUsms
L0ProcessSensors, actuators, valves, motors, the physicsμs–ms

The key control point is the L3.5 DMZ: no traffic should flow directly between enterprise IT (L4/5) and the control network (≤L3). Everything brokers through the DMZ, so a compromised office PC cannot talk straight to a PLC.

The Golden Rules (Traditional Purdue)
  • Traffic flows vertically through boundaries, never around them. No L4→L1 shortcuts.
  • A Safety Instrumented System (SIS) sits alongside L1 but on its own segregated network — an attacker in the BPCS must not reach the SIS (the TRITON lesson).
  • Real-time control lives low (L0–L2); anything that can tolerate latency lives high.
  • Lower is more trusted and less patchable; higher is less trusted and more exposed. Security effort concentrates at the seams.
Why IIoT Shatters It (the 2025 problem)

Industry 4.0 (see A33) puts computation and cloud connectivity at the bottom of the pyramid. A modern "smart" sensor at L0 may have an onboard CPU, a TCP/IP stack, and a direct MQTT link to AWS or Azure — bypassing L1, L2, L3 and the DMZ in one hop. The neat vertical layering assumed low-level devices were dumb. They no longer are.

The break

An L0/L1 IIoT device with a north-bound cloud connection is simultaneously in the most-trusted zone and talking to the internet. Purdue's boundary model has no box for it.

The response

62443-1-6 (2025) addresses IIoT devices explicitly. Architecturally the answer is zone-based / zero-trust thinking: stop trusting a device because of where it sits; trust it because of what it proves (identity, authentication, least privilege) on every connection — including its cloud link, which becomes its own conduit with its own controls.

Purdue Isn't Dead — It's a Logical Model Now

The pragmatic 2026 view: Purdue remains an excellent logical reference for reasoning about trust and data flow, even where the physical network is flatter and IIoT devices break the strict hierarchy. You keep the DMZ and the safety segregation, and you supplement "trust by layer" with explicit per-conduit authentication so a flattened or cloud-connected topology is still governed. Zones & conduits (Page 4) are the tool that survives the flattening.

Quick Check

A vibration sensor (see A42) is installed on a critical pump with a built-in 4G modem that streams data to the vendor's cloud dashboard for predictive maintenance. In Purdue terms, what is the problem and how do you govern it?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The device sits physically at L0/L1 (most trusted) but its 4G link is an uncontrolled conduit straight to the internet, bypassing every Purdue boundary and the DMZ. Governance: treat the cloud link as its own conduit with explicit controls — put the device in its own monitoring/IIoT zone that is read-only toward the process and cannot inject commands; ensure the modem cannot route into the control LAN (physical/logical isolation); require mutual authentication and encryption on the cloud link; and confirm the vendor's product meets 62443-4-2 / 1-6. If it can only observe and is isolated from the control path, the exposure is bounded to data confidentiality, not process integrity.

Zones & Conduits

The central engineering abstraction of 62443. Get this right and the rest is bookkeeping.

A zone is a grouping of assets that share the same security requirements — the same trust level and consequence. A conduit is a special zone that carries the communication between zones and controls what may cross. You segment the plant into zones, connect them only through defined conduits, and apply controls at each conduit boundary. This is "defence in depth" made concrete and auditable.

Definitions That Matter

Zone

Assets grouped by shared security needs. Can be physical (a room), logical (a VLAN), or both. Has a boundary, a target SL, and a defined list of what's inside.

Conduit

The controlled channel between zones. It has endpoints, the protocols it carries, and the security controls (firewall rules, authentication, encryption) applied to it. Nothing crosses a zone boundary except through a conduit.

How You Draw Them
  • Group by consequence, not convenience. The safety system, the basic control system, and the enterprise all have different consequences of compromise → different zones. Two PLCs doing the same critical job with the same exposure can share a zone.
  • Separate the SIS. The Safety Instrumented System is always its own zone with the tightest conduit — ideally one-way or read-only from the BPCS. This is the TRITON-proofing principle.
  • The DMZ is a zone of conduits. L3.5 exists precisely so that IT↔OT data exchange happens in a controlled buffer, never as a direct conduit from enterprise to control.
  • Wireless, remote access and cloud links are conduits — each needs its own controls, not a hole in the firewall.
  • Smaller zones = better containment but more conduits to manage. Balance blast-radius against operational overhead.
A Reference Zone/Conduit Diagram

Every arrow is a conduit with its own firewall rules, protocol allow-list and authentication. The dashed purple path is the IIoT problem from Page 3 — an unmanaged conduit that skips the DMZ. Finding and governing those is half the real job.

Controls You Put on a Conduit

Quick Check

You need to export process data to a cloud analytics platform for predictive maintenance, but the security team forbids any inbound path to the control zone. What conduit design satisfies both?

Tap to reveal ↓A: A one-way conduit. Put a historian mirror in the DMZ (L3.5); replicate data up to it from the control zone through a strictly outbound-only rule — ideally a hardware data diode that makes an inbound path physically impossible. The cloud platform then pulls from the DMZ historian, never from the control zone. Data flows out for analytics; no command can ever flow back in. This is exactly why the DMZ zone exists.

Security Levels (SL 1–4)

A vector, not a score. The insight that stops teams over- or under-spending on security.

A Security Level expresses how capable an attacker the zone must resist. It is defined by the means, resources, skills and motivation of the threat you're defending against — deliberately parallel to how SIL in A52 expresses risk-reduction capability.

The Four Levels
SLProtect against…Attacker profile
SL 1Casual / coincidental violationNo specific intent — accidental misuse, careless staff, commodity malware that wandered in
SL 2Intentional, low resources, generic skills, low motivationA hacker with common tools; a disgruntled insider without specialist knowledge
SL 3Intentional, moderate resources, IACS-specific skills, moderate motivationA skilled attacker who understands control systems — hacktivist group, sophisticated criminal
SL 4Intentional, extended resources, IACS-specific skills, high motivationNation-state; the Stuxnet/TRITON class of adversary

Most industrial zones target SL 2; genuinely critical infrastructure and safety zones aim for SL 3. SL 4 is rare, expensive and reserved for the highest-consequence national assets — you do not put SL 4 everywhere any more than you put SIL 4 on every loop.

The Three Flavours of SL — Don't Confuse Them

SL-T (Target)

What a zone needs, derived from risk assessment (62443-3-2). The requirement.

SL-C (Capability)

What a component or system can deliver if configured right (62443-4-2 / 3-3). The vendor claim.

SL-A (Achieved)

What the installed, operating system actually delivers. The audited reality.

Goal for every zone:   SL-A  ≥  SL-T   (and this requires SL-C ≥ SL-T and correct integration & operation)

SL Is a Vector Across the 7 Foundational Requirements

A zone's SL is not one number — it's a 7-element vector, one SL per Foundational Requirement (Page 7). You might need SL 3 for Use Control (who can issue commands) but only SL 1 for Data Confidentiality (the setpoints aren't secret). Writing it as a vector stops you paying for confidentiality you don't need while under-protecting the access control you do.

SL-T(zone) = { IAC 3, UC 3, SI 2, DC 1, RDF 2, TRE 2, RA 2 }

(IAC = Identification & Authentication Control, UC = Use Control, SI = System Integrity, DC = Data Confidentiality, RDF = Restricted Data Flow, TRE = Timely Response to Events, RA = Resource Availability.)

Quick Check

A team sets every zone in the plant — including the office historian mirror — to SL 3 "to be safe." Why is this poor engineering?

Tap to reveal ↓A: SL is risk-driven, like SIL. Setting everything to SL 3 wastes money and effort on low-consequence zones (the office historian mirror can't hurt the process) while diluting attention from the zones that genuinely need SL 3 (safety, critical control). It also imposes operational friction — heavy authentication, tighter controls — where it buys little. Correct practice: run the 62443-3-2 risk assessment per zone, target SL to the actual consequence and threat, and express it as a per-FR vector so you protect what matters at the level it matters.

Risk Assessment (62443-3-2)

The method that turns "we should be more secure" into a defensible per-zone target. The engineering heart of the standard.

62443-3-2 gives a workflow from a system under consideration (SUC) to a set of zones, conduits and target SLs, with documented residual risk. It mirrors the HAZOP→LOPA logic of process safety (A52) but for cyber threats.

The Workflow (ZCR steps)
StepActivityOutput
ZCR 1Identify the System Under Consideration (boundary of the assessment)Asset inventory & scope
ZCR 2Perform an initial (high-level) risk assessmentWorst-case unmitigated risk — is detailed work justified?
ZCR 3Partition the SUC into zones & conduitsZone/conduit diagram
ZCR 4Decide if initial risk exceeds tolerable riskGo/stop gate for detailed assessment
ZCR 5Detailed risk assessment per zone/conduitLikelihood × consequence, existing controls, gaps
ZCR 6Document results & assign SL-T per zone/conduitThe risk assessment report + residual risk sign-off
The Risk Equation

Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Consequence

You reduce risk by attacking any factor: lower threat exposure (segmentation, no internet path), reduce vulnerability (patching, hardening, removing services), or limit consequence (safety systems, physical relief, fail-safe design). Because you can rarely change an adversary's motivation, most cyber effort targets vulnerability and exposure — which is exactly what zones/conduits and hardening do.

62443-3-2 also introduces the idea of cyber-security PHA: consequence is frequently taken from the existing process-safety hazard study, so a cyber event's worst physical outcome is already quantified. The cyber assessment then asks "how likely is a threat to reach that consequence, given our controls?"

Setting SL-T From Consequence & Threat

A practical matrix crosses the consequence severity (from the PHA — minor, serious, major, catastrophic) with the threat likelihood / attacker capability to land on a target SL. High consequence + capable adversary → SL 3. Low consequence + casual threat → SL 1. This is the cyber twin of the SIL-selection matrix, and it keeps spending proportionate.

Quick Check

Your initial (ZCR 2) assessment shows a zone whose worst-case compromise is a nuisance data error, well below tolerable risk. What does 62443-3-2 let you do, and what must you still record?

Tap to reveal ↓A: If the initial high-level risk is already below the tolerable threshold (ZCR 4 gate), you may skip the detailed per-zone assessment for that zone — the method is risk-proportionate and doesn't force exhaustive analysis where consequence is trivially low. But you must still document the decision: the zone definition, the initial risk finding, the tolerable-risk criterion used, and the rationale for stopping. Undocumented "we judged it low" is the audit finding; a recorded, criteria-based screen-out is defensible engineering.

The 7 Foundational Requirements

The backbone of 62443-3-3 and 4-2. Every technical control in the standard hangs off one of these seven.

The Foundational Requirements (FRs) are the seven categories that all System Requirements (SRs) and Component Requirements (CRs) organise under. Learn the seven and you have the shape of the entire technical standard.

FR 1–7
#Foundational RequirementPlain meaningExample control
FR1Identification & Authentication Control (IAC)Prove who (or what) you areUnique user IDs, MFA on remote access, device certificates
FR2Use Control (UC)Enforce what an authenticated identity may doRole-based access, least privilege, session lock, authorised command lists
FR3System Integrity (SI)Data & software are not tampered withSigned firmware, malware protection, communication integrity checks
FR4Data Confidentiality (DC)Keep sensitive data secretEncryption at rest/in transit for recipes, credentials, config
FR5Restricted Data Flow (RDF)Segment the network; control what crossesZones & conduits, firewalls, DMZ, data diodes
FR6Timely Response to Events (TRE)Detect, log, and react to security eventsAudit logging, SIEM, alerting, forensic evidence, IR plan
FR7Resource Availability (RA)Keep the control system running under stressDoS resistance, backups, redundancy, resource limits

Mnemonic: "I Use Some Data — Restrict, Track, Availability." FR7 (Availability) being last on the list but first in OT priority is the recurring irony — the OT world reads C-I-A backwards, and FR7 is where the plant-floor stakes live.

SL Enhances Each FR

Under each FR sit specific SRs, and each SR has Requirement Enhancements (RE) that ratchet the strength up SL 1→4. Example for FR1 authentication:

SLFR1 authentication strength (illustrative)
SL 1Identify & authenticate human users (basic passwords)
SL 2+ unique per-user IDs, authenticate all users & devices, harden against guessing
SL 3+ multi-factor for untrusted networks, public-key / certificate auth, protect authenticators
SL 4+ MFA for all access, strongest cryptographic device identity
System vs Component View

62443-3-3 (System SRs)

"The zone shall enforce MFA on remote access." A property of how the system is assembled and configured.

62443-4-2 (Component CRs)

"The PLC shall support certificate-based authentication." A capability the product must ship with so the system can meet its SR.

The integrator's job is to compose SL-C-rated components (4-2) into a system that meets the SL-T-rated SRs (3-3) for each zone. Both use the same seven FRs, which is why the framework composes cleanly.

Quick Check

A plant encrypts every OT link end-to-end (strong FR4) but shares one generic admin login across all engineers with no logging (weak FR1, FR2, FR6). Under 62443, is this zone "secure"?

Tap to reveal ↓A: No. Security under 62443 is the vector across all seven FRs, not one strong dimension. This zone has excellent Data Confidentiality (FR4) but near-zero Identification & Authentication (FR1), Use Control (FR2) and event Response (FR6) — a shared unlogged admin account means no accountability, no least privilege, and no forensic trail. An attacker (or careless insider) with that one credential owns the zone, and you'd never know who. The SL vector is only as strong as its weakest FR relative to its target; over-investing in FR4 doesn't compensate for a hole in FR1.

Secure Development & the Supply Chain

62443-4-1: security has to be built into the product, not bolted onto the plant. And you can't secure what your suppliers left broken.

62443-4-1 defines a Secure Development Lifecycle (SDL) for product suppliers — the process that produces PLCs, drives and software that can be secured. It's the cyber analogue of the safety lifecycle: security is a property of how the thing was made, verified across eight practices.

The 8 SDL Practices (62443-4-1)
#PracticeWhat it demands
SMSecurity ManagementA defined, resourced process owner & governance
SRSpecification of Security RequirementsThreat model & security requirements per product
SDSecure by DesignDefence-in-depth, least privilege, secure defaults in the architecture
SISecure ImplementationSecure coding, avoid known-bad patterns
SVVSecurity Verification & Validation TestingPen-test, fuzzing, vulnerability scanning before release
DMDefect ManagementTrack & triage security defects through their life
SUMSecurity Update ManagementDeliver patches & advisories to customers
SGSecurity GuidelinesShip hardening & secure-deployment docs to the integrator
The Supply Chain: SBOM & Vulnerability Disclosure (2025–2026 focus)

Modern controllers embed open-source libraries, RTOS components and third-party stacks. A vulnerability in one library (think of the industrial "Ripple20", "URGENT/11" or "OT:ICEFALL" families of TCP/IP-stack bugs) can silently affect thousands of devices from dozens of vendors.

  • SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) — an ingredient list of every component in the firmware. Increasingly mandated (US Executive Order, EU CRA) so an owner can answer "am I affected by CVE-X?" in minutes, not months.
  • Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) — suppliers publish security advisories (via ICS-CERT / CISA and their own PSIRT) with CVE IDs, CVSS scores and mitigations. Reading these is a core asset-owner duty.
  • Provenance & signing — firmware and updates must be cryptographically signed so a device rejects tampered images (this is what Stuxnet exploited the absence of).
  • Third-party / integrator risk — the 62443-2-4 requirements exist because integrators and service providers are a major attack path (remote-access tools, shared laptops, weak credentials brought on-site).
CVSS in an OT Context — Read It Twice

A CVE's CVSS base score is IT-centric: it assumes availability is least important. In OT you must re-score for your context — a "medium" CVSS bug that causes a controller to crash may be critical to you because availability is king, while a "critical" data-leak bug on an isolated PLC may be low-risk in practice. Use CVSS as an input, temper it with your zone/consequence analysis, and prioritise by reachability (can a threat actually get to it, given your conduits?).

Quick Check

A CISA advisory reports a CVSS 9.8 remote-code-execution flaw in a protocol stack used by one of your PLC models. Your first action is to patch immediately. Why might that be the wrong first move in OT?

Tap to reveal ↓A: In OT, the first move is assess reachability and consequence, then plan — not reflexively patch a running controller. Ask: is that PLC actually reachable by the threat, given your zones/conduits? (If it's in a segmented zone with no path to an attacker, the effective risk is far below 9.8.) Is the vendor patch validated for your firmware/application, and can you afford the reboot? Often the correct immediate action is a compensating control — tighten the conduit firewall to block the vulnerable protocol/port, add monitoring — which buys time to schedule and test the real patch during a planned outage. Rushing an unvalidated patch onto a live safety-critical controller can cause the very outage the attacker wanted.

Patching, Hardening & Backups

The day-to-day defensive hygiene — done the OT way, where you cannot simply reboot to fix.

Most real-world compromise doesn't need a nation-state exploit; it walks in through default passwords, unpatched decade-old CVEs, flat networks and USB sticks. The unglamorous controls stop the majority of attacks — the trick is doing them without breaking availability.

Hardening — Reduce the Attack Surface First
  • Disable unused services & ports — the web server, FTP, Telnet and discovery protocols shipped "on" by default are free attacker footholds. Turn off what the application doesn't need.
  • Change every default credential — the single highest-value, lowest-cost control. Default passwords are published in manuals.
  • Least functionality & least privilege — engineering workstations shouldn't browse the web or read email; operators shouldn't have programming rights.
  • Physical security — lock panels, disable/epoxy unused USB ports, control the key switch. Stuxnet and many insider events are physical-access stories.
  • Application allow-listing on Windows-based HMIs/workstations — far more OT-appropriate than signature antivirus because the software set is fixed and known.
Patching Without Rebooting the Plant
RealityOT patching practice
Can't reboot a running unitPatch during planned outages / turnarounds; maintain a patch backlog with risk-ranking
Unvalidated patch may break controlTest on an offline replica / test bench before production; wait for vendor validation
Some devices are unpatchable (EoL)Compensating controls: isolate in a tighter zone, virtual-patch at an industrial firewall, monitor closely
Patch delivery is slow in OTRisk-based schedule: patch internet-reachable & high-consequence first; deep-zone legacy last

Virtual patching — blocking exploitation of a known vulnerability at a network device (IPS / industrial firewall rule) without touching the vulnerable asset — is the OT world's escape hatch for the unpatchable. It doesn't fix the flaw; it makes it unreachable.

Backups & Recovery — the Ransomware Antidote

The most likely serious incident a typical plant faces in 2026 is not Stuxnet — it's ransomware reaching OT (usually via the IT side or a shared engineering laptop). The defence that actually works is tested recovery:

  • 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, 2 media, 1 off-site/offline. An offline (air-gapped) backup ransomware can't encrypt is the point.
  • Back up controller programs, HMI projects, network configs and full system images — not just data. You need to rebuild a controller from bare metal.
  • Test the restore. An untested backup is a hope, not a control. Rehearse rebuilding a station within your recovery-time objective (RTO).
  • Keep golden images and documented configurations so a compromised device can be re-flashed to a known-good state quickly.
Secure Remote Access — the Perennial Hole

Vendor remote support and work-from-anywhere engineering are operational necessities and the #1 real-world entry path. Do it right: no direct inbound to OT; a brokered jump host in the DMZ; MFA; session recording & logging; time-boxed, approved access that is off by default; and no shared/persistent VPN tunnels that live forever. "The vendor left a permanent VPN with a shared password" is a recurring root cause.

Quick Check

A critical PLC runs firmware with a known RCE flaw, but the vendor has ended support and no patch exists. It must stay in service for three more years. What is the 62443-aligned response?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Compensating controls, documented as accepted residual risk. Since you can't patch, you make the flaw unreachable and detectable: put the PLC in its own tight zone with a restrictive conduit (only the exact protocol/port the process needs, from only the exact source it needs); virtual-patch the specific exploit at an industrial firewall/IPS; add monitoring to detect anomalous access; enforce strict physical & remote-access control; and keep tested backups/golden image for fast recovery. Record the decision, the controls, and the residual risk with management sign-off, and put the device on the replacement roadmap. This is FR5 (restricted data flow) + FR6 (response) compensating for an unfixable FR3 gap.

Monitoring & Incident Response

You will not keep every attacker out. FR6 is about seeing them and responding without making things worse.

Prevention (segmentation, hardening) is necessary but not sufficient. Detection and a rehearsed response are what separate a contained event from a headline. In OT, both must respect the golden rule: the safe state of the process comes first.

OT Monitoring — Why It's Different
  • Passive first. Prefer passive network monitoring (a SPAN/mirror port feeding an OT-aware IDS) over active scanning that can crash fragile devices. You watch the traffic; you don't poke the PLCs.
  • Baseline the normal. OT traffic is remarkably regular — the same devices talk the same protocols at the same rates. That predictability is a gift: anomaly detection works far better here than in chaotic IT. A PLC suddenly receiving a firmware-write, or a new device appearing, stands out.
  • Protocol awareness. An OT IDS understands Modbus, DNP3, EtherNet/IP, OPC UA, S7 — so it can flag "a write to a safety register from an unexpected source", not just "TCP packet".
  • Asset inventory is prerequisite. You can't detect a rogue device if you never knew what "all devices" was. Continuous, passive asset discovery underpins everything (and is the first thing most programmes lack).
SIEM, Logging & the SOC

Audit logs from HMIs, workstations, firewalls and the OT IDS feed a SIEM for correlation and alerting. Best practice in 2026 is a converged or dedicated OT SOC where analysts understand control systems — an IT SOC that treats "engineer downloaded a PLC program" as routine will miss the one time it's an attacker. Keep logs on a system the attacker can't easily erase (write-once / off-zone).

"Shut It Down" Is a Safety Decision, Not an IT One

The instinct from IT — "pull the plug, contain the breach" — can be the most dangerous action on a plant. Killing power to a running furnace, reactor or pipeline mid-process can create the very hazard the SIS exists to prevent. In OT, the containment decision is made jointly with operations and safety, and often the right move is a controlled, orderly shutdown to a safe state rather than an abrupt kill. The IR plan must pre-authorise who makes that call and how.

Quick Check

Your OT IDS flags an engineering workstation issuing program downloads to three PLCs at 03:00 — outside any maintenance window. Walk through the first response steps in OT-correct order.

Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) Analyse before acting — confirm it's genuinely anomalous (no approved change, no ticket) and not a legitimate remote session; check what was written. (2) Preserve evidence — capture logs/traffic before anything is changed. (3) Assess process state — are the PLCs currently controlling a live process safely? Loop in operations. (4) Contain proportionately — cut the workstation's network access (kill its conduit / disable the remote-access session) to stop further writes, rather than dropping the PLCs that are running the process. (5) Verify PLC integrity — compare running programs against golden images; if tampered, plan a controlled recovery with operations, not an abrupt trip. (6) Escalate per the IR plan. The theme: stop the attacker's access, keep the process safe, recover deliberately.

Standards describe the destination; a programme is how you get there without boiling the ocean. 62443-2-1 calls this the CSMS (Cyber Security Management System) — the OT analogue of an ISO 27001 ISMS, but tuned to control systems.

People & Governance — the Half Everyone Skips
  • Ownership. Name an OT security owner who bridges automation and IT security. "Everyone's job" = no one's job.
  • Training & culture. The operator who plugs in a found USB, the engineer who reuses a password, the manager who approves a permanent vendor tunnel — awareness is a control (62443-2-1 requires it).
  • Management of Change. Every network/config change goes through MoC — the same discipline as process safety. The "temporary" firewall rule that became permanent is a classic root cause.
  • Third-party governance. Integrators & vendors get 62443-2-4-style requirements written into contracts: their laptops, their remote access, their credentials are your attack surface.

Quick Check

A site has a modest budget and can do exactly one thing this year. Which single control gives the greatest real-world risk reduction, and why?

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

The whole module in one screen, the six ways OT security programmes fail, and five exam-grade checks.

The 8 Ideas That Matter
  • OT flips the triad to Availability → Integrity → Confidentiality; the consequence of failure is physical and the system can't stop.
  • 62443 is a family with three roles (owner / integrator / supplier) — security is a shared responsibility, and capability (SL-C) ≠ target (SL-T) ≠ achieved (SL-A).
  • Purdue gives the layered reference & the DMZ; IIoT breaks it, and zones/conduits + zero-trust thinking survive the flattening.
  • Zones & conduits are the engineering core: group by consequence, connect only through controlled conduits, segregate the SIS.
  • Security Levels 1–4 express attacker capability as a per-FR vector; target them by risk, not everywhere at max.
  • 62443-3-2 risk assessment turns consequence × threat into a defensible SL-T; the 7 FRs organise every technical control.
  • Prevention + detection + recovery: harden & segment, monitor passively, keep tested offline backups — ransomware makes recovery the decisive control.
  • "Shut it down" is a safety decision; containment is weighed against safe operation, made jointly with operations.

① Flat network

No IT/OT boundary or DMZ. One office phishing email reaches a PLC. Fix: segment first — it's the highest-leverage control.

② Rogue conduits

Forgotten vendor VPN, a 4G modem on a "smart" sensor, an engineer's hotspot. Invisible internet paths into OT. Fix: asset & connection discovery; govern every conduit.

③ Default credentials

Published passwords, shared admin logins, no MFA. The cheapest attack there is. Fix: change defaults, unique IDs, MFA on remote access.

④ Patch paralysis

Either reckless auto-patching that breaks control, or years-old CVEs never touched. Fix: risk-based schedule + compensating/virtual patching for the unpatchable.

⑤ Untested backups

Backups exist but no one ever restored one; or they're online and ransomware encrypts them too. Fix: 3-2-1 with an offline copy, rehearse the restore to your RTO.

⑥ IT-style incident response

"Pull the plug" on a live process; no OT IR plan; IT SOC blind to control-system context. Fix: OT IR plan, joint safety/ops containment, controlled shutdown to safe state.

Final Quiz

Q1. Explain why "SL-C 3 component" does not make a "SL 3 plant".

Tap to reveal ↓A: SL-C is the component's capability if configured correctly (62443-4-2). Achieved level (SL-A) also needs the integrator to build the zones/conduits to meet the system SRs (3-3) and the owner to operate the programme (2-1). A capable component in a flat network with default passwords achieves nothing near SL 3. Capability ≠ target ≠ achieved.

Q2. Why is the Purdue model said to be "breaking", and what replaces the assumption it relied on?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Purdue assumed low-level devices were dumb and traffic flowed strictly through layer boundaries. IIoT devices at L0/L1 now have onboard compute and direct cloud links, bypassing the DMZ — the layering assumption fails. It's replaced by zero-trust / per-conduit authentication: trust a device for what it proves (identity, least privilege) on each connection, not for where it sits. Purdue survives as a logical reference; zones & conduits are the tool that adapts.

Q3. A zone's SL-T is written {IAC 3, UC 3, SI 2, DC 1, RDF 2, TRE 2, RA 2}. What does the DC 1 tell a designer, and why isn't it a weakness?

Tap to reveal ↓A: SL is a per-FR vector. DC 1 (Data Confidentiality) says the data in this zone isn't secret enough to justify strong confidentiality controls — so don't spend on encrypting non-sensitive setpoints. It's not a weakness because confidentiality isn't the risk here; access control and command authority are, hence IAC 3 and UC 3. Targeting each FR to its actual risk is the whole point — it prevents wasting effort on DC while properly funding the FRs that matter.

Q4. Why can a CVSS 9.8 be lower-priority in OT than a CVSS 5.0, in the right circumstances?

Tap to reveal ↓A: CVSS base scores are IT-centric and ignore reachability and OT's availability-first priorities. A 9.8 RCE on a PLC that sits in a segmented zone with no path an attacker can reach is effectively low-risk in your context; a 5.0 flaw that crashes a controller reachable from a poorly-segmented zone may be critical because availability is king and it's exploitable. Re-score CVEs against your zones/conduits (can a threat get there?) and consequence, using CVSS as one input, not the verdict.

Q5. During a suspected intrusion on a live reactor, IT wants to immediately power off the control network. Give the OT-correct position.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Abruptly powering off a live reactor's control network can create a physical hazard worse than the intrusion — losing control/visibility of an exothermic process is exactly what the safety system is meant to prevent. The OT-correct position: containment is a joint safety/operations decision. Contain the attacker's access (isolate the compromised workstation, cut the malicious conduit, disable remote sessions) while keeping the process under control, then perform a deliberate, controlled shutdown to a safe state if needed. Preserve evidence, and recover from golden images/backups. The process's safe state ranks above rapid IT-style containment.
Memory Hook

"Availability first, group by consequence, guard every conduit — you can't stop every attacker, so segment to contain, monitor to see, and back up to recover."

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems worked end-to-end — the reasoning an assessor wants to see, not just the answer.

WE-1 · Deriving a Target SL From Consequence & Threat

A batch chemical reactor's basic control zone: worst-case cyber compromise (from the PHA) is a runaway leading to a vessel rupture — catastrophic. The credible threat is a financially-motivated criminal group with control-system tooling — IACS-skilled. Existing controls: segmented zone, MFA remote access, but shared engineering login and no monitoring.

Consequence = Catastrophic (4)  →  drives SL up
Threat capability = IACS-skilled (3)  →  SL-T floor = 3
Matrix(Catastrophic, IACS-skilled) ⇒ SL-T = 3 (SL 4 reserved for nation-state / national-critical)

Now the per-FR refinement. Command authority to the reactor is the dominant risk → UC 3, IAC 3. Program/firmware integrity matters (TRITON-class) → SI 3. Setpoints aren't secret → DC 1. Segmentation is central → RDF 3. Detection is currently absent but needed at this consequence → TRE 3. Availability of control is safety-relevant → RA 2–3.

SL-T = { IAC 3, UC 3, SI 3, DC 1, RDF 3, TRE 3, RA 3 }

Gap analysis vs current SL-A: shared login fails IAC 3 & UC 3 (need unique IDs + least privilege); no monitoring fails TRE 3 (need passive OT IDS + logging). These two gaps are the funded work; segmentation & MFA already meet RDF/part of IAC.

Insight: a single scalar "SL 3" hides the actual work. The vector plus a gap analysis converts a risk assessment directly into a prioritised, costed remediation list — and shows an auditor exactly why each control exists.

WE-2 · Conduit Design for a Cloud Analytics Feed

Management wants pump vibration & process data (see A42) in a cloud PdM platform. Constraint: security policy forbids any inbound path to the control zone (SL-T RDF 3). Design the data path.

Solution architecture:

Control zone (L1/2) → [outbound-only replication] → Historian mirror in DMZ (L3.5) → [cloud pulls from DMZ] → Cloud PdM

  • Control→DMZ conduit: strictly outbound, single protocol (e.g. historian replication or OPC UA read), firewall rule source=historian dest=DMZ-mirror only. For SL 3 assurance, enforce with a hardware data diode so an inbound path is physically impossible, not just rule-based.
  • DMZ→Cloud conduit: TLS, mutual authentication, the cloud initiates the pull from the DMZ; the DMZ never holds a credential into the control zone.
  • No credential, route or trust relationship exists from cloud → DMZ → control. A full cloud compromise reaches, at worst, a read-only DMZ copy.

Residual risk = data confidentiality of the mirrored dataset only. Process integrity risk ≈ 0 (no command path exists).

Insight: "get data out without letting anything in" is the archetypal OT conduit problem, and the data diode is the archetypal answer — it moves the guarantee from configuration (a rule someone can fat-finger) to physics (light only goes one way down the fibre).

WE-3 · Prioritising CVEs by Reachability

Three advisories land in one week. Rank them for a plant whose control zone is well-segmented behind a DMZ, with one legacy HMI exposed to the corporate network through a mis-configured rule.

CVECVSSAffected assetReachability
A9.8 RCEPLC deep in segmented zoneNo path from any threat source
B7.5 DoSLegacy HMI reachable from corporate net (mis-config)Directly reachable from IT
C5.3 info-leakHistorian mirror in DMZReachable but read-only, non-sensitive data

Effective risk ≈ CVSS × reachability × consequence
B: 7.5 × reachable × (HMI DoS loses operator view = high consequence) → rank #1, act now
A: 9.8 × unreachable × catastrophic → base score scary but effective risk low → rank #2, schedule patch
C: 5.3 × reachable × low consequence (read-only, non-secret) → rank #3, routine

Immediate actions: for B, fix the firewall mis-config today (removes reachability — a free "virtual patch") and schedule the HMI patch; for A, verify segmentation still holds, then patch at the next window; for C, log & batch it.

Insight: the highest CVSS is not the top priority. Reachability — a direct product of your zone/conduit design — reorders the list, and the cheapest fix (correcting one firewall rule) beats the scariest number.

WE-4 · Attack-Path Likelihood & the Value of Segmentation

Model an office→OT ransomware path as a chain of steps that each must succeed. Rough per-step success probabilities before segmentation: phish an office user 0.5, pivot office→server 0.6, reach flat OT LAN 0.8, encrypt reachable OT hosts 0.7.

P(reach & damage OT)_before = 0.5 × 0.6 × 0.8 × 0.7 ≈ 0.168 (≈ 1 in 6 per serious campaign)

Now insert a DMZ + segmentation that cuts the "reach OT LAN" step from 0.8 to 0.05 (must defeat the boundary) and reduces "encrypt reachable OT" to 0.3 (fewer hosts reachable, some unpatchable ones now isolated):

P(...)_after = 0.5 × 0.6 × 0.05 × 0.3 ≈ 0.0045  →  a ~37× reduction from one architectural control

Insight: because the path is a product of independent steps, hammering the one step you control best — the IT/OT boundary — collapses the whole chain. This is the quantitative case behind "segment first": it's the term in the product you can push closest to zero, and it protects everything behind it at once. (This is the cyber cousin of the SIF/LOPA decade-arithmetic in A52.)

Problem Set

Ten problems from recall to design judgement. Work each fully before revealing.

P1. State the OT priority triad and give one concrete reason each ordering differs from IT.

Tap to reveal ↓A: OT = Availability → Integrity → Confidentiality. Availability first: a stopped process can injure people/destroy equipment (vs IT downtime = inconvenience). Integrity second: a corrupted setpoint/command has physical consequence. Confidentiality last: leaked setpoints rarely cause physical harm. IT is C-I-A because its asset is data.

P2. Match each 62443 part to its role: 2-1, 2-4, 3-3, 4-2.

Tap to reveal ↓A: 2-1 → Asset Owner (security programme / CSMS). 2-4 → Service Provider / Integrator (requirements for service providers). 3-3 → Integrator (system security requirements & SLs). 4-2 → Product Supplier (component technical requirements).

P3. A device is claimed "SL-C 3". List the two additional conditions needed before its zone can be SL-A 3.

Tap to reveal ↓A: (1) The integrator must configure zones/conduits and system controls so the zone's SRs meet SL-T 3 across all seven FRs (62443-3-3) — e.g. actually enable the device's MFA/cert auth, restrict the conduits. (2) The asset owner must operate the programme (62443-2-1) so it stays there — patching, access management, monitoring, MoC. Capability + correct integration + sustained operation = achieved.

P4. Why must the SIS be its own zone, and what conduit type ideally connects it to the BPCS?

Tap to reveal ↓A: The SIS is the last line of defence against a physical catastrophe (see A52); an attacker who reaches it can disable protection while attacking the process (the TRITON scenario). Isolating it in its own zone with the tightest possible conduit — ideally one-way / read-only from BPCS to SIS (the SIS may publish status out, but the BPCS cannot write to the SIS) — means compromising the control system does not compromise safety. Physical separation and independent credentials reinforce it.

P5. Give the 3-2-1 backup rule and explain which element specifically defeats ransomware.

Tap to reveal ↓A: 3 copies, on 2 different media, with 1 off-site. The element that defeats ransomware is the offline / air-gapped copy — ransomware can only encrypt what it can reach on the network, so a physically disconnected backup survives. Equally important: the backups must include controller programs, HMI projects, configs and full images, and the restore must be tested against your RTO — an unrestorable backup is not a control.

P6. An engineer proposes a permanent VPN with a shared password so the drive vendor can support the plant anytime. List four things wrong and the correct design.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Wrong: (1) permanent (should be off-by-default, time-boxed); (2) shared password (no unique identity/accountability, fails FR1); (3) direct into OT (no DMZ broker, fails FR5); (4) no MFA / no logging / no session recording (fails FR1/FR6). Correct: brokered jump host in the DMZ, unique per-person accounts + MFA, access requested & approved per session, time-boxed, fully logged and session-recorded, with no standing inbound path.

P7. Explain "virtual patching" and when it is the right choice.

Tap to reveal ↓A: Virtual patching blocks exploitation of a known vulnerability at a network device (industrial firewall / IPS rule that drops the malicious protocol pattern or restricts the reachable source/port) without modifying the vulnerable asset. Right choice when: the asset is end-of-life/unpatchable, a validated patch doesn't exist yet, or a reboot/outage isn't available. It doesn't fix the flaw — it makes it unreachable — so pair it with monitoring and a replacement plan, and record it as a compensating control.

P8. In the attack-path model of WE-4, likelihoods were multiplied. Why, and what design principle follows?

Tap to reveal ↓A: A successful compromise requires every step in the chain to succeed, so (assuming rough independence) the overall probability is the product of the per-step probabilities. Principle: defence in depth — driving any single step toward zero collapses the whole product. Concentrate on the step you can most reduce (the IT/OT boundary), because a 16× cut there (0.8→0.05) dominates the result and protects everything behind it.

P9. Why is passive network monitoring preferred over active scanning in OT, and what makes anomaly detection unusually effective here?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Active scanning sends probes that can crash fragile legacy devices (the scan becomes a DoS) and can consume real-time resources. Passive monitoring reads a mirror/SPAN of traffic and touches nothing. Anomaly detection works unusually well because OT traffic is highly regular — the same devices, protocols and rates repeat predictably — so deviations (a new device, an unexpected program-write, an out-of-window session) stand out sharply against a stable baseline, unlike chaotic IT traffic.

P10. During an active intrusion on a running furnace, the IR plan must specify who authorises isolation. Why is this an OT-specific requirement, and who decides?

Tap to reveal ↓A: Because isolating/powering a live furnace can create a physical hazard (loss of control/visibility of a hot process) potentially worse than the cyber event — so containment is a safety decision, not purely a security one. The IR plan must pre-authorise a joint decision between security, operations and process safety, with operations/safety holding veto on actions that affect the process's safe state. The usual answer is to contain the attacker's access while the process is brought to a controlled safe shutdown, rather than an abrupt kill.

Key Frameworks, Derivations & Exam Traps

The reasoning behind the numbers, a symbol/term glossary, and the seven mistakes that cost marks and cause breaches.

D-1 · Why Defence-in-Depth Multiplies (the attack-path product)

Model a compromise as a chain of n independent barriers, each defeated with probability pᵢ. The attacker succeeds only if all barriers fail:

P(success) = ∏ᵢ pᵢ = p₁·p₂·…·pₙ

Adding an independent barrier with defeat-probability p_new multiplies the result by p_new < 1 — it can never increase risk, and a strong barrier (small p) slashes it. The marginal value of a barrier is largest where the current product is dominated by a single large term: cutting the flat-network step from 0.8 to 0.05 (×0.0625) beats improving an already-small term. Corollary — independence matters: if two "barriers" share a common cause (same credential, same flat VLAN), their failures correlate and the product overstates protection (the cyber twin of common-cause β in A52).

D-2 · Effective Risk vs CVSS — Reachability as a Multiplier

A CVSS base score B is a context-free severity. Your effective risk weights it by whether a threat can reach the asset and how bad the outcome is:

R_eff ≈ B × ρ × C
ρ = reachability ∈ [0,1] (product of the conduits an attacker must cross)
C = consequence factor (OT: availability-weighted, from the PHA)

Because ρ is a function of your zone/conduit design, you control a multiplier on every CVE. Good segmentation drives ρ→0 for deep assets, which is why an unreachable 9.8 can rank below a reachable 5.0. This is also the formal reason "patch by CVSS order" is wrong in OT: it ignores ρ, the term you engineered.

D-3 · The SL Vector & the "Weakest-FR" Rule

A zone's achieved security is the vector SL-A = (a₁…a₇) across the seven FRs; its requirement is SL-T = (t₁…t₇). Conformance is per component:

Zone conforms ⇔ aᵢ ≥ tᵢ for all i (not on average)

An overall "security posture" that averages FRs is misleading: strong FR4 (a₄=4) cannot compensate for weak FR1 (a₁=1) if t₁=3, because an attacker exploits the minimum, not the mean. The governing quantity is minᵢ(aᵢ − tᵢ): if it's negative, the zone fails at that FR regardless of how strong the others are. This is why "we encrypt everything" is not an answer to "who can log in".

⚠ Seven Exam Traps
  • Reversing the triad. Writing C-I-A for OT. It's A-I-C; availability leads because failure is physical.
  • Confusing SL-C/T/A. "SL-3 certified component" ≠ "SL-3 plant". Capability, target and achieved are three different things.
  • Averaging the FR vector. Security is per-FR; the weakest FR relative to its target governs. Don't offset a weak FR1 with a strong FR4.
  • Patching by CVSS. Ignoring reachability. Re-score with ρ and OT consequence; an unreachable 9.8 < a reachable exploitable 5.0.
  • IT-style containment. "Pull the plug" on a live process can be more dangerous than the attack — containment is a joint safety decision, aim for controlled safe shutdown.
  • Air-gap complacency. "It's air-gapped" is usually false (vendor VPNs, USB, IIoT modems) and, even if true, Stuxnet crossed one. Air gap is not a substitute for the FR controls.
  • Treating security as a project. It's a lifecycle (CSMS): threats and the plant both change, so risk assessment, patching and monitoring recur. A one-off audit decays.

  • 4 · Run an OT protocol. Use a soft-PLC (OpenPLC, see A45) + Modbus; watch the traffic in Wireshark; craft a rogue write and see it in the capture.
  • 5 · Anomaly baseline. Record 10 minutes of "normal", then introduce a new device / unexpected write and confirm it stands out — feel why OT anomaly detection works.
  • 6 · Do a mini 62443-3-2. For one zone, write the SUC, consequence, threat, SL-T vector and gap list — one page, defensible.
  • 7 · Tabletop an incident. Walk the "03:00 rogue PLC download" scenario with colleagues; decide containment vs safe-state as a group.
  • 8 · Backup & restore drill. Image a controller/HMI, wipe it, and rebuild from the golden image within a target RTO — time it.
