From the Street to the Socket

One line diagram explains every house: origin, protection, distribution, final circuits.

Definition

A domestic installation is a tree: one origin (the supply), one trunk (the consumer unit), and branches (final circuits) — every branch protected against overload, short circuit and earth fault at its root.

The rulebooks: IEC 60364 internationally, implemented as BS 7671 (UK & Sri Lanka's SLS 1080 lineage), NEC in North America. This module speaks IEC/BS: 230 V line-to-neutral, 50 Hz, brown = line, blue = neutral, green/yellow = protective earth (CPC). The engineering logic transfers to every code even where the numbers differ.

The supply chain
The domestic supply chain. The service-head fuse (60–100 A) is the installation's upstream guarantee: it caps the energy any downstream fault can draw, and it is the reason meter tails may run short distances unprotected.
230 V

Line–neutral

Single-phase domestic standard (400 V line-line where three-phase enters — → A30).

L·N·E

Three conductors

Brown line, blue neutral, green/yellow CPC — the CPC carries current ONLY during a fault.

3 evils

Overload · short · earth fault

Every protective device in the CU exists for one (or two) of these three.

Page 2 / 14 · Earthing

Earthing Systems — TN-S, TN-C-S, TT

The fault current must get home. The earthing system is the road it takes — and its quality decides everything downstream.

Why earthing exists

When a live conductor touches a metal case, the case rises toward 230 V. Earthing gives the fault current a low-impedance path back to the transformer, making it LARGE — large enough to trip protection fast, before a person becomes the alternative path.

Counter-intuitive but central: a good earth makes fault currents bigger on purpose. Protection speed depends on fault size; fault size depends on loop impedance; loop impedance starts with the earthing system.

The three systems
Ze = external loop impedance, measured at the origin. TT's tens-of-ohms rod means an earth fault draws only a few amps — invisible to an MCB, lethal without an RCD. The earthing system dictates the protection strategy.

The TT arithmetic that decides everything: with a 100 Ω rod, an earth fault draws 230/100 = 2.3 A. A 32 A breaker sees nothing wrong — forever. Only a 30 mA RCD (trips at 0.03 A) can protect this installation. Meanwhile TN-C-S's single wire carrying combined neutral+earth (PEN) is superbly low-impedance — until it breaks: a lost PEN puts load current through every bonded pipe and case, the reason PME earths are forbidden for some outdoor/EV/caravan supplies without extra measures.

Consumer Unit Anatomy

Main switch → residual protection → circuit protection → busbar: energy flows left to right, responsibility flows with it.

Inside the enclosure
A dual-RCD split-load consumer unit. The alternative — an RCBO per circuit — costs more and isolates faults to a single circuit: the modern default for new work.

Design logic worth memorising: interleave circuits so upstairs lights and downstairs sockets share an RCD (a tripped socket fault never leaves you on a dark staircase); smoke-alarm and freezer circuits prefer their own RCBO; the SPD (surge protective device, now standard in BS 7671 Amd 2) clamps transients from switching and lightning before they reach electronics; metal enclosures are required (fire containment) with amendment-era boards.

DP

Main switch

Breaks line AND neutral — the one guaranteed dead-making device in the house.

RCBO

MCB + RCD in one

Per-circuit residual protection: a fault trips one way, not half the board.

SPD

Surge protection

MOV-based clamp diverting kV transients to earth — sacrificial, with an end-of-life flag.

MCBs — The Thermal-Magnetic Gatekeeper

Two trip mechanisms in one lever: a slow bimetal for overload, a fast solenoid for short circuit.

How it trips

An MCB carries two detectors in series: a bimetal strip heated by load current (minutes at small overloads — I²t thermal memory) and a magnetic solenoid that fires in milliseconds when current exceeds its instantaneous threshold.

instantaneous (magnetic) trip bands, multiples of rating In: Type B : 3–5 × In homes — low inrush loads Type C : 5–10 × In motors, discharge lighting (→ A28) Type D : 10–20 × In transformers, welders, high inrush breaking capacity: domestic MCBs rated 6 kA (Icn) — must exceed the prospective fault current at the board

Type choice is an inrush problem, not a load problem: a compressor that draws 7×In for 100 ms holds a Type C in but nuisance-trips a Type B. The price of C: it needs twice the fault current of B to trip instantly — which halves the allowed loop impedance (page 8). Choosing C "to be safe" can silently make an installation non-compliant.

The time-current curve
The B-curve: minutes at 1.5×In, seconds at 3×, guaranteed instantaneous beyond 5×. Compliance calculations always use the WORST-case corner — 5×In for Type B — because a real breaker may sit anywhere in its band.

MCB ≠ RCD, permanently: the MCB answers overload and short circuit (amps to kiloamps). It is numerically blind to a 30 mA shock current — three orders of magnitude below its senses. The two devices answer different questions, which is why the RCBO merges them rather than either replacing the other.

1.45×

Overload rule

The device must operate at ≤1.45×Iz of the cable it protects — In ≤ Iz closes the coordination chain Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz.

6 kA

Breaking capacity

The maximum fault the MCB can interrupt without failing — verify against measured PSCC at the origin.

I²t

Energy let-through

What the cable must survive during clearance — the adiabatic check on page 8.

RCDs — Physics of the 30 mA Guardian

A current-balance transformer that notices when electrons leave home and don't come back.

The core-balance principle
Line and neutral thread the same magnetic core in opposite directions. Equal currents cancel; any imbalance — current escaping via a person or damaged insulation — leaves net flux that induces the trip. The RCD measures what is MISSING.
why 30 mA / 40 ms — IEC 60479 physiology: ~0.5–2 mA perception · ~10 mA muscular "let-go" limit ~30–50 mA ventricular fibrillation risk grows with time 30 mA · ≤40 ms @ 5×IΔn sits below the fibrillation charge threshold

Types matter now: Type AC (sinusoidal only) is effectively obsolete; Type A (+ pulsating DC — required for anything with a rectifier: LED drivers, induction hobs, chargers) is the domestic default; Type F for frequency-mixing (inverter drives); Type B for smooth DC leakage — EV chargers and PV inverters, whose DC can quietly blind an upstream Type AC core by saturating it. A modern house with an EV point is a mixed-type design exercise, not a habit.

Additional protection

The T button exercises the mechanism; an electrician's ramp test verifies ≤40 ms at 5×IΔn.

Circuit Design — Ring, Radial, Dedicated

Each circuit is a sized promise: conductor, protective device and load agree before the plaster goes on.

Ring vs radial — the classic pair
The ring shares current between two legs (hence 2.5 mm² on a 32 A device); its r1+rn+r2 cross-connect test exists precisely because a broken ring is invisible in service. Radials trade copper for testability.

Cable Sizing & Voltage Drop

A cable is sized four times: for load, for heat, for volt drop, and for fault survival. The biggest answer wins.

The coordination chain
Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz Ib = design current (load) · In = device rating · Iz = cable capacity INSTALLED Iz = It × Ca × Cg × Ci × Cf derating for: Ca ambient temp · Cg grouping · Ci thermal insulation · Cf device type 2.5 mm² T&E: ~27 A clipped direct → ~13.5 A buried in insulation (Ci 0.5!)

Derating is where paper designs die: the same 2.5 mm² that comfortably carries 27 A on a wall loses HALF its rating inside loft insulation. Method of installation (reference methods A–F) is not bureaucracy — it is the thermal boundary condition of the conductor. When in doubt, size up: copper is cheaper than a rewire.

Voltage drop
VD = (mV/A/m) × Ib × L / 1000 limits: 3% lighting (6.9 V) · 5% power (11.5 V) mV/A/m (Cu, single-phase): 1.0→44 · 1.5→29 · 2.5→18 · 4→11 · 6→7.3 · 10→4.4 · 16→2.8

Page 8 / 14 · Fault Math

Earth-Fault Loop & Disconnection Times

Zs = Ze + R1 + R2 — five characters that decide whether protection is real or decorative.

The loop
The earth-fault loop: out on the line conductor, back on the CPC. Ze is the network's share (measured at the origin); R1+R2 is yours (line + CPC resistance of the circuit). Small Zs ⇒ big fault current ⇒ fast trip.
disconnection required (230 V TN): 0.4 s final circuits ≤63 A · 5 s distribution Type B trips instantly at 5×In ⇒ need If ≥ 5×In Zs(max) = Cmin × 230 / (5 × In) (Cmin = 0.95) B16→2.73 Ω · B20→2.19 · B32→1.37 · B40→1.09 · Type C: halve them
🧪 Interactive — Zs compliance checker
Ze (external)0.35 Ω
R1 + R2 (circuit)0.60 Ω

When Zs cannot comply (long circuit, TT system), the 30 mA RCD becomes the disconnection device: it needs Zs only below 230/0.03 ≈ 7.6 kΩ — trivially met by any rod. That single fact is the whole logic of TT design.

The adiabatic check — cable survival
S ≥ √(I²·t) / k minimum CPC area to survive the fault k = 115 (PVC/Cu conductor) · I = fault current · t = disconnection time example: If 242 A · t 0.4 s → S = √(242²×0.4)/115 = 1.33 mm² ⇒ the 1.5 mm² CPC in 2.5 mm² T&E survives — barely, and by design

The adiabatic equation assumes no heat escapes during the fault (hence the name) — conservative and simple. It is why T&E's reduced CPC is legitimate only when disconnection is fast: slow clearance (high Zs) and a skinny CPC is the combination that melts insulation and starts fires.

Bonding, Bathroom Zones & Safe Isolation

Equipotential thinking, water-and-electricity geometry, and the ritual that keeps electricians alive.

Bonding ≠ earthing

Earthing clears faults; bonding equalises potential. Main protective bonding (10 mm²) ties incoming metallic services — water, gas — to the main earthing terminal so that during any fault, everything a person can touch rises and falls together.

A person is only shocked by a difference in potential. Bonding does not make pipes "safe" — it makes them equal. Supplementary bonding in bathrooms became largely unnecessary once 30 mA RCDs covered all circuits — a nice example of one protective philosophy (fast disconnection) retiring another (local equipotential zones), with the regulations tracking the physics.

The zones encode one idea: wet skin drops body resistance from ~1000+ Ω toward 200 Ω, turning a survivable contact into a fatal one. Distance and IP ratings substitute for the resistance the water removed.

Safe isolation — the non-negotiable ritual
  • 1
    Identify the circuit and its isolation point; agree it with anyone else affected.
  • 2
    Isolate and lock off — padlock + retained key + warning tag. Switching off is not isolation.
  • 3
    Prove the tester on a known live source (proving unit).
  • 4
    Test dead — L-N, L-E, N-E at the point of work with a two-pole voltage tester (never a neon screwdriver, never a multimeter on the wrong range).
  • 5
    Re-prove the tester on the live source — the tester might have died between steps 3 and 4. Prove-test-prove, every time, forever.

Verification sequence after work (dead → live): continuity of CPCs and ring legs (r1, rn, r2), insulation resistance ≥ 1 MΩ at 500 V DC, polarity, then Ze/Zs, PSCC and RCD trip times — recorded on the certificate. Testing is not admin: every line traps a specific installation error before it meets a human.

Recap, Failure Modes & Quiz

Eight ideas, six real-world failure patterns, six checks.

Eight ideas that carry the module
  • 1
    The earthing system dictates the protection strategy — TT means RCDs or nothing.
  • 2
    Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz — load, device, installed cable, in that order, with derating honest.
  • 3
    MCB type = inrush class; choosing C over B halves your allowed Zs.
  • 4
    The RCD measures what is missing — 30 mA, ≤40 ms, and the Type letter must match the load's electronics.
  • 5
    Rings share, radials tell the truth — a broken ring leg is invisible in service.
  • 6
    VD = mV/A/m × Ib × L/1000 against 3%/5% — long runs are sized by millivolts.
  • 7
    Zs = Ze + R1+R2; Zs ≤ 0.95×230/(5In) for a Type B — the compliance heartbeat.
  • 8
    Prove-test-prove — the isolation ritual outranks every clever calculation.
Six classic failure patterns
⚠️ Broken ring leg

Everything works; one leg carries all current and cooks. Caught only by the r1+rn+r2 cross-connect test — the reason periodic inspection exists.

⚠️ Borrowed neutral

Two circuits sharing a neutral (old lighting practice): RCBOs trip mysteriously, and isolating one circuit leaves a live-fed neutral in the "dead" one.

⚠️ Loose neutral terminal

Arcing at a screw terminal → heat → carbonised insulation → fire risk, with no overcurrent for any breaker to see. Thermal imaging (A41) finds it; torque screwdrivers prevent it.

⚠️ Wrong RCD type

EV charger or PV inverter DC leakage saturates a Type AC core — the RCD sits blind while "protected". Type A/B selection is a safety calculation, not a catalogue preference.

⚠️ DIY spur chains

Spur off a spur off a ring: one 2.5 mm² leg feeding a heater row. The ring's maths assumed one spur per point — chains defeat it.

⚠️ Lost PME neutral

Network PEN break: every bonded pipe carries diverted load current; touch voltage appears between "earthed" metal and true earth. The reason PME has special rules outdoors.

🧠 Quick Check 1

A TT installation has a 120 Ω earth rod. Why can't a B32 MCB provide earth-fault protection, and what does?

Tap to reveal Fault current = 230/120 ≈ 1.9 A — a B32 needs 160 A (5×32) to trip instantly and would never operate at 1.9 A. A 30 mA RCD trips at 0.03 A, needing only Zs < ~7.6 kΩ. On TT systems the RCD IS the earth-fault protection.
🧠 Quick Check 2

Why does a ring final use 2.5 mm² cable on a 32 A breaker when 2.5 mm² is only rated ~27 A?

Tap to reveal The ring gives every load two parallel paths; current splits between legs, so neither normally exceeds its rating (worst case ~⅔ of load near one end). The design is legitimate ONLY while the ring is intact — a broken leg silently converts it into two overloaded 27 A radials on a 32 A device, which is why the ring continuity test is mandatory.
🧠 Quick Check 3

Ze = 0.35 Ω, R1+R2 = 0.85 Ω, device B32. Compliant?

Tap to reveal Zs = 1.20 Ω ≤ 1.37 Ω (B32 max) → compliant, with margin 0.17 Ω. Fault current = 230/1.20 ≈ 192 A ≥ 160 A needed for instantaneous trip. On a C32 (max 0.68 Ω) the same circuit would FAIL.
🧠 Quick Check 4

Which loads make a Type AC RCD unsafe, and why?

Tap to reveal Loads leaking DC or pulsating DC — EV chargers, PV inverters, VFDs, some appliances with rectifier front-ends. DC pre-magnetises (saturates) the toroid, so a subsequent AC fault produces too little flux change to trip: the RCD is present but blind. Type A handles pulsating DC; Type B handles smooth DC.
🧠 Quick Check 5

A 2.5 mm² radial (18 mV/A/m) runs 38 m to a workshop drawing 18 A. Volt-drop verdict?

Tap to reveal VD = 18 × 18 × 38 / 1000 = 12.3 V = 5.4% — exceeds the 5% (11.5 V) limit. Upsize to 4 mm² (11 mV/A/m): VD = 7.5 V = 3.3% ✓. Long runs are sized by volt-drop, not ampacity.
🧠 Quick Check 6

In safe isolation, why prove the tester twice?

Tap to reveal Because a dead tester reads 0 V on a LIVE circuit. Proving before confirms the tester works; testing dead gives the reading; re-proving after confirms the tester survived the measurement — otherwise "0 V" might mean a blown tester fuse, not a dead circuit. Prove-test-prove converts a measurement into evidence.

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals, solved step by step.

Worked Example 1 — Sizing a 9.5 kW shower circuit

Given: 9.5 kW shower, 230 V, 18 m route clipped direct (no derating), T&E cable. Select device and cable; verify volt drop.

Ib = 9500/230 = 41.3 A (no diversity — showers run flat out) device: In ≥ Ib → B45 (or B40 if measured load ≤40 A) cable: Iz ≥ In → 10 mm² T&E (Iz ≈ 64 A clipped) ✓ 6 mm² (47 A) also ≥45 but margin thin — 10 mm² is the norm VD = 4.4 × 41.3 × 18/1000 = 3.3 V = 1.4% ≤ 5% ✓

Discussion: the chain ran Ib → In → Iz in order, then VD. Note what was NOT done: no diversity (continuous load), no undersizing "because it's only a shower". A 30 mA RCD (Type A) covers the circuit; the isolator pull-cord in the bathroom gives local isolation. If the route had passed through insulation, Iz would collapse and 10 mm² becomes mandatory rather than comfortable.

Worked Example 2 — Ring final continuity (r1 + rn + r2)

Given: ring measurements at the CU: r1 (L–L ends) = 0.52 Ω, rn (N–N) = 0.52 Ω, r2 (CPC–CPC) = 0.86 Ω. Predict the expected (R1+R2) at every socket and explain the test.

cross-connect method: join L of one leg to CPC of the other (both ways), measure at each socket: reading should be ≈ constant = (r1 + r2)/4 = (0.52 + 0.86)/4 = 0.35 Ω at every socket a socket reading ≈ (r1+r2)/4 everywhere → ring intact readings RISING toward one end → that leg is broken (it's a radial now)

Discussion: the ÷4 comes from two half-rings in parallel: each parallel pair is (r/2 ∥ r/2)... precisely, at any socket the two paths sum to r1+r2 and their parallel combination is maximised mid-ring at (r1+r2)/4. The near-constant reading is the fingerprint of an intact ring — the only routine test that catches the "everything still works" broken-leg failure. r2/r1 = 0.86/0.52 = 1.65 ≈ the 2.5/1.5 mm² CSA ratio ✓ — a built-in sanity check.

Worked Example 3 — Full Zs compliance check

Given: TN-C-S supply, Ze = 0.30 Ω. A 28 m radial in 2.5/1.5 mm² T&E (r1+r2 = 19.51 mΩ/m at 20 °C, ×1.2 for operating temperature) on a B20. Verify disconnection.

R1+R2 = 19.51 × 28 × 1.2 / 1000 = 0.656 Ω Zs = 0.30 + 0.656 = 0.96 Ω Zs(max) B20 = 0.95×230/(5×20) = 2.19 Ω → 0.96 ≤ 2.19 ✓ PASS If = 230/0.96 ≈ 240 A (≥ 100 A needed) → trips <10 ms

Discussion: the ×1.2 multiplier converts table resistance (20 °C) to conductor operating temperature (70 °C PVC) — omitting it is the classic way paper passes and reality fails. Margin here is generous; the same circuit at 70 m would give Zs = 1.94 Ω, still passing a B20 but failing a C20 (1.09 Ω) — device curve choice consumes loop budget.

Worked Example 4 — Adiabatic verification of a CPC

Given: the circuit above (Zs = 0.96 Ω, 1.5 mm² CPC, k = 115). Confirm the CPC survives the earth fault.

If = 230/0.96 = 240 A B20 magnetic trip at 240 A → t ≈ 0.01 s (instantaneous) S_min = √(I²t)/k = √(240² × 0.01)/115 = 24.0/115 = 0.21 mm² 1.5 mm² ≥ 0.21 mm² → ✓ SURVIVES with huge margin

Discussion: fast disconnection is what makes small CPCs viable — the energy let-through I²t at 10 ms is tiny. Rerun the numbers with a sluggish 0.4 s clearance and S_min rises √40 ≈ 6.3× to 1.34 mm²: the margin nearly vanishes. The adiabatic check, loop impedance and device curve are one coupled system — change any one and re-verify the others.

Problem Set — 10 Questions

Work each one before revealing — the coordination chain and the loop equation answer everything.

Problem 1

A 7.2 kW cooker at 230 V: design current before diversity, and after (10 A + 30% remainder)?

Tap to reveal Full: 7200/230 = 31.3 A. Diversity: 10 + 0.3×(31.3−10) = 10 + 6.4 = 16.4 A (add 5 A if the unit has a socket → 21.4 A). A B32 on 6 mm² is the standard answer.
Problem 2

Why is diversity forbidden for the shower but expected for the cooker?

Tap to reveal Diversity models statistically intermittent loads. A cooker cycles thermostatically with rings on/off — average ≪ peak. A shower element runs at 100% duty for the whole use; its average IS its peak. Diversity applied to continuous loads is just under-sizing with paperwork.
Problem 3

2.5 mm² T&E rated 27 A clipped direct runs 3 m through loft insulation (Ci = 0.5). New Iz, and consequence for a ring?

Tap to reveal Iz = 27 × 0.5 = 13.5 A. Even with the ring's current-sharing this is below the ~20 A a leg can see — the insulated section becomes the thermal weak link. Fix: route around insulation, derate the circuit, or upsize the cable through that section.
Problem 4

Max Zs for a C40 device (Cmin = 0.95)?

Tap to reveal Type C trips at 10×In: Zs = 0.95×230/(10×40) = 0.55 Ω. Compare B40's 1.09 Ω — the C-curve halves the loop budget, often forcing bigger cable purely for fault-loop reasons.
Problem 5

An RCD trips at 22 mA on a ramp test and holds 250 ms at 1×IΔn on older equipment. Pass?

Tap to reveal Ramp 22 mA is within the 50–100% of IΔn window (15–30 mA) ✓. At 1×IΔn the limit is ≤300 ms → 250 ms ✓ PASS; the ≤40 ms requirement applies at 5×IΔn (150 mA). Knowing which limit belongs to which multiple is a standard exam trap.
Problem 6

Insulation resistance test: what voltage and minimum value for a 230 V circuit, and what must you do first?

Tap to reveal 500 V DC, ≥ 1 MΩ (L+N together to E for a quick global check). First: disconnect or protect voltage-sensitive equipment (SPDs, dimmers, boiler electronics, smart devices) — the test voltage that proves the cable kills the electronics.
Problem 7

A garden office needs 32 A at 45 m. Check 4 mm² (11 mV/A/m) and 6 mm² (7.3) against 5%.

Tap to reveal 4 mm²: 11×32×45/1000 = 15.8 V = 6.9% ✗. 6 mm²: 7.3×32×45/1000 = 10.5 V = 4.6% ✓. Volt-drop, not ampacity, sizes this SWA run — and a sub-board at the office restarts the VD budget for its final circuits.
Problem 8

Why must main bonding conductors connect gas/water pipes NEAR their point of entry?

Tap to reveal The pipe is an extraneous-conductive-part — it can import true-earth potential from outside. Bonding at entry clamps the whole internal pipework to the installation's earth potential before it runs anywhere touchable; bonded mid-run, the entry section can still sit at a different potential during a fault (especially under PME lost-neutral conditions).
Problem 9

During isolation you measure L-N: 0 V, L-E: 0 V, N-E: 0 V — but the proving unit now shows your tester dead. Status of the circuit?

Tap to reveal Unknown — treat as LIVE. A tester that fails the re-prove invalidates every reading it just gave (a blown internal fuse reads 0 V on anything). Replace/repair the tester, prove it, and repeat all three dead tests. This exact scenario is why prove-test-prove exists.
Problem 10

An EV charger install on a PME (TN-C-S) supply: name the two special hazards and the standard mitigations.

Tap to reveal (1) Lost PEN: the car body (outdoors, on true earth) could rise to dangerous touch voltage → use a charger with open-PEN detection/disconnection, or convert the circuit to TT with its own rod. (2) DC leakage blinding the RCD → Type A + 6 mA DC detection (built into most chargers) or Type B RCD. Both are checkbox items on a modern EV certificate.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Where Zs(max), the adiabatic equation and the ring's ÷4 come from.

Derivation 1 — maximum Zs from the disconnection requirement
requirement: disconnect in 0.4 s ⇒ use the device's INSTANTANEOUS region Type B guaranteed instantaneous at 5×In ⇒ need If ≥ 5·In If = Cmin·U₀/Zs ⇒ Cmin·U₀/Zs ≥ 5·In Zs ≤ Cmin·U₀/(5·In) Cmin = 0.95 covers supply-voltage sag same logic: Type C → 10·In · Type D → 20·In · RCD → IΔn (tiny ⇒ Zs huge)

Why it matters: every max-Zs table row is this one line evaluated. Derive it once and you can reconstruct the whole table in an exam, handle non-standard voltages, and explain why an RCD rescues any loop an MCB cannot clear.

Derivation 2 — the adiabatic equation
assume all fault heat stays in the conductor (no time to escape): energy in = I²·R·t with R ∝ 1/S ; allowed temperature rise fixes allowable energy per mm² → material constant k I²·t = k²·S² ⇒ S = √(I²t)/k k: 115 (PVC/Cu) · 143 (XLPE/Cu) · 76 (PVC/Al)

Why it matters: it exposes the coupling — I comes from Zs, t from the device curve at that I. Faster disconnection (lower Zs, B-curve) slashes I²t and legitimises T&E's reduced CPC; anything that slows clearance re-opens the check. The same equation sizes earthing conductors and SWA armour used as CPC.

Derivation 3 — the ring's (r1+r2)/4
socket at fraction x around the ring; two paths to the CU: path A = x·(r1+r2) · path B = (1−x)·(r1+r2) (after cross-connection) parallel: R(x) = (r1+r2)·x(1−x) maximum at x = ½ : R = (r1+r2)/4 — and nearly flat around it broken leg ⇒ single path ⇒ R rises linearly to r1+r2 at the far end

Why it matters: the flat x(1−x) profile is why an intact ring reads "the same everywhere" and a broken one reads a ramp — the test interprets a parabola. It also yields the worst-case (R1+R2) for Zs verification of a ring: measured maximum ≈ (r1+r2)/4.

Seven exam & field traps
  • 1
    Table resistance is 20 °C. Multiply r1+r2 by ~1.2 for operating temperature before any Zs verdict — cold measurements flatter the loop.
  • 2
    RCD ≠ overload protection. A 30 mA RCD happily passes 80 A forever; an MCB is numerically blind to 30 mA. Each device answers only its own question.
  • 3
    Type C "for reliability" halves max Zs. Curve upgrades are loop-impedance decisions, not preferences.
  • 4
    Neutral is a live conductor. It carries load current at earth-ish potential — a borrowed or broken neutral bites precisely because people treat it as safe.
  • 5
    The 40 ms limit belongs to 5×IΔn. At 1×IΔn the allowance is 300 ms — quoting 40 ms against a 1× test fails good RCDs on paper.
  • 6
    VD limits differ: 3% lighting, 5% power — and both are from the ORIGIN, so a sub-main's drop eats the final circuit's budget.
  • 7
    IR testing fries electronics. 500 V DC through an SPD, dimmer or boiler PCB is a warranty claim — disconnect first, and record that you did.

Hands-on ladder — eight exercises
  • 1
    Map your own board. Open (visually!) your consumer unit: identify main switch, RCD banks/RCBOs, each circuit's device type and rating; draw the schedule.
  • 2
    Identify your earthing system from the service head arrangement (sheath connection? PEN split? rod?) and note the implications for RCD dependence.
  • 3
    Zs table from scratch. Derive max Zs for B6–B40 and C6–C40 with the one-line formula; check against the published table.
  • 4
    Design a circuit on paper. Garage supply: pick load, do Ib→In→Iz with honest derating, VD at length, Zs prediction, adiabatic check — full chain, one page.
  • 5
    Ring math rehearsal. With three resistors as legs, compute r1+rn+r2 predictions, then the ÷4 figure-eight readings — before ever touching a real ring.
  • 6
    Watch (or perform under supervision) the test sequence on one circuit: continuity, IR, polarity, Zs, RCD times — and fill in a model certificate.
  • 7
    Practise prove-test-prove with a two-pole tester and proving unit until the ritual is automatic — muscle memory is the deliverable.
  • 8
    Audit one failure mode. Find (in photos or the field) one example of the page-10 gallery — spur chains, loose neutrals, wrong RCD type — and write the fix.
