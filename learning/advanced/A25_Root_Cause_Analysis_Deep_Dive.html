What a Root Cause Actually Is

Precise Definition

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a structured, evidence-based method for tracing a problem back to the deepest fixable cause — the point in the causal chain where, if you intervene, the problem stops recurring and does not migrate elsewhere.

The micro card called RCA "a structured method to find the true source of a problem." Correct — but the word that does the heavy lifting is fixable. A true root cause has three tests, drawn from the classic RCA literature (Latino & Latino, ASQ): (1) when you remove it, the problem goes away; (2) when you remove it, the problem does not reappear by another path; and (3) it is something the organisation can actually control. "Gravity," "the laws of physics" or "the operator was careless" all fail at least one test — they are either uncontrollable or are themselves symptoms of a system.

1

Removal eliminates

Take it away and the problem stops — proof you reached deep enough.

2

No recurrence

It does not return by a different route — you didn't just patch a symptom.

3

Controllable

The organisation can act on it — otherwise keep digging to a cause you can.

Symptom vs. cause — the central confusion

Most failed problem-solving stops at a symptom (the motor stopped), a causal factor (the overload tripped), or a convenient scapegoat (the new operator). RCA's whole job is to push past these to the physical, human and latent system layers beneath. A mature analysis usually finds three layers of root cause: the physical (bearing seized), the human (lubrication step skipped), and the latent / systemic (no lubrication task on the PM schedule and no audit to catch it). Fix only the physical and you replace bearings forever; fix the latent cause and the failure is gone.

Page 2 · The causal chain

The Causal Chain — From Symptom to Latent System

The three layers of cause

Every failure sits on a chain. The micro card's 5-Whys ladder is really a walk down this chain — each "why" moves you one link deeper. The skill is knowing which link is the root, and not stopping one link too early (a recurring patch) or one too late (boiling the ocean).

The causal chain. RCA's craft is choosing the deepest controllable link — not the first plausible one.

This is why RCA practitioners say "stop at human error and you have stopped too early." Human error is almost never a root cause — it is a signal that the surrounding system (training, procedure, tooling, workload, design) permitted or invited the error. Page 9 develops this into James Reason's Swiss-cheese model.

The Seven Classic RCA Tools — and When to Reach for Each

No single tool fits every problem

The micro card showed two tools (5 Whys and Fishbone). In practice RCA is a toolbox, and choosing the right tool for the failure type is half the skill. A simple, single-thread breakdown wants 5 Whys; a complex defect with many possible contributors wants a Fishbone or Fault Tree; a fleet of recurring issues wants Pareto to pick the vital few first. 5️⃣ The 5 Whys

Iteratively ask "why?" along a single causal thread until you reach a controllable system cause. Fast, needs no software, great for shop-floor problems with one dominant chain.

Best for: simple, single-cause breakdowns  ·  Weak for: defects with several interacting causes (it tunnels down one branch and misses the others).

Inductive vs. deductive — the deep distinction

RCA tools split into two logics. Inductive tools start from the observed failure and reason upward to possible causes (5 Whys, Fishbone, FMEA, cause maps). Deductive tools start from a top undesired event and reason downward through logic gates to the basic events that could produce it (Fault Tree Analysis). Inductive tools are quick and exploratory; deductive tools are rigorous and quantifiable. Serious reliability work uses both — brainstorm inductively, then test the leading hypothesis deductively.

Page 4 · 5 Whys, rigorously

5 Whys Done Right — The Interactive Ladder, Plus Its Failure Modes

Walk the ladder

The micro card let you reveal a 5-Whys ladder one step at a time. Here is the same engine — but the lesson is in the rules that separate a real 5-Whys from a guessing game. Reveal each "why," then read the discipline below.

❌ PROBLEM: Robot weld cell down — parts not clamping
Why didn't the parts clamp?
The pneumatic clamp lost holding force.
Why did it lose force?
Supply pressure dropped below 5 bar at the manifold.
Why did pressure drop?
The FRL filter element was clogged — high pressure drop.
Why was the filter clogged?
It had not been changed in 14 months.
Why hadn't it been changed?
No filter-replacement task existed on the PM schedule, and condition wasn't monitored.
🎯 ROOT CAUSE: Missing PM task + no condition monitoring on the FRL
Fix: add ΔP-triggered filter change to PM (links A12 Pneumatics & A23 TPM)
The five rules that make it real (not theatre)
  • 1
    Evidence at every rung. Each "why" must be confirmed with data — a gauge reading, a photo, a log — not assumed. An unverified chain is just a story.
  • 2
    "Five" is a guide, not a law. Sometimes the root appears at 3 whys, sometimes at 7. Stop when you hit a controllable system cause, not when you hit number five.
  • 3
    Beware the single-thread trap. 5 Whys follows one branch. If a problem has parallel causes (A and B both needed), switch to a Fishbone or Fault Tree.
  • 4
    Don't let it become "5 Blames." If an answer names a person, the next why must ask why the system allowed it.
  • 5
    Test the chain backwards. Read root → symptom with "therefore." If each step doesn't logically force the next, a link is missing or wrong.
⚠️ Confirmation tunnelling

The team picks a favourite cause at why-1 and bends every later answer to fit it. Cure: have a sceptic challenge each rung.

⚠️ Premature stop

Stopping at "operator error" or "worn part" — a symptom of the real system gap. Always ask one more why past the human/physical layer.

Page 5 · Fishbone

The Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram — Brainstorm Before You Bet

The 6 M framework

Where 5 Whys tunnels down one thread, the Ishikawa / fishbone diagram fans out across every category of cause before you commit to investigating any one. Invented by Kaoru Ishikawa at Kawasaki in the 1960s, it forces a team to brainstorm causes under the 6 Ms — Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement and Mother-nature (Environment) — so no category is forgotten. The "head" is the effect; each bone is a category; each twig is a candidate cause to test.

A 6-M fishbone for weld porosity. Every category gets candidates before the team picks which to test with data.
From brainstorm to proof — the discipline that prevents waste

A fishbone generates hypotheses; it does not prove anything. Its danger is that a team admires a full diagram and then "fixes" five things at once, learning nothing about which mattered. The professional workflow: brainstorm the bones → rank each candidate by likelihood and ease of test → verify the top suspects with data (a measurement, a controlled trial, a designed experiment) → keep only the causes the evidence supports. In Six Sigma this hands off directly to the Analyze phase — the fishbone supplies the X's, and data confirms which X's truly drive the Y. Variants you'll meet: the Process fishbone (bones = process steps) and the 4 P framework for services (Policy, Procedure, People, Plant).

Page 6 · Fault Tree + math

Fault Tree Analysis — Boolean Logic Made Quantitative

The deductive tool

When a failure is high-consequence (a press crushes, a reactor over-pressures, a robot enters a guarded zone), brainstorming is not enough — you need to quantify how it can happen. Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) starts from a single top event (the undesired outcome) and works downward through Boolean logic gates to the basic events (component failures, human errors) that can cause it. Developed at Bell Labs in 1962 for the Minuteman missile, it is the backbone of functional-safety standards (IEC 61508, ISO 13849).

A fault tree for "guard fails to stop motion." AND = both branches needed; OR = either basic event suffices.
The Boolean probability math

FTA turns a diagram into a number. Each basic event has a failure probability Q. Gates combine them with two rules (events assumed independent):

OR gate (either causes it): QOR = 1 − (1−Qa)(1−Qb) ≈ Qa + Qb (for small Q) AND gate (both needed): QAND = Qa × Qb Worked example (tree above): OR branch = 1e-3 + 2e-3 = 3.0e-3 AND of (OR branch) and (relay) = 3.0e-3 × 5e-4 TOP EVENT Q = 1.5e-6 per demand

The lesson the math teaches RCA: an AND gate is a redundancy — two independent things must fail together, so the combined probability is tiny (multiply small numbers). An OR gate is a vulnerability — any single basic event triggers the top, so probabilities add. This is exactly why safety design adds redundant channels (forcing an AND) and why a single un-redundant component is a "single point of failure." A minimal cut set is the smallest combination of basic events that alone causes the top event — finding the smallest cut sets tells you precisely where the system is weakest. It connects forward to A28-style reliability and to functional-safety SIL/PL ratings.

Page 7 · Pareto + FMEA

Pareto for "What to Fix First," FMEA for "What Could Fail"

Pareto — the 80/20 of root causes

RCA does not happen in a vacuum of one problem; a plant has dozens. Pareto analysis (Vilfredo Pareto, popularised by Juran) ranks failure modes by frequency or cost so you spend your limited RCA effort on the vital few, not the trivial many. The rule of thumb: ~80% of the losses come from ~20% of the causes. You attack the tallest bar first because it returns the most.

Bars = frequency/cost per cause; gold line = cumulative %. The first 2–3 causes ("vital few", cyan) cross 80% — fix those first.
Cumulative % = (Σ losses up to cause i) ÷ (Σ all losses) × 100 Vital few = smallest set of causes whose cumulative % ≥ 80% Priority = attack the tallest bar first → highest return per RCA hour
FMEA — RCA run in reverse, before failure happens

RCA is reactive — it acts after a failure. Failure Mode & Effects Analysis (FMEA) is its proactive twin: you brainstorm how each part or step could fail, then prioritise prevention before anything breaks. Each potential failure mode is scored on three 1–10 scales and multiplied into a Risk Priority Number:

RPN = S × O × D S = Severity (how bad if it happens; 10 = catastrophic) O = Occurrence (how likely; 10 = almost certain) D = Detection (how hard to catch BEFORE it reaches the customer; 10 = undetectable) Example: S=8, O=4, D=6 → RPN = 192 (work the highest RPNs first) Note: high D = BAD (you can't detect it). Improving detection lowers D and RPN.

Severity (S)

Consequence of the failure. You usually can't lower S without a design change — it's fixed by physics.

Occurrence (O)

Frequency. RCA + robust design lowers O — this is where prevention lives.

Detection (D)

Can you catch it first? Poka-yoke, sensors and inspection lower D (and RPN).

How they pair in practice: Pareto tells you which recurring failure to RCA; RCA finds why it happens; FMEA captures the lesson so the same failure mode is designed out of the next product or line — and modern AIAG-VDA FMEA replaces the single RPN with Action-Priority (High/Medium/Low) tables to stop teams gaming the number.

Page 8 · Corrective action

From Cause to Cure — The 8D and A3 Frameworks

RCA is the middle, not the whole

Finding the root cause is necessary but not sufficient — the problem isn't solved until a verified, permanent corrective action is in place and the system is updated so it can't drift back. Two industry-standard frameworks wrap RCA in this discipline: the automotive 8D (Eight Disciplines, from Ford) and the Toyota A3 (one-page problem-solving on A3 paper). RCA tools live inside step D4 of 8D and the "Analysis" box of A3.

D3 ContainInterim action to protect the customer NOWStops the bleeding while you investigate

D6 ImplementRoll out the permanent fix; remove the interimMake the cure the new normal

D8 CloseRecognise the team; confirm sustained resultVerify the gain held over time
Corrective vs. preventive vs. corrective-of-the-system

A subtle but exam-critical distinction. A correction fixes the immediate instance (replace the seized bearing). Corrective action removes the cause of this problem so it won't recur (add the lubrication task). Preventive / systemic action asks "where else could this same gap exist?" and closes it everywhere (audit every PM schedule for missing lube tasks). Weak RCA delivers only corrections; strong RCA delivers corrective and systemic action — which is exactly the read-across in D7 and the link to TPM's Focused-Improvement pillar.

Page 9 · Systems & pitfalls

Human Error Is a Symptom — The Swiss-Cheese Model & Classic Traps

Reason's Swiss-cheese model

James Reason's model (1990) is the antidote to "operator error." Every system has layers of defence — training, procedures, supervision, interlocks, design. Each layer has holes (latent weaknesses). An accident happens only when the holes in every layer momentarily line up so a hazard passes straight through. The active error at the sharp end (the operator) is the last hole, not the cause — the cause is the latent holes that let it through.

The Swiss-cheese model. RCA's job is to find and close the latent holes, not punish the sharp-end error.
The pitfalls that make teams solve the wrong problem
⚠️ Stopping at blame

"Operator error" ends the analysis. It's the last hole, never the root. Ask: what in the system invited the error?

⚠️ Opinion, not evidence

Plausible-sounding chains that were never verified with data. Every link needs proof or it's fiction.

⚠️ Single-cause bias

Forcing one root onto a problem that has several interacting causes. Use a fishbone/fault tree to check for parallels.

⚠️ Solving the symptom

A fast patch that makes the alarm stop but leaves the cause — it returns, often worse, by the same path.

⚠️ Confirmation tunnelling

Picking the answer first and bending evidence to fit. Assign a devil's advocate to challenge each step.

⚠️ No verification of the fix

Closing the action without proving the problem actually stopped. D8 / Control exists for exactly this.

⭐ The reframe that separates experts from novices

A novice asks "who did this?"; an expert asks "what about our system made this outcome likely?" Blameless RCA isn't soft — it is the only version that finds causes you can actually fix. People are usually the inheritors of a bad system, not its authors.

The RCA loop — five moves
  • 1
    Describe & contain: quantify the problem (is/is-not), protect the customer with an interim action.
  • 2
    Pick the tool: single thread → 5 Whys; many causes → Fishbone; high-consequence → Fault Tree; many problems → Pareto first.
  • 3
    Dig with evidence: verify every "why" with data; push past the human/physical layer to the latent system.
  • 4
    Verify the cause: removing it must eliminate the problem and stop recurrence — and you must be able to control it.
  • 5
    Act & prevent: permanent corrective action + read-across + update FMEA/SOP so it can't return anywhere.
Quick Check 1

Q: A 5-Whys ends at "the operator forgot the lubrication step." Why is that not yet a root cause, and what's the next why?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: It stops at the human layer — a symptom of the system, not a controllable system cause. The next why: "Why was it possible to forget?" → no step on the PM checklist, no poka-yoke, no condition monitoring. The root is the missing system control; the operator is the last Swiss-cheese hole, not the cause.
Quick Check 2

Q: In a fault tree, two basic events sit under an AND gate (Q = 1×10⁻³ each). Under an OR gate the same two would be… — compute both top probabilities and say which design is safer.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: AND: Q = 1e-3 × 1e-3 = 1×10⁻⁶. OR: Q ≈ 1e-3 + 1e-3 = 2×10⁻³. The AND is ~2000× safer because both independent things must fail together (redundancy). An OR gate is a single-point-of-failure vulnerability. This is why safety functions use redundant channels — they convert an OR into an AND.
Quick Check 3

Q: An FMEA line reads S=9, O=3, D=8. Compute the RPN, name the cheapest lever to reduce risk, and explain why.

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: RPN = 9×3×8 = 216. Severity (9) is usually locked by physics/design and hard to move. Occurrence (3) is already low. The big, cheap lever is Detection (8 = nearly undetectable): add a poka-yoke or sensor so the failure is caught before it escapes. Drop D from 8→2 and RPN falls 216→54 with no redesign.
Quick Check 4

Q: Why does fixing only the "correction" (replace the part) keep an engineer busy forever, and how do D7 / systemic action break the cycle?

Tap to reveal answer ↓ A: A correction restores the instance but leaves the cause intact, so the failure recurs by the same path — you replace bearings forever. Corrective action removes this cause; D7 systemic action (read-across) then asks "where else does this same gap exist?" and closes it on every sister line, plus updates the FMEA/SOP so the failure mode can't be re-introduced. That converts a recurring firefight into a one-time, permanent fix.
