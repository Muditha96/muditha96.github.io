The Measurement & Control Chain

Control quality can never exceed measurement quality — the loop starts at the tapping point, not at the PID block.

Definition

Instrumentation is the engineering of measuring process variables — pressure, temperature, flow, level, and analytical values — and delivering them as trustworthy signals to indication, control and safety systems. Every control loop is a chain: primary element → transmitter → signal → controller → final element, and the chain is only as honest as its weakest link.

The classic split: the primary element touches the process (orifice plate, RTD, diaphragm); the transmitter converts its tiny raw signal into a standard transmission signal (4–20 mA, digital); the controller (PLC/DCS — A07, PID law — A10) computes; the final element (control valve, VFD — A08) acts. Operators watch it all through SCADA (A14).

Error (%span) = (measured − true) / (URV − LRV) × 100 A "±0.25 % span" transmitter ranged 0–100 °C may be wrong by ±0.25 °C anywhere — re-ranged 0–400 °C the same ±0.25 % becomes ±1.0 °C. Range tight, measure right.
Dynamic characteristics — the instrument as a filter

Most sensors respond as a first-order lag: a step change in the process produces an exponential approach in the reading. The time constant τ is the time to reach 63.2% of the change; practical completeness (98%) takes ≈ 4τ. A bare thermocouple may have τ ≈ 1 s; the same element inside a heavy thermowell can be 30–90 s — and that lag sits inside your control loop, eating phase margin exactly like the filter lags in A10.

First-order response: PV(t) = PV₀ + ΔPV·(1 − e^(−t/τ)) t = τ → 63.2 % t = 2τ → 86.5 % t = 3τ → 95.0 % t = 4τ → 98.2 % Rule: measurement τ should be ≲ 1/10 of the process time constant it watches — otherwise you are tuning the sensor, not the process.
🧠 Quick check

Two datasheets: transmitter A "±0.075% of span", transmitter B "±0.15% of reading". Ranged 0–100 °C and operating at 20 °C, which reads truer?

Tap to reveal ↓ A: ±0.075% × 100 °C span = ±0.075 °C — everywhere. B: ±0.15% × 20 °C reading = ±0.03 °C at this point. B wins at low readings, loses above 50 °C (±0.075 vs A's constant ±0.075 at 50; B grows to ±0.15 at 100). %-of-reading specs flatter the bottom of the range; %-of-span specs are honest but constant. Always convert both to engineering units at YOUR operating point.

4–20 mA, Live Zero & the Smart Transmitter

Seventy years old and still running the world's plants — for very good electrical reasons.

Why current, why 4, why 20

Why current, not voltage? In a series loop the same current flows everywhere — cable resistance and moderate contact resistance cannot change the reading (they only consume voltage headroom). A voltage signal would drop along every metre of copper. Why 4 mA and not 0? The live zero: a healthy loop never sits at 0 mA, so 0 mA unambiguously means broken wire / dead transmitter — measurement and diagnostics in one signal. And the 4 mA floor powers the transmitter itself (2-wire loop-powered device: signal and supply share one pair). NAMUR NE 43 refines it: ≤ 3.6 mA = fault-low, ≥ 21 mA = fault-high — the transmitter can deliberately signal its own failure.

Scaling: I = 4 + 16 · (PV − LRV)/(URV − LRV) [mA] Inverse: PV = LRV + (I − 4)/16 · (URV − LRV) Checkpoints: 4 = 0 % 8 = 25 % 12 = 50 % 16 = 75 % 20 = 100 % DCS input: 250 Ω sense resistor → 1–5 V · NAMUR NE 43: <3.6 fault ↓ / >21 fault ↑
The 2-wire loop, drawn

One pair of wires: power out, measurement back, HART digital data on top. Elegant since 1957.

Beyond analogue

HART superimposes FSK digital data on the 4–20 mA line: range, tag, diagnostics, secondary variables — readable with a handheld communicator or clamp-on modem, and the reason "smart transmitter" means re-rangeable without a screwdriver. Fieldbuses (PROFIBUS PA, FOUNDATION Fieldbus) and Ethernet-APL go fully digital; IO-Link (A05) does the same for discrete/factory sensors. The engineering logic never changes: a standard signal, a live zero, and diagnostics beat a perfect-precision signal that cannot tell you it is lying.

Pressure Measurement

The most versatile variable: measured directly, and borrowed to infer flow and level.

Three references, three instruments
TypeReferenceReadsClassic use
Gauge (barg)local atmospherep − p_atmpumps, compressors, utilities
Absolute (bara)vacuumpvacuum processes, boiling-point math
Differential (DP)a second process pointp₁ − p₂flow (orifice), level, filter blockage
Hydrostatics (the equation instrumentation borrows everywhere): p = ρ·g·h → 10 m of water ≈ 0.981 bar ≈ 98.1 kPa 1 bar = 100 kPa = 10.197 mH₂O = 750 mmHg = 14.50 psi

Sensing elements: the classic Bourdon tube (a flattened C-tube straightens under pressure — still on every mechanical gauge), and for transmitters the diaphragm read electronically: piezoresistive (silicon strain bridge, cheap, fast) or capacitive (metal diaphragm between capacitor plates — the architecture of the classic DP cell, superbly stable). The DP cell is the multitool of the plant: flow across an orifice, level from hydrostatic head, filter health from ΔP across the element.

Installation is half the measurement
  • 1
    Impulse lines carry the process to the cell — and its lies. Gas service: tap on top, lines slope up to the transmitter (condensate drains back). Liquid: tap on the side, lines slope down (bubbles rise back). Steam: condensate-filled lines with equal heads on both sides.
  • 2
    The 3-valve / 5-valve manifold (block-block-equalise) lets you zero a DP cell in place: block both, equalise → the cell must read 0. Every DP commissioning starts here.
  • 3
    Diaphragm seals + capillaries for corrosive, viscous or hygienic service — at the price of added τ and temperature-driven fill-fluid errors (keep capillaries short, symmetric, shaded).
  • 4
    Overrange & static pressure ratings differ. A 0–250 mbar DP cell may sit on a 40 bar line: it measures millibars of difference while surviving the full static pressure — two different specs on the datasheet.
🧠 Quick check

A gauge on a pump discharge reads 6.0 barg on a day when atmospheric pressure is 1.01 bar. The vacuum gauge on the suction reads −0.4 barg. What absolute pressures do the two ports see, and what does the pump add?

Tap to reveal ↓ Discharge: 6.0 + 1.01 = 7.01 bara. Suction: −0.4 + 1.01 = 0.61 bara. The pump adds Δp = 7.01 − 0.61 = 6.4 bar (≈ 65 m of water head — see A47 for the head language). Gauge readings subtract cleanly only because both share the same atmosphere; mixing barg and bara is the classic datasheet accident.

Temperature: RTDs, Thermocouples & Thermowells

Two rival technologies, one cold junction, and a steel finger that slows everything down.

Pt100 RTD — resistance as a thermometer
IEC 60751 (Callendar–Van Dusen, T ≥ 0 °C): R(T) = R₀·(1 + A·T + B·T²) R₀ = 100 Ω A = 3.9083×10⁻³ /°C B = −5.775×10⁻⁷ /°C² R(100 °C) = 138.51 Ω → mean sensitivity ≈ 0.385 Ω/°C ("α = 0.00385") Class A tolerance: ±(0.15 + 0.002·|T|) °C Class B: ±(0.30 + 0.005·|T|) °C

A platinum element's resistance rises predictably with temperature — stable, repeatable, linear-ish, the precision choice from −200 to ~600 °C. Its enemy is lead resistance: 0.385 Ω of copper looks like 1 °C. Hence the wiring ladder: 2-wire (leads add straight into the reading — short runs only), 3-wire (the standard: measurement cancels one lead pair, assuming equal leads), 4-wire (true Kelvin sensing, leads fully excluded — labs and custody). Excitation current must stay small (≈1 mA) or the element self-heats.

Thermocouples — the Seebeck workhorses

Two dissimilar metals joined at the hot end generate a µV-level EMF proportional to the temperature difference between hot junction and the meter's terminals. That means every thermocouple reading needs cold-junction compensation (CJC) — the input card measures its own terminal temperature and adds it back. TCs win on range (to +1700 °C), speed, ruggedness and price; RTDs win on accuracy and stability. Extension/compensating cable must match the TC type all the way to the CJC point — a copper splice creates two parasitic junctions and a silent offset.

Thermowells — protection at a price

The sensor almost never touches the process: it sits in a thermowell — a drilled or fabricated steel finger that permits sensor replacement under pressure. The costs: added time constant (τ jumps from ~2 s bare to 30–90 s in a heavy well — the dynamic lag of page 2), conduction error (a short well conducts pipe-wall temperature into the tip; immerse ≥ 10× tip diameter), and in high-velocity lines vortex-induced vibration — flow sheds vortices off the well and can snap it at resonance, which is why ASME PTC 19.3 TW wake-frequency calculations exist for critical installations. Spring-loaded inserts pressing the element to the well tip and thermal paste reclaim response time.

🧠 Quick check

A 2-wire Pt100 runs 40 m on 0.5 mm² copper (≈0.036 Ω/m per conductor). How large is the temperature lie, and which two fixes remove it?

Tap to reveal ↓ Two conductors in series: R_leads = 2 × 40 × 0.036 = 2.88 Ω → 2.88/0.385 ≈ +7.5 °C of pure fiction. Fixes: (1) go 3-wire (or 4-wire) so the input cancels lead resistance; (2) better — mount a head transmitter in the sensor connection head and run 4–20 mA instead of raw ohms: current loops don't care about cable resistance (page 3).

Flow: the Square-Root Family & Its Rivals

Half the art of flow measurement is remembering that an orifice measures ΔP, not flow.

DP flow — Bernoulli put to work
Orifice / venturi / nozzle (ISO 5167): Q = C·ε·A₂·√(2ΔP/ρ(1−β⁴)) Engineering shorthand: Q ∝ √ΔP with β = d/D (typ. 0.2–0.75) → 100 % flow = 100 % ΔP; 50 % flow = 25 % ΔP; 10 % flow = 1 % ΔP The transmitter (or DCS) applies square-root extraction to linearise.

A restriction accelerates the fluid; the pressure it loses is recoverable head converted to velocity (A47 Bernoulli). The DP cell reads that drop. Because ΔP falls with the square of flow, low-flow readings starve: at 10% flow the cell works on 1% of its span, and its fixed %-span error is magnified 50× in flow terms (interactive below). Practical turndown: ~3–4:1 classic, ~8–10:1 with a smart multivariable cell. Orifice plates demand straight pipe runs (rule-of-thumb 10D upstream / 5D downstream, more after double bends) and square, sharp edges — a nicked edge is a calibration change nobody logged.

Retrofit without cutting pipe → clamp-on ultrasonic. The orifice survives everywhere because it is cheap, understood, and ISO-standardised — with its square-root tax always attached.

Level: Hydrostatics, Radar & the Wet Leg

Level is pressure wearing a disguise — until radar removed the disguise entirely.

DP level — p = ρgh again
Open tank: ΔP = ρ·g·h (LP port vented) Closed tank, dry leg: ΔP = ρ·g·h + (p_vap cancels via LP line) Closed tank, wet leg: ΔP = ρ·g·h − ρ_fill·g·H (LP leg permanently full, height H) → at h = 0 the cell reads −ρ_fill·g·H: a NEGATIVE number. Fix by ranging, not plumbing: LRV = −ρ_fill·g·H (zero elevation) (The mirror case — transmitter mounted below the tap — needs zero suppression.)

In a pressurised vessel the LP side must see the vapour space so vapour pressure cancels. If that reference line can condense (steam drum, hot hydrocarbons), it is deliberately kept full — the wet leg — and its constant hydrostatic head is subtracted by ranging the transmitter with an elevated zero. Get ρ wrong (density changes with temperature or product grade) and the "level" moves while the surface doesn't — the classic DP-level lie, and the reason density-critical tanks graduate to radar or add a second cell for density compensation.

🧠 Quick check

A steam-drum DP level with a wet leg reads high after a cold start and drifts back over an hour. What's happening?

Tap to reveal ↓ The wet-leg fill was cold and dense at start; as the plant heats the leg, its fill warms, expands and ρ_fill drops, shrinking the subtracted head — the reading rises with no real level change (and partial leg boil-off does the same, violently). Cures: condensate pots that keep the leg topped and thermally stable, insulation, density-compensated ranging, or moving the duty to GWR. The instrument is honest; the reference column changed.

Calibration: As-Found, As-Left & the 5-Point Run

Calibration is comparison against a better standard, documented — everything else is adjustment folklore.

The vocabulary that audits check

Calibration = comparing the instrument to a reference standard and recording the errors. Adjustment (trim) = changing the instrument to reduce those errors. The two records that matter: as-found (state on arrival — this is the data that tells you whether last year's production measurements were valid) and as-left (state after any trim — the new starting point). An instrument found outside tolerance triggers an out-of-tolerance investigation: what product shipped against the bad reading? That question is why as-found data is sacred and why "just tweak it to read right" without records is a quality-system offence (ISO 9001 ¸ 7.1.5, IATF, FDA alike).

The 5-point up-down run: 0 % → 25 % → 50 % → 75 % → 100 % → 75 % → 50 % → 25 % → 0 % approach each point from ONE direction, never overshoot-and-back error_i (%span) = (reading_i − ideal_i)/span × 100 hysteresis at a point = |error_up − error_down| zero error: uniform offset at all points → zero trim span error: error growing with input → span trim (re-check zero after!)
Standards, traceability & tools

Traceability is an unbroken, documented chain: your working calibrator → the lab's reference standard → the national metrology institute (NIST, NPL, or Sri Lanka's MUSSD — Measurement Units, Standards & Services Department) → the SI. Each link carries a stated uncertainty, and ISO/IEC 17025 is the accreditation that says a lab's chain and methods hold. Field toolkit: documenting loop calibrators (source/measure mA, V, Ω, TC types — Fluke 754, Beamex MC6), pressure pumps + reference modules, deadweight testers (primary pressure standard: p = F/A from real masses), dry-block calibrators for temperature, and the HART communicator for range/trim on smart devices. Rule of thumb for intervals: start at manufacturer recommendation (6–12 months), then let as-found drift history stretch or shrink it — calibrate the drifters often, the stable rarely.

🧠 Quick check

A transmitter's as-found run shows errors of +0.42, +0.44, +0.41, +0.43, +0.42 %span at the five points. Zero trim, span trim, or both?

Tap to reveal ↓ Zero trim only. The error is uniform (~+0.42 %span everywhere) — a pure offset. A span error would grow with input (small at 0 %, large at 100 %). Trim zero, re-run the five points to produce a clean as-left, and log both tables. If the errors had been 0.0 / +0.2 / +0.4 / +0.6 / +0.8, that ramp is a span error: trim span at 100 %, then re-verify zero — the two adjustments interact.

Uncertainty, TUR & the Loop Error Budget

A measurement without an uncertainty is an opinion with digits.

Uncertainty in one card (GUM lite)
Independent error sources u₁, u₂, … combine by root-sum-square: u_c = √(u₁² + u₂² + u₃² + …) (combined standard uncertainty) U = k·u_c, k = 2 → ≈95 % confidence (expanded uncertainty) Type A: evaluated statistically (repeat readings, s/√n) Type B: everything else (datasheets, cert values, resolution/√12)

The RSS combination is the same variance algebra as the tolerance stacks in A21 — uncorrelated errors add in quadrature, so one dominant term rules the budget: a 0.5 % error next to three 0.1 % errors gives √(0.25+0.03) ≈ 0.53 %, not 0.8 %. Improve the biggest term or improve nothing.

TUR — is your standard good enough?
Test Uncertainty Ratio: TUR = tolerance of unit-under-test ÷ uncertainty of the standard Target: TUR ≥ 4:1 (ANSI/NCSL Z540 tradition) Checking a ±0.5 %-span transmitter needs a standard good to ≲0.125 % at the test points. Below 4:1 → guard-banding: shrink the acceptance limits so a "pass" still means pass (accept if |error| ≤ tolerance − U_standard — the ISO 14253 decision-rule idea).

This is why a shop multimeter cannot "calibrate" a laboratory-grade transmitter: with TUR near 1:1 a pass verdict is a coin toss at the limits. The ratio, not the brand, decides what may calibrate what.

The whole-loop error budget

The operator reads the sum of the chain: primary element error (orifice ±1 %), transmitter (±0.075 %), ADC input card (±0.1 %), and display quantisation. RSS them: √(1² + 0.075² + 0.1²) ≈ ±1.006 % — the orifice owns the budget; polishing the transmitter is cosmetic. The professional habit: build the budget before buying, spend money on the dominant term, and quote the loop (not the transmitter) accuracy in the design dossier. For safety loops, this same arithmetic feeds the safety-accuracy claims in SIL verification (IEC 61511 — its own module, next in this series).

Reading P&IDs — the ISA-5.1 Grammar

A P&ID is a sentence diagram of the plant. Learn the alphabet once, read every plant forever.

The tag: two letter groups + a loop number
F T - 101 │ │ └── loop number (all members of loop 101 share it) │ └────── succeeding letters = FUNCTION: T transmitter · I indicator · C controller │ V valve · S switch · A alarm (with H/L) · E element · Y compute/relay · Q totalise └──────── first letter = VARIABLE: F flow · P pressure · T temperature · L level A analysis · S speed · W weight · Z position (+ modifier: D differential, F ratio) Examples: PDT = pressure-differential transmitter · TIC = temp indicating controller LSH = level switch high · FQI = flow totaliser indicator · PSV = pressure safety valve

Read tags in order: what is measured → what is done with it → which loop. FT-101, FIC-101 and FV-101 are one sentence: flow is transmitted, controlled, and actuated in loop 101. The succeeding-letter Y (FY-101) is the quiet workhorse — square-root extractors, I/P converters, selectors and calculations all hide behind it.

Balloons & lines — where the function lives

The four balloon homes (field, panel, DCS, PLC) and the four signal line styles — 90 % of P&ID literacy.

Reading discipline & the valve fine print
  • 1
    Walk the loop, not the page: find the primary element, follow its signal through Y-functions to the controller balloon, then out to the final element. One loop = one story; highlight it.
  • 2
    Failure position is on the drawing: FC (fail closed), FO (fail open), FL (fail last) under each control valve — chosen for process safety on air/power loss, not convenience (cooling water FO, fuel FC). It pairs with actuator action and positioner config; commissioning must verify it physically.
  • 3
    SIS lives apart: interlock balloons (diamond) and their sensors/valves form the safety instrumented system — deliberately separate from control instruments (page 7's diverse point switches; IEC 61511 module next). Never "borrow" a control transmitter for a trip without the study saying so.
  • 4
    Cross-references are the plot: off-page connectors, note flags and the line list carry half the design intent. The instrument index + loop diagrams (ISA-5.4) turn one P&ID line into wiring reality.
🧠 Quick check

Decode: LT-204 → LY-204 → LIC-204 (circle-in-square) → LV-204 (FC). Then: what is LSH-205 doing on the same vessel, and why is it a separate loop number?

Tap to reveal ↓ Level transmitter 204 feeds a compute/relay function (LY — perhaps damping or a characteriser), then a level indicating controller running in the DCS, which drives level valve 204 that fails closed on air loss. LSH-205 is an independent level switch high — a separate device on a separate loop number because it belongs to the protection layer (alarm or trip), deliberately independent of the 204 control loop so one failure cannot silence both control and alarm. Diversity by design.
Page 11 / 16 · Closing the Loop

Final Elements & Loop Commissioning

The measurement chain earns its keep only when the valve moves right and the loop folder proves it.

Control valves in one card
Valve capacity: Q = C_v·√(ΔP/SG) [US gpm, psi] (metric K_v = 0.865·C_v) Inherent characteristics: quick-opening · linear · equal-percentage (=% most common: equal increments of travel → equal % increases of flow — compensates the installed ΔP collapse as flow rises, keeping loop gain roughly constant) Sizing habit: normal flow at 60–80 % open; never buy the line size by default.

The valve stack: body + trim (sets C_v and characteristic), actuator (spring-diaphragm or piston; spring direction sets the fail position on the P&ID), and positioner — a feedback controller on stem position, today a smart digital device whose diagnostics (stiction, air leakage, travel deviation) feed predictive maintenance (A23). Stiction + deadband in a valve produce the tell-tale sawtooth limit cycle no PID tuning can remove (A10) — fix the valve, then tune. I/P converters (4–20 mA → 0.2–1 bar) and the plant air quality behind them (A12) are part of the measurement chain's last metre.

Loop check & commissioning ritual
  • 1
    The loop folder: P&ID extract, loop diagram (ISA-5.4), instrument datasheet, range/units, cal certificate, alarm/trip list. If it isn't written, it isn't ranged.
  • 2
    Wire check (cold): continuity, screen landed one end, loop resistance, insulation — then power and confirm the live zero: 4.0 mA at LRV, not 0.
  • 3
    Simulate at the source: inject pressure/temperature/mA at the field end and confirm 0-25-50-75-100 % arrives correct at HMI, in engineering units, with alarms firing at listed values — this catches scaling errors the bench never sees (A07's 0–27648 raw counts, byte order, √ extraction on the wrong side).
  • 4
    Stroke the valve: 0/25/50/75/100 % commands both directions — verify travel, speed, fail action (dump the air!), and positioner autotune report filed.
  • 5
    Bump before closing the permit: a small controller-in-manual step; watch PV respond in the right direction with sane dynamics. Only then hand the loop to auto and to A10's tuning rules.

Equal-percentage trim keeps installed gain flat as line ΔP collapses with flow.

Loop killer

stiction

Sawtooth PV + square OP = valve stem sticking. No tuning cures mechanics.

Recap, Failure Modes & Self-Test

The discipline in eight lines — then the six classic ways measurements lie.

Eight ideas to keep
  • 1
    The chain is the instrument. Primary element → transmitter → signal → controller → valve; the loop reads the sum of all of them.
  • 2
    Errors live in %span. Range tight: the same ±0.25 % is 4× more °C on a 4× wider span.
  • 3
    4–20 mA endures for reasons: immunity to cable resistance, loop power on two wires, live zero + NAMUR fault bands, HART on top.
  • 4
    Each variable has its physics and its traps: impulse-line slopes (P), lead resistance & CJC (T), √ΔP turndown (F), wet-leg density (L).
  • 5
    Calibration = comparison + record. 5-point up-down, as-found/as-left, zero vs span diagnosis, traceable standards, TUR ≥ 4:1.
  • 6
    Uncertainty adds RSS — find and fix the dominant term; quote the loop, not the transmitter.
  • 7
    ISA-5.1 is a grammar: variable-function-loop tags, balloon homes, line styles, fail positions — read the loop as a sentence.
  • 8
    Commissioning is a ritual: loop folder, live zero, source-to-screen 5-point, valve stroke + fail action, bump test. Then — and only then — tune.
Six ways measurements lie
⚠ Plugged / wrong-slope impulse line

Frozen reading or slow lies while the process moves. Blowdown routines and correct slopes (gas up, liquid down) are maintenance, not options.

⚠ The %-of-span stretch

Transmitter re-ranged 4× wider "to cover the new case" — every error and alarm deadband silently 4× worse in engineering units.

⚠ Copper in the thermocouple run

A terminal-block "repair" with copper wire creates parasitic junctions; the reading offsets by the cabinet-to-field temperature difference and drifts daily.

⚠ Square-root twice (or never)

Extraction enabled in both transmitter and DCS → flow reads √ of itself; enabled in neither → parabolic lies. One √, one place, documented.

⚠ Wet leg half empty

Boiled-off or leak-drained reference column shrinks the subtracted head — level reads high, operators drain a "full" drum. Condensate pots exist for this.

⚠ Calibrated to a worse standard

TUR ~1:1 "calibration" with a shop meter stamps uncertainty as compliance. As-found data against a real standard later rewrites a year of production.

🧠 Final quiz — 5 questions

1 · Why does a broken wire read differently from a true zero-flow signal on a 4–20 mA loop?

Tap to reveal ↓ Zero flow = 4.0 mA (the live zero). A broken wire = 0 mA — below the NAMUR 3.6 mA fault threshold, so the system declares sensor failure instead of displaying a fake 0 % reading. That's the whole point of not starting the scale at 0 mA.

2 · A Pt100 reads 138.51 Ω on a 4-wire bench check. What temperature is that, and why did the technician insist on 4-wire?

Tap to reveal ↓ 138.51 Ω is the IEC 60751 value for exactly 100 °C. 4-wire (Kelvin) sensing drives current down one pair and measures voltage on the other, excluding all lead resistance — on a bench verification you want the element's truth, not the test-lead story (0.4 Ω of leads would masquerade as +1 °C).

3 · An orifice flow loop must work from 20 t/h down to 2 t/h. Why is one DP transmitter not enough, and what are two engineering outs?

Tap to reveal ↓ 10:1 flow turndown = 100:1 DP turndown (ΔP ∝ Q²): at 2 t/h the cell sees 1 % of its DP span, where its %-span error is amplified ~50× in flow. Outs: (1) stacked transmitters — a high-range and a low-range cell on the same taps with automatic switchover; (2) change technology — Coriolis or magmeter with genuine 40–100:1 turndown. (A smart multivariable cell stretches to ~10:1 but not 100:1.)

4 · The as-found run shows errors 0.0 / +0.1 / +0.3 / +0.6 / +1.0 %span with 0.3 % hysteresis between up and down at mid-scale. Diagnose.

Tap to reveal ↓ The ramp growing with input is a span error (gain too high) → span trim, then re-check zero. The 0.3 % up-down gap is hysteresis — mechanical (linkage, diaphragm set) — which no trim removes; if it exceeds the tolerance the instrument is repaired or replaced, not adjusted. Two different diseases in one table: that's why the run is five points, both directions.

5 · On the P&ID, FV-301 carries "FO". The loop cools a reactor. Why FO, and what must commissioning physically verify?

Tap to reveal ↓ Fail Open: on instrument-air or signal loss the valve springs open so cooling water keeps flowing — the safe direction for an exothermic process. Commissioning must dump the air at the actuator and watch the valve actually travel to open (and time it): fail action lives in spring orientation and positioner/actuator hardware, not in software, and a wrong-sprung actuator passes every electronic test until the day it matters.
Memory Hook

"Live zero, tight span, traceable trim —
the loop is only as honest as its weakest link."

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade numericals — elevated-zero scaling, RTD arithmetic, DP-flow ranging and the wet-leg level.

WE-1 · Elevated-zero temperature loop
Range: −50 … +150 °C → LRV = −50, URV = +150, span = 200 °C (a) I at PV = 25 °C: I = 4 + 16·(25 − (−50))/200 = 4 + 16·0.375 = 10.0 mA (b) PV at I = 17.6 mA: PV = −50 + (17.6 − 4)/16 · 200 = −50 + 0.85·200 = +120 °C (c) The 4 mA point IS −50 °C — a live, valid reading, not a fault: fault-low is ≤ 3.6 mA (NAMUR), fully below the scale.

Discussion: elevated zeros are where scaling mistakes hide. A technician who assumes "4 mA = 0 °C" mis-enters alarms by 50 °C. Always state LRV/URV on the loop sheet and check the 0-25-50-75-100 % table in engineering units: −50 / 0 / +50 / +100 / +150 °C here.

WE-2 · Pt100 arithmetic + the unequal-lead lie
(a) R at 60 °C (IEC 60751): R = 100·(1 + 3.9083×10⁻³·60 − 5.775×10⁻⁷·60²) = 100·(1 + 0.23450 − 0.00208) = 123.24 Ω (b) 3-wire circuit assumes equal leads. Real leads: 1.2 Ω and 1.0 Ω → uncompensated residual = 1.2 − 1.0 = 0.2 Ω → error = 0.2 / 0.385 ≈ +0.52 °C (c) Class A element tolerance at 60 °C: ±(0.15 + 0.002·60) = ±0.27 °C → the lead imbalance is 2× the sensor's own tolerance!

Discussion: 3-wire compensation is only as good as lead symmetry — corroded terminals and mixed cable batches break it. Where the number matters, go 4-wire or head-mounted transmitter (WE-1's loop), and record which wiring scheme the calibration used.

WE-3 · Orifice ranging & low-flow error
DP cell ranged 0–250 mbar ≙ 0–40 m³/h (√ extraction in the transmitter). (a) Flow at ΔP = 90 mbar: Q = 40·√(90/250) = 40·0.60 = 24 m³/h (b) ΔP at 10 m³/h: ΔP = (10/40)²·250 = 0.0625·250 = 15.6 mbar (6.25 % of DP span!) (c) Cell accuracy ±0.1 % span = ±0.25 mbar → flow error = ½ · (0.25/15.6) ×100 = ±0.8 % at 25 % flow at 10 % flow (2.5 mbar): ½·(0.25/2.5) = ±5 % — the turndown wall.

Discussion: part (c) is the page-6 demo in exam form. The ½ factor comes from differentiating Q ∝ √ΔP: dQ/Q = ½·dΔP/ΔP (Derivation 2). State it, use it, and you will justify stacked cells or a Coriolis without hand-waving.

WE-4 · Wet-leg level ranging
Vessel: level span h = 0–2.0 m of product, ρ = 850 kg/m³. Wet leg: H = 2.5 m, fill ρ_f = 1000 kg/m³. Transmitter at bottom-tap height. LRV (h = 0): ΔP = 0 − 1000·9.81·2.5 = −24.53 kPa URV (h = 2 m): ΔP = 850·9.81·2.0 − 24 525 = 16 677 − 24 525 = −7.85 kPa Span = 16.68 kPa; the whole range is NEGATIVE (elevated zero). Half tank (h = 1 m): ΔP = −16.19 kPa → I = 4 + 16·(−16.19+24.53)/16.68 = 12.0 mA ✓

Discussion: the sanity check is always the 50 % point: half the product head above LRV must give 12.0 mA. If the technician "zeroes" this transmitter at h = 0 with the equalising valve open instead of entering the calculated LRV, the wet leg's −24.5 kPa gets erased and the range is fiction. Range by arithmetic, verify by manifold.

Problem Set — 10 Questions

Scaling, sensors, calibration verdicts and P&ID grammar. Work first, reveal second.

P1 · A 0–16 bar transmitter reads 13.2 mA. What pressure — and what % of span?

Tap to reveal ↓(13.2−4)/16 = 57.5 % → PV = 0.575 × 16 = 9.2 bar. The %-first habit catches gross errors instantly (13.2 mA must be a bit above mid-scale; 12 mA would be exactly 8 bar).

P2 · The DCS shows a temperature frozen at −62 °C on a −50…+150 °C loop. mA meter reads 3.2 mA. Diagnosis?

Tap to reveal ↓3.2 mA is below the NAMUR 3.6 mA fault-low band: the transmitter is signalling its own failure (or the loop is starving — supply/wiring). The −62 °C is just 3.2 mA pushed through the scaling math. Treat as diagnostics, not data; check supply voltage, loop resistance, then the device via HART.

P3 · Type-K thermocouple, 25 m to the panel. The stores had no K-extension cable, so copper instrument cable was used "temporarily". The panel sits 18 °C cooler than the field head. What error appears?

Tap to reveal ↓The copper splice moves the effective cold junction from the panel (where CJC measures) to the field head. The uncompensated span is the head-to-panel difference: reading ≈ 18 °C low, varying with weather and cabinet heating. Fix: K-extension (KX) or compensating (KCA/KCB) cable end-to-end, or a head transmitter converting to mA at the sensor.

P4 · A thermowell upgrade doubles the well mass and the measured τ goes from 8 s to 45 s on a process whose dominant time constant is 60 s. Consequence for the control loop?

Tap to reveal ↓The sensor lag is now ~75 % of the process lag — far beyond the ≤1/10 rule. The loop's apparent dead time and phase lag balloon; the PID must be detuned (slower Ti, lower Kc) and disturbance rejection degrades badly (A10's phase-margin budget eaten by the well). Cures: spring-loaded tip-contact insert, thermal paste, thinner/tapered well within PTC 19.3 limits, or accept slower control and say so in the design.

P5 · Why will a magmeter not read demineralised water or diesel, and what property draws the line?

Tap to reveal ↓Faraday's law needs the fluid itself to be the moving conductor: E = k·B·v·D requires conductivity above ~5 µS/cm. Demin water (~0.1 µS/cm) and hydrocarbons (~pS/cm) can't complete the electrode circuit — the meter reads noise. Use Coriolis, ultrasonic, vortex or turbine instead.

P6 · As-found errors: −0.9 / −0.7 / −0.4 / −0.2 / 0.0 %span at 0/25/50/75/100 %. Zero trim, span trim, or both — in which order?

Tap to reveal ↓Both. There's a −0.9 % offset at zero AND a +0.9 % ramp across the span (errors shrink as input rises → gain slightly high... actually output slope is high by +0.9 % of span across the range). Procedure: zero trim first (fix −0.9 at 0 %), then span trim at 100 %, then re-check zero (they interact), then a full clean as-left run. One-point "calibrations" cannot even see this pattern.

P7 · Your reference standard has U = 0.15 %; you certify transmitters to ±0.5 %. TUR? Is a measured error of 0.45 % a pass?

Tap to reveal ↓TUR = 0.5/0.15 ≈ 3.3:1 — below the 4:1 target, so guard-band: accept only if |error| ≤ 0.5 − 0.15 = 0.35 %. The 0.45 % result lands in the grey zone — "undetermined": it may be conforming, but you cannot prove it with this standard. Options: better standard, or report with the decision rule stated (ISO 14253 logic).

P8 · Loop budget: orifice ±1.0 %, DP cell ±0.075 %, input card ±0.1 %, √ extraction rounding ±0.05 %. Loop accuracy — and where would you spend an upgrade dollar?

Tap to reveal ↓RSS = √(1.0² + 0.075² + 0.1² + 0.05²) = √(1.0 + 0.0056 + 0.01 + 0.0025) ≈ ±1.01 %. The orifice IS the budget: replacing the transmitter with a ±0.04 % model changes nothing visible. Money goes to the primary element (better plate condition, meter run, or a magmeter/Coriolis) — or nowhere, if ±1 % meets the process need.

P9 · Decode fully: PDIT-405 (circle-in-square), PDAH-405, and the dashed line joining them.

Tap to reveal ↓PDIT-405: Pressure-Differential Indicating Transmitter, loop 405, displayed on the DCS (circle-in-square) — likely a filter ΔP monitor. PDAH-405: the associated Alarm High on the same differential pressure. The dashed line is an electric signal. Sentence: "filter DP is transmitted and indicated; high DP raises an alarm" — a maintenance-planning instrument (A23's predictive input), not a control loop.

P10 · During loop check, HMI shows exactly half the injected value at every point (50 % in → 25 % shown). Name the two classic culprits and the test that separates them.

Tap to reveal ↓A clean proportional halving means a scaling mismatch, not wiring: (1) PLC raw-count range wrong — e.g. configured 0–55296 instead of 0–27648 (S7 world, A07), or 4–20 mA entered as 0–20 mA; (2) engineering-units span entered double (URV 200 instead of 100). Separate them by reading the RAW counts at a known mA: if raw is right but EU is wrong → units/span entry; if raw itself is half → card range/jumper config. (If the relationship were √-shaped rather than ½-proportional, suspect double √ extraction.)

Key Derivations, Glossary & Exam Traps

Three derivations worth owning, the symbol table, and seven traps that separate technicians from engineers.

Derivation 1 · Why a current loop ignores cable resistance
Series loop: 24 V supply, transmitter (a regulated current sink), cable R_c, sense R_s = 250 Ω. KVL: 24 = V_tx + I·R_c + I·R_s The transmitter electronically absorbs whatever V_tx the equation leaves over, holding I = f(PV) — current is the regulated variable, voltage is the slack. Reading at the DCS: V = I·R_s — depends only on I and the known R_s. R_c vanished. Limit: the compliance budget. V_tx(min) ≈ 10–12 V, so R_c(max) = (24 − V_tx(min) − 20 mA·250 Ω)/20 mA ≈ (24 − 12 − 5)/0.02 = 350 Ω — kilometres of copper before the loop even notices. A voltage signal would have divided itself across R_c from the first metre. QED, and quod erat 1957.
Derivation 2 · Q ∝ √ΔP and the ½ error factor
Bernoulli between pipe (1) and vena contracta (2), incompressible, level line: p₁ + ½ρv₁² = p₂ + ½ρv₂² Continuity: v₁A₁ = v₂A₂ → v₁ = v₂·(A₂/A₁) → ΔP = ½ρv₂²·(1 − β⁴) (β = d/D, A₂/A₁ = β²) → v₂ = √( 2ΔP / ρ(1−β⁴) ) → Q = C·ε·A₂·√(2ΔP/ρ(1−β⁴)) (C: discharge, ε: expansibility) Error propagation: ln Q = ½·ln ΔP + const → dQ/Q = ½·dΔP/ΔP — the factor that turned 0.1 % of DP span into 5 % of flow at 10 % rate (WE-3).
Derivation 3 · How 3-wire RTD compensation works
Leads a, b (current-carrying pair) and c (sense). Assume R_a = R_b = R_L. Measurement 1 (element path): M₁ = R_RTD + R_a + R_b = R_RTD + 2R_L Measurement 2 (lead loop via c): M₂ = R_a + R_b = 2R_L (modern inputs measure this directly) Compensated result: R_RTD = M₁ − M₂ — exact IF the leads match. Unequal leads (R_a = R_b + δ): residual error = δ → δ/0.385 °C per ohm (WE-2's 0.52 °C). 4-wire: force I down one pair, read V on the other (no current in sense leads) → R_RTD = V/I with zero lead sensitivity — why labs refuse anything less.

Seven exam & plant traps
  • 1
    % of span ≠ % of reading. Convert both to engineering units at the operating point before comparing datasheets — the page-2 quiz in disguise, and examiners' favourite bait.
  • 2
    barg vs bara vs ΔP. Pumps add differential; datasheets rate absolute or gauge; vacuum reads negative gauge. Write the suffix every single time.
  • 3
    One √, one place. Square-root extraction in transmitter OR DCS, never both, never neither — and the loop folder must say which.
  • 4
    Approach direction matters. Calibration points overshot-and-backed hide hysteresis; the 5-point run is up THEN down, monotonic approaches only.
  • 5
    Re-ranging is not calibrating. HART re-range moves LRV/URV in software; it adjusts nothing physical and needs no reference — a trim against a standard does. Confusing the two voids the certificate.
  • 6
    Two grounds, one loop, big noise. Screen landed at both ends → ground loop currents ride the signal. Screen: one end (panel), insulated at the field.
  • 7
    The instrument is rarely the liar. Impulse lines, wet legs, lead wires, CJC, scaling entries — the chain around the sensor causes most "instrument faults". Walk the chain before condemning the device.
