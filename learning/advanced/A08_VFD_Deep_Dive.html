Page 1 · Foundations

What a VFD Really Does

It does not "slow the motor down with a resistor." It synthesises a brand-new AC supply at whatever frequency and voltage the motor needs.

Precise Definition

A Variable Frequency Drive is a power-electronic converter that takes fixed-frequency, fixed-voltage mains and produces an adjustable-frequency, adjustable-voltage three-phase output, so an AC induction or PM motor can run at any speed and torque on demand.

An induction motor's synchronous speed is locked to supply frequency: ns = 120·f / p. A 4-pole motor on 50 Hz turns at 1500 rpm — and nothing you do to the voltage alone changes that. To change speed you must change f. The VFD's entire reason to exist is to manufacture that variable f cleanly, while keeping magnetic flux correct so the motor still makes torque.

Speed = frequency

Output Hz sets rotor speed. 0–high Hz → standstill to over-speed.

Torque = flux × current

Keep flux constant by ramping V with f. That's the core trick.

Soft everything

Controlled accel/decel removes inrush, water-hammer, belt shock.

Why not a throttle valve or a gearbox?

Mechanically throttling a pump or fan to reduce flow wastes the energy as heat and turbulence — the motor still runs full speed. A VFD instead drops the motor's speed, and because pump/fan power follows the cube of speed (affinity laws, page 7), a 20% speed cut can save ~50% of the energy. That single fact is why VFDs dominate HVAC, water and process industries.

Page 2 · Hardware

The Power Chain: Rectifier → DC Bus → Inverter

Every standard VFD is three stages in series. Understand these three and you understand the whole drive.

The universal VFD topology — a voltage-source inverter (VSI). Power flows left to right; the brain sits underneath.

① Rectifier

A diode bridge turns incoming AC into rough DC. Cheap, robust, but draws non-sinusoidal current → harmonics (page 9).

② DC Bus

Big electrolytic capacitors smooth the DC and store energy. Bus voltage ≈ 1.35× line-to-line RMS (≈ 565 V DC on 415 V mains).

③ Inverter

Six IGBTs switched by PWM chop the DC bus into a synthetic 3-phase AC of any frequency and voltage.

The pre-charge detail engineers forget

Those bus capacitors look like a short circuit at power-up. Without protection, the inrush would weld the rectifier diodes. So every drive has a pre-charge resistor (or thyristor soft-charge) that limits initial current, then a relay/contactor bypasses it once the bus is charged. A failed pre-charge relay is a classic "drive trips on power-up" fault.

Page 3 · The Synthesis

PWM: How DC Becomes a Sine Wave

The inverter output is not a sine wave at all — it is a train of fixed-height DC pulses whose average traces a sine.

Sine-triangle PWM. A high-frequency triangle "carrier" compared against a sine "reference" decides each pulse width. The motor's inductance integrates the pulses back into near-sinusoidal current.

The carrier-frequency trade-off

The switching (carrier) frequency is one of the most consequential settings on a drive:

Higher fsw (e.g. 8–16 kHz)Lower fsw (e.g. 2–4 kHz)
✓ Quieter motor (above audible whine)✓ Lower switching losses → cooler drive
✓ Smoother current, less heating✓ Higher continuous output current
✗ More heat in IGBTs (derate output)✗ Audible acoustic whine from motor
✗ Worse reflected-wave / cable effects✗ More current ripple & torque ripple

Rule of thumb: keep it as low as the acoustic requirement allows, then add an output filter if cable length forces a higher value.

Page 4 · Control Strategy I

V/f (Scalar) Control — Keep the Flux Constant

The original, open-loop strategy. Simple, cheap, good enough for fans and pumps.

The governing idea

Motor flux is set by voltage ÷ frequency. If you lower frequency to slow the motor but keep voltage high, flux soars, the iron saturates and the motor overheats. So V/f control ramps voltage in proportion to frequency to hold flux — and therefore available torque — roughly constant.

Φ ∝ V / f → hold V/f = constant
e.g. 400 V / 50 Hz = 8.0 → at 25 Hz, apply 200 V
Above base speed: V is capped → flux falls → "field weakening" (constant power, less torque)

The V/f profile. Note the small "voltage boost" near 0 Hz that compensates for stator resistance so the motor can break away under load.

Where V/f shines

Variable-torque loads (centrifugal pumps, fans, blowers) where torque demand falls with speed and precise low-speed control isn't critical. Also for running multiple motors from one drive.

⚠ Where V/f struggles

Poor torque below ~5 Hz, no torque control, sluggish dynamic response, and it can't hold a stalled load. For cranes, winders and positioning you need vector control.

Page 5 · Control Strategy II

Vector Control & DTC — Controlling Torque Directly

Instead of guessing flux from a V/f ratio, these methods model the motor in real time and command flux and torque independently.

Field-Oriented Control (FOC / vector)

Vector control mathematically rotates the three stator currents into a reference frame that spins with the rotor flux (the d–q transform, via Clarke + Park). In that frame the motor looks like a DC motor: the d-axis current controls magnetising flux and the q-axis current controls torque — and they no longer interfere. The drive can then command exact torque, even at zero speed.

T ∝ i_q · Φ_d   (torque from q-axis current)
Φ_d set by i_d   (flux from d-axis current)
→ decoupled control = fast, precise, full torque at 0 rpm

Sensorless vector

Estimates rotor position from a motor model (back-EMF, current). No encoder. ~0.5% speed accuracy, torque down to ~1 Hz.

Closed-loop vector

Adds an encoder for true rotor feedback. Full torque at 0 rpm, <0.01% speed accuracy. For cranes, lifts, winders.

Direct Torque Control (DTC)

ABB's alternative: skip the PWM modulator entirely. DTC estimates flux and torque every ~25 µs and uses a hysteresis switching table to pick the next inverter state directly — driving torque and flux to stay inside tight bands. The result is the fastest torque response available (microseconds) with no tacho needed, at the cost of variable switching frequency and more torque ripple.

Page 7 · Why It Pays

The Affinity Laws — Where the Energy Goes

The single most important economic fact about VFDs on centrifugal loads.

The cube law
Flow  Q ∝ N   (speed)
Pressure H ∝ N²
Power   P ∝ N³  ← the money line

Run a pump at 80% speed and it delivers 80% flow — but draws only 0.8³ ≈ 51% of the power. Run at 50% speed → just 12.5% power. A throttle valve at the same flow would still draw ~90%+. This non-linearity is why a VFD retrofit on an oversized pump often pays back in under two years.

100%

Speed → 100% power (baseline)

51%

80% speed → ~51% power

12.5%

50% speed → ~12.5% power

Caveat engineers miss

The cube law only holds for variable-torque centrifugal loads against a friction-dominated system curve. With high static head (lifting water uphill) the savings shrink — and below a minimum speed the pump may not overcome the static head at all. Always check the system curve, not just the pump curve.

Page 8 · Stopping

Braking & Regeneration

When a load drives the motor (overhauling), the motor becomes a generator and pumps energy back into the DC bus. That energy must go somewhere.

METHOD 1

Dynamic (resistor) braking

A brake chopper IGBT dumps the excess bus energy into a braking resistor as heat when bus voltage rises. Simple, cheap, common on cranes and centrifuges. Wastes the energy.

METHOD 2

Regenerative (active front-end)

An active rectifier (IGBTs instead of diodes) feeds braking energy back to the grid. Costs more but recovers energy and gives clean, low-harmonic input. Used on lifts, test rigs, downhill conveyors.

DC injection braking

For a quick stop without a resistor, the drive can inject DC current into the stator. This creates a stationary magnetic field that drags the rotor to a halt. Good for fans and saws — but it heats the rotor and gives no holding torque once stopped, so it's not a substitute for a mechanical brake on a hoist.

⚠ The "overvoltage on decel" trip

The #1 nuisance trip: you set too fast a decel ramp on a high-inertia load with no brake resistor → regenerated energy spikes the DC bus → the drive trips on DC bus overvoltage (OV) to protect the capacitors. Fix: extend the decel time, enable the resistor/chopper, or fit a regen front-end.

Page 9 · Side Effects

Harmonics, dV/dt & Cable Effects

A VFD is a non-linear load and a fast switch. Two distinct problems: dirty input current, and a violent output edge.

Input-side: harmonics

The diode rectifier draws current in sharp pulses, not a clean sine — injecting 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th harmonics back onto the supply. High THDi overheats transformers/neutrals, distorts voltage and can trip other equipment. IEEE 519 sets the limits.

Mitigations, in rough order of effectiveness/cost: line reactor / DC choke → passive harmonic filter → 12/18-pulse rectifier → active front-end (lowest THDi, also enables regen).

Output-side: reflected-wave & dV/dt

IGBT edges are brutally fast (rise times ~50–100 ns). On a long motor cable, the PWM pulse reflects off the impedance mismatch at the motor and can double the voltage at the motor terminals — stressing winding insulation and causing premature failure. The same fast edges drive common-mode shaft currents that pit bearings via EDM (electric discharge machining).

Long cables

Add an output dV/dt or sine filter; keep cable within drive's spec length.

Bearing currents

Use an insulated NDE bearing, a shaft grounding ring, and shielded symmetrical cable.

EMC

Bond shields 360°, separate power/signal runs, fit the internal EMC filter.

Page 10 · Engineering Practice

Commissioning & Selection Checklist

What separates a drive that runs from a drive that lasts.

  1. 1
    Size for current, not just kW. The motor's full-load amps (FLA) and the load's torque profile (constant vs variable torque) decide the frame — a constant-torque load needs more overload headroom (150% for 60 s).
  2. 2
    Run autotune. Vector and DTC modes measure stator resistance, inductance and rotor time-constant. Skipping autotune cripples low-speed torque and accuracy.
  3. 3
    Set accel/decel to the inertia. Match ramps to load inertia; add a brake resistor for high-inertia decel to avoid OV trips.
  4. 4
    Protect the motor. Set electronic thermal overload (I²t) to motor FLA; on long runs add a motor thermistor (PTC) input — VFD cooling differs from across-the-line.
  5. 5
    Mind cable length. Beyond the drive's rated metres, fit an output reactor/filter and use shielded symmetrical cable to control reflected-wave and bearing currents.
  6. 6
    Use Safe Torque Off (STO). The built-in SIL-rated STO input removes torque safely without dropping the line contactor — the modern way to integrate the drive into the machine safety system.
Real Industrial Example — full circle to Micro 08

On an automated bottling line, a single 22 kW VFD runs the main conveyor in sensorless vector mode for smooth product handling, while booster-pump VFDs on the rinse station run V/f and ride the affinity-law savings. The micro card's "Change the Frequency, Change the Speed" is exactly this — and now you know which control mode does it, and why each station picked the one it did.

Quick Check

A high-inertia centrifuge trips on DC bus overvoltage every time it decelerates. The drive has no brake resistor fitted. Give two valid fixes and say which one also recovers energy.

tap to reveal answer ▾
During decel the load drives the motor as a generator, pumping energy into the DC bus and raising its voltage until the OV trip fires. Fixes: (1) lengthen the decel ramp so the regenerated power is small enough for the drive to bleed off; (2) fit a brake chopper + resistor to dump the energy as heat; or (3) use a regenerative (active front-end) drive. Only option 3 recovers the energy back to the grid — the resistor merely wastes it as heat.
Page 11 · MSc Practice

Worked Examples

Four exam-grade problems, solved in full.

WE 1 · The affinity-law payback

A 45 kW supply fan runs 6 000 h/yr at 75% flow. Today a damper throttles it (motor still draws ≈ 90% power). Compute the annual energy saved by a VFD at 75% speed, and the CO₂-adjacent number your manager actually reads: money at 30 LKR/kWh.

VFD at 75% speed: P ∝ n³ → 0.75³ = 0.42 → P ≈ 0.42 × 45 = 19.0 kW Damper at 75% flow: P ≈ 0.90 × 45 = 40.5 kW Saving = (40.5 − 19.0) × 6 000 h = 129 000 kWh/yr At 30 LKR/kWh → ≈ 3.9 million LKR/yr — the drive pays for itself in months.

Discussion: the cube law is the whole business case (A47's fan laws). Honesty check: the law assumes pure friction systems — high static head flattens the curve (see Trap 2), and drive losses (~3%) plus motor efficiency off-BEP shave the ideal number. Even so, the order of magnitude survives every correction.

WE 2 · V/f arithmetic at 35 Hz

A 4-pole, 400 V/50 Hz motor (rated slip 3%) runs from a V/f drive at 35 Hz. Find the applied voltage, synchronous and shaft speeds, and the torque available.

V/f ratio = 400/50 = 8 V per Hz → V(35) = 8 × 35 = 280 V ns = 120·f/P = 120×35/4 = 1 050 rpm slip ≈ 3% (slip in rpm scales with flux & load, roughly constant torque) n ≈ 1 050 × 0.97 ≈ 1 019 rpm Torque: flux Φ ∝ V/f = constant → full rated torque available (minus cooling caveat)

Discussion: below base speed the drive is a constant-torque machine — flux is held, current limits torque. Two footnotes carry the marks: at low Hz the stator IR drop eats a bigger voltage fraction, so drives add voltage boost / IR compensation; and a self-cooled motor at 1 000 rpm has lost ~⅓ of its fan cooling — continuous full torque may need a blower or a bigger frame.

WE 3 · Sizing a brake resistor

A centrifuge (J = 12 kg·m² at the motor shaft) must decelerate from 1 450 rpm to rest in 10 s. The 400 V drive's chopper turns on at Vdc = 750 V. Find the braking energy, mean and peak power, and a resistor value.

ω = 2π×1450/60 = 151.8 rad/s E = ½Jω² = ½ × 12 × 151.8² = 138 kJ Mean power = 138 kJ / 10 s = 13.8 kW Peak (start of linear ramp, full speed & full braking torque) ≈ 2×mean = 27.6 kW Resistor: R ≤ Vdc²/Ppk = 750²/27 600 ≈ 20 Ω (round down to standard, check chopper Imax) Continuous rating: one stop per 5 min → duty = 10/300 = 3.3% → Pcont ≈ 0.033×13.8 kW ≈ 0.5 kW (size 1–2 kW with margin)

Discussion: the ½Jω² line is the same energy ledger as Page 8: ramp longer and the chopper may vanish entirely; stop often and the resistor's continuous rating — not the peak — drives its size and its ventilation (A46's thermal budget). A regenerative front end turns this heater into a credit note.

WE 4 · Long motor cables & the 2× spike

A drive feeds a motor through 60 m of cable. IGBT rise time tr = 100 ns; pulse velocity in cable v ≈ 1.5×10⁸ m/s. Show why the motor sees ~2× the DC bus voltage, and what fixes exist.

Critical length: lc ≈ v·tr/2 = 1.5×10⁸ × 100×10⁻⁹ / 2 = 7.5 m 60 m ≫ 7.5 m → each PWM edge arrives as a travelling wave; the motor's high surge impedance reflects it → terminal voltage → up to 2×Vdc ≈ 2×565 = ~1 130 V spikes at the carrier frequency, thousands of times per second.

Discussion: this is transmission-line physics arriving uninvited in a motor circuit. Fixes in ascending cost: inverter-duty motor (reinforced first-turn insulation, IEC 60034-25), dV/dt filter at the drive, sine filter (kills the problem, adds loss and cost), shorter/symmetric screened cable (A46's 360° EMC discipline). First-turn insulation failure from edge reflections is a classic "mystery" winding death — now not a mystery.

Problem Set

Ten problems. Work each before revealing — the reveal is the marking scheme.

Problem 1

A pump runs at 60% speed. What fraction of rated flow, head and power do the affinity laws predict?

tap to reveal answer ▾
Q ∝ n → 60% flow; H ∝ n² → 36% head; P ∝ n³ → 21.6% power. Caveat before applying: if the system has significant static head, the operating point doesn't slide down the ideal curve — check the real system curve (A47) before promising the 21.6%.
Problem 2

Compute the ideal DC bus voltage of a 400 V AC drive, and the voltage a 690 V drive would have.

tap to reveal answer ▾
Vdc ≈ √2 × VLL: 400 V → 565 V; 690 V → 976 V. (Six-pulse rectified line-to-line peak; real buses ride slightly lower under load, and the brake chopper threshold sits above — e.g. ~750 V on a 400 V drive.)
Problem 3

Why does available torque fall above base speed — and why does it fall as 1/f first, then ~1/f² at the top end?

tap to reveal answer ▾
Above base speed the voltage ceiling is reached, so flux Φ ∝ V/f falls as 1/f (field weakening) → torque at constant current falls as 1/f (constant-power region). Push further and the pull-out torque — ∝ Φ², i.e. 1/f² — closes in on the load torque; you hit the breakdown limit region. Practical ceiling ≈ 1.5–2× base speed for standard motors (mechanical limits permitting).
Problem 4

Rank for a frequently-stopping high-inertia load: longer ramp, brake resistor, active front end — by capex and by energy outcome.

tap to reveal answer ▾
Capex: ramp (free) < resistor+chopper < AFE. Energy: ramp avoids fast braking (production cost instead); resistor wastes ½Jω² as heat every stop; AFE recovers it to the grid. Frequent stops × high J is exactly where the AFE's premium pays back — compute WE 3's energy × stops/day before deciding.
Problem 5

A fan system has a structural resonance at ~32 Hz drive output. The drive runs 20–50 Hz. What feature handles this and how does it work?

tap to reveal answer ▾
Skip / jump frequencies: program a band (e.g. 30–34 Hz) the output may pass through but never dwell in — the ramp crosses it quickly, steady-state operation is excluded. Find the band with a vibration survey (A42's resonance detection); a drive that dwells on a structural resonance is a fatigue machine (A42/A19).
Problem 6

Six months after a VFD retrofit, the motor's DE bearing shows fluting (washboard raceways — A19). Mechanism and three fixes?

tap to reveal answer ▾
PWM common-mode voltage capacitively couples to the shaft; when it exceeds the oil-film breakdown of the bearing, micro-EDM discharges pit the raceway — thousands per second (ISO 15243 electrical erosion, A19's VFD-fluting entry). Fixes: shaft grounding ring, insulated NDE bearing (breaks the circulating path — use both for large frames), common-mode filter/chokes at the drive, plus proper 360° screen termination so the current has a designed return path (A46).
Problem 7

A 22 kW drive lists "22 kW heavy duty (150% 60 s) / 30 kW normal duty (110% 60 s)". Which rating applies to a crusher, and why do drives have two?

tap to reveal answer ▾
The crusher needs heavy duty (constant-torque): it demands overload torque headroom (150%) for lumps and starts. Fans/pumps (variable torque, P∝n³) never see overload at reduced speed, so the same silicon can carry a bigger motor at 110% margin — the "normal duty" rating. Two ratings = one inverter, two thermal duty assumptions. Size by load type AND motor FLC, not nameplate kW alone.
Problem 8

Carrier frequency: 2 kHz vs 12 kHz — list the trade-offs an engineer weighs.

tap to reveal answer ▾
Higher carrier (12 kHz): quieter motor (whine above human annoyance band), smoother current, better low-speed control — but higher switching losses (derate the drive), more dV/dt edges per second stressing insulation and bearings, more EMC energy. Lower carrier (2 kHz): efficient and robust but audible whine and rougher current. Default mid (4–8 kHz); derate per the manual above that; never raise it to "fix" a noise complaint without checking the derating table.
Problem 9

The drive runs in a 45 °C plant room at 1 800 m altitude. What happens to its rating?

tap to reveal answer ▾
Double derating: typically full rating to 40 °C then ~1–1.5%/°C above (45 °C → ~93–95%), and full rating to 1 000 m then ~1%/100 m for the thinner cooling air (1 800 m → ~92%). Combined ≈ 0.94 × 0.92 ≈ 86% of nameplate — a "22 kW" drive is now a 19 kW drive. Panel thermal design (A46's ΔT = P/kA) must use the drive's real dissipation too.
Problem 10

Commissioning basics: name six parameters you must set (or verify) before first start on any drive, any brand.

tap to reveal answer ▾
Any six of: motor nameplate set (V, Hz, FLC, rpm, cosφ — feeds the model and the electronic OL), control mode (V/f vs vector; run motor-ID/autotune for vector), accel/decel ramps, current/torque limits, min/max frequency (+ skip bands), stop mode (ramp/coast/DC brake), start source & reference source (terminals/fieldbus — A45's dialects), and the fault relay wiring into the starter logic (A51). The motor-data page is the one that protects the motor — never skip it.

Key Derivations & Exam Traps

Three derivations worth owning, and the seven classic traps.

Derivation 1 · Why flux = V/f (the constant that rules the drive)
Stator EMF (Faraday, sinusoidal steady state): E = 4.44 · f · N · Φm (A32's transformer eqn — same physics) Neglect stator drop: V ≈ E → Φm ≈ V / (4.44·N·f) ∝ V/f Hold V/f constant → flux constant → rated torque per amp at any speed ∎ Low-f correction: V = E + I·Rs — at 2 Hz, I·Rs is a large share of the tiny V, so drives add boost; above base speed V hits its ceiling → Φ ∝ 1/f (field weakening).
Derivation 2 · The affinity cube from first principles
Impeller velocity triangles scale with speed n: flow Q ∝ (blade velocity)·(area) → Q ∝ n head H ∝ u²/g (Euler, A47) → H ∝ n² power P = ρ·g·Q·H/η ∝ n · n² → P ∝ n³ ∎ Fine print: valid along similar operating points (same efficiency), i.e. friction-dominated systems. Static head breaks similarity — re-intersect pump and system curves instead (A47's honest method).
Derivation 3 · Braking energy & the DC-bus ledger
Decelerating inertia J from ω₁ to ω₂ releases E = ½J(ω₁² − ω₂²) Power flow: load → motor (as generator) → inverter (reverse) → DC bus Bus capacitor stores only Ecap = ½C(Vmax² − Vnom²) — typically a few hundred J, vs WE 3's 138 kJ → 1000× too small → voltage rises in ms → OV trip. Hence the three exits: time (long ramp), heat (chopper+resistor), grid (AFE). ∎
Seven exam & field traps
  • Boost or stall: pure V/f at 1–3 Hz under-fluxes the motor (IR drop) — no breakaway torque. Enable boost/IR comp, or use vector mode for high starting torque.
  • Static head breaks the cube: quoting P∝n³ savings for a boiler-feed or high-lift pump is an audit finding — check the system curve (A47).
  • 2×Vdc at the motor: any cable beyond ~10 m puts reflected spikes on the winding — inverter-duty insulation or filters (WE 4).
  • Drive FLC ≠ motor kW: size by current and duty class (heavy vs normal, Problem 7); a "22 kW" drive can be wrong for a 22 kW motor.
  • THDi vs THDv: the drive draws distorted current; whether the bus voltage distorts depends on source impedance — quote IEEE 519 at the PCC, not at the drive terminals.
  • Field-weakening cliff: above base speed torque ∝ 1/f and pull-out ∝ 1/f² — a "just run it at 70 Hz" request needs a torque check, not a parameter change.
  • The unread derating table: ambient, altitude and carrier frequency each shave the rating (Problems 8–9); stacked, they can hide 20%+.

Hands-on ladder — eight exercises
  • Rectify–smooth–invert from memory: draw the power chain with voltages (400 V AC → 565 V DC → PWM) and name every component's failure mode.
  • On any drive (or its simulator/manual), do the six-parameter commissioning of Problem 10; run a motor-ID tune and note what changed.
  • Measure or compute V/f pairs at 10/25/35/50 Hz for a motor you know; verify the constant, then find the boost parameter and see its effect at 2 Hz.
  • Log input current THD (clamp meter with THD, or the drive's own meters) with and without the DC choke option — see Page 9 become numbers.
  • Compute WE 3 for a machine at your plant (J from coupling + load datasheets); decide ramp vs resistor vs AFE on real stop frequency.
  • Walk one drive installation against the EMC checklist: screened cable, 360° glands both ends, separation from signal runs, filter fitted (A46).
